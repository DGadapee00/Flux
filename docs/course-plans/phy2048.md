## Units

Each lab says what it shows, what it recomputes, and the second method that checks it. Every
application lab is a real everyday or industrial device (D25), and its explainer ends with **What the
course model leaves out.** (D30). The device's real numbers and their citations are chosen when
the unit is built, because the sources could not be opened here. Labs marked *(exists)* are in
`courses/phy2048/src/labs/` as logic only.

Every unit's notes follow PHY 2049's structure (D32): parts with a legend, six or more worked
examples each with a **Show this in the lab** setup, and a `box out` where a section uses an
idealisation. Printed answers for the worked cases come from the matching OpenStax examples
(CC BY 4.0), cited by section and example. Problem statements are written fresh.

**Unit 1. Kinematics** (8.01SC week 1; §1.2 to §1.7, §2.1 to §2.3, §3.1 to §3.6, §4.1 to §4.3)

- Topics: units, conversion, dimensional analysis and significant figures; estimates; vectors and
  components; position, displacement, average and instantaneous velocity; the derivative;
  acceleration and the integral; constant acceleration; free fall; two-dimensional motion;
  projectiles.
- `kinematics-graphs`: linked x–t, v–t and a–t graphs, with a piecewise a(t) the student edits and a
  secant that shrinks to the tangent. Shows that velocity is the slope of x–t and the area under
  a–t, that average velocity over a shrinking interval becomes the instantaneous one, and that
  distance and displacement part when v changes sign. Recompute: exact integration of each piece.
  Check: quadrature of the same a(t), and ∫|v| dt against the sum of |Δx| between zero crossings of v.
- `projectile` *(exists)*: launch angle against range, from level ground and from a height. Shows
  that complementary angles give the same range on level ground and not from a height, and that
  the horizontal and vertical motions do not affect each other. Recompute: closed form with the
  quadratic landing time. Check: step integration of the same motion.
- Application lab `elevator`: a cab with a trapezoidal speed profile and a rider on a scale. Shows
  that the area under v–t is the floors travelled, and that the scale reads differently only while
  the cab accelerates. Numbers: speed, acceleration and jerk limits of a mid-rise elevator, from a
  published ride-quality standard.
- Problem families: which quantity is unknown (time, distance, speed, acceleration); the
  representation (graph, table, formula, words); a reversal of direction; projectile to, from and
  at a height; unit conversion with significant figures. Conceptual: the sign of v and a together;
  a at the top of a toss; vector against scalar.
- Traps: average velocity as (v₀ + v)/2 when a varies; total distance reported as net displacement;
  sine and cosine swapped when the angle is from the vertical; the level-ground range formula used
  for a launch from a height; km/h to m/s by ×3.6 instead of ÷3.6.
- Predictions: `kinematics-graphs`: the same Δv from a short large a and a long small one; v = 0 at
  an instant, so is a = 0? `projectile`: 30° to 60° at one speed (range the same, height larger);
  doubling the launch speed (range ×4, time ×2).

**Unit 2. Newton's laws and forces** (week 2; §4.5, §5.1 to §5.7, §6.1, §6.2, §13.1, §13.2)

- Topics: the three laws; inertial and accelerating frames; relative motion; mass and weight;
  universal gravitation and g at the surface; normal, tension, static and kinetic friction;
  ideal ropes and pulleys; Hooke's law; free-body diagrams.
- `incline` *(exists)*: a block on an incline with friction. Shows that whether it slides depends on
  tan θ against μ_s and not on the mass, and that a = g(sin θ − μ_k cos θ) once it moves. Recompute:
  ΣF = ma. Check: integrate the motion, and the work of friction against the energy change.
- `atwood`: two masses over an ideal pulley, and a cart pulled by a hanging mass. Shows that the
  tension is not the hanging weight while the system accelerates. Recompute: solve ΣF = ma for a and
  T. Check: ½(m₁ + m₂)v² against (m₂ − m₁)g h over a drop.
- `frames`: a hanging bob and a scale in an accelerating cart and elevator, seen from the ground and
  from the cart. Shows that the apparent weight is m(g + a) and the plumb line tilts by tan θ = a/g.
  Recompute: ΣF = ma in the ground frame. Check: the cart frame with a pseudo-force gives the same
  numbers.
- Application lab `abs-braking`: stopping on dry and wet pavement with locked and rolling wheels.
  Shows that a rolling tyre uses the larger static friction and stops shorter than a skid. Recompute:
  d = v²/(2μg). Check: a time-stepped stop. Numbers: friction coefficients from a published road
  or tyre source.
- Problem families: free-body diagram to equations, solved for a, T, N or μ; an incline that holds
  or slides; connected bodies; apparent weight; pseudo-force problems. Conceptual: action–reaction
  pairs; ranking apparent weights; tension at rest against in motion.
- Traps: T = mg while accelerating; N = mg on an incline (it is mg cos θ); μ_k used to decide whether
  a block starts to slide; weight and the normal force named as an action–reaction pair; one body
  analysed when two are linked.
- Predictions: `incline`: doubling the mass at one angle (a unchanged); raising θ past the angle where
  it slips. `atwood`: doubling both masses (a unchanged, T doubles). `frames`: an elevator speeding
  up against at constant speed.

**Unit 3. Circular motion** (week 3; §4.4, §6.3, §10.1 to §10.3)

- Topics: polar coordinates; angular position, velocity and acceleration; uniform circular motion;
  period and frequency; centripetal acceleration; nonuniform circular motion; banked curves;
  a rotating bucket.
- `circular`: a particle on a circle with ω(t) the student sets. Shows that the acceleration of a
  steady circular motion points at the centre and not along the motion, scales as v²/r = ω²r, and
  gains a tangential part when the speed changes. Recompute: a_c = ω²r and a_t = αr. Check: numerical
  second derivative of x(t) and y(t), projected on the radius and the tangent.
- `banked-curve`: a car on a banked, circular road. Shows the band of speeds that hold with friction
  and the design speed v² = rg tan θ that needs none. Recompute: ΣF = ma in normal and tangential
  axes. Check: the same forces in x–y components; the residual ΣF − ma is zero.
- Application lab `spin-cycle`: clothes against the drum of a washing machine. Shows that the
  normal force from the drum is the centripetal force, and the "g-force" at the drum wall is
  ω²r/g. Numbers: drum diameter and spin speed from a published manufacturer specification.
- Problem families: rpm, rad/s and m/s conversions; flat and banked curves; the top and bottom of a
  vertical circle (tension, normal); a conical pendulum. Conceptual: the direction of a at several
  points; what happens when the string breaks.
- Traps: degrees in ω; the diameter used as r; "centrifugal force" drawn on a free-body diagram in an
  inertial frame; v²/r with v in rpm; the normal force taken as mg at the top of a loop.
- Predictions: `circular`: doubling the speed at one radius (a ×4); halving the radius at one speed
  (a ×2). `banked-curve`: the same car at the design speed on a wet road.

**Unit 4. Drag, constraints and continuous systems** (week 4; §6.4, §6.1)

- Topics: resistive forces, linear at low speed and quadratic at high; terminal speed; the time
  constant; pulley constraints from the length of the string; a massive rope's tension (a
  differential element); wrapping friction.
- `drag`: a body falling with linear or quadratic drag, compared with free fall. Shows that the
  terminal speed does not depend on the starting speed, that the linear case reaches 63% of it in one
  time constant m/b, and that quadrupling the area halves a quadratic v_t. Recompute: the
  exponential and the tanh closed forms. Check: RK4 integration of m dv/dt = mg − F(v).
- `drag-projectile`: a projectile with quadratic drag. Shows that the range falls, the descent is
  steeper than the climb, and the best angle drops below 45°. Recompute: RK4. Check: the same run at
  half the step, and the linear-drag closed form for the linear case.
- `pulleys`: a movable-pulley system. Shows that the constraint (a total string length) ties the
  accelerations, and that the two ropes do not carry the same tension. Recompute: the constraint
  with ΣF = ma. Check: energy gained equals gravitational energy lost over a drop.
- Application lab `bollard`: a rope wrapped around a mooring post. Shows that holding force falls
  exponentially with the wrap angle, T_load = T_hold e^(μφ), so a few turns hold a ship. Recompute: the
  closed form. Check: numerical integration of dT/dφ = μT around the wrap. Numbers: rope and post
  friction and a mooring load from a published source. The `drag` lab's parachute scenario uses
  published canopy values.
- Problem families: terminal speed, and the time to reach a fraction of it, in either drag law;
  which acceleration is twice which; the hold needed for a given load and wrap; the tension at a
  point in a hanging rope. Conceptual: which of two shapes falls first with drag.
- Traps: a = g at terminal speed; the linear scaling used on the quadratic law; the same tension taken
  on both sides of a massive rope; φ in degrees; the constraint ignored, so a₁ = a₂.
- Predictions: `drag`: four times the area (v_t halves); double the mass (linear: v_t doubles;
  quadratic: ×√2). `pulleys`: add a mass to the movable pulley. `drag-projectile`: the range
  against the no-drag range at 45°.

**Unit 5. Momentum, impulse and centre of mass** (week 5; §9.1 to §9.3, §9.6)

- Topics: momentum; impulse as an area and a vector; the momentum of a system; the force on a system;
  conservation and momentum diagrams; centre of mass of points and of continuous bodies; its motion.
- `impulse`: an F–t curve the student shapes. Shows that a short large force and a long small one
  with equal area give the same Δp, and that impulse is a vector. Recompute: the area under F(t).
  Check: integrate m dv/dt = F.
- `center-of-mass`: point masses, a rod, a plate with a hole, and a shell bursting in flight.
  Shows that the centre of mass keeps its parabola whatever the fragments do. Recompute: the weighted
  position. Check: numerical integration of x dm over the plate, and the burst's centre of mass
  against the parabola.
- Application lab `crumple-zone`: a car stopping from a barrier-test speed. Shows that stopping the
  same Δp over a longer time cuts the average force, F = Δp/Δt. Recompute: Δp over the crush time.
  Check: the work–energy view, F × crush distance = ½mv². Numbers: a published barrier-test speed and
  crush distance.
- Problem families: impulse from a graph; a bounce and a stop; a system's momentum by components;
  the centre of mass of discrete, continuous and composite bodies (a hole as a negative mass);
  explosions and recoil. Conceptual: equal momentum and unequal kinetic energy.
- Traps: a rebound impulse taken as mv instead of 2mv; momentum added as magnitudes; the midpoint taken
  as the centre of mass of unequal masses; a hole added as positive mass.
- Predictions: `impulse`: the same area, a longer time (smaller peak force). `center-of-mass`: a burst
  in flight (the centre of mass is unmoved).

**Unit 6. Recoil and rockets** (week 6; §9.7)

- Topics: relative velocity in recoil, in the ground and the moving frame; the rocket equation; thrust;
  rocket with and without gravity; staging.
- `recoil`: a person on a cart throwing masses one at a time or together. Shows that a speed quoted
  relative to the cart changes the answer, and that throwing in two steps differs from one.
  Recompute: momentum conservation in the ground frame. Check: solve in the cart's frame and
  transform back.
- `rocket`: a rocket burning at a steady rate. Shows that Δv grows with ln(m₀/m_f) and not with the mass
  burned, and that gravity costs g t_b. Recompute: Δv = v_e ln(m₀/m_f) − g t_b. Check: numerical
  integration of m dv/dt = ṁ v_e − mg.
- Application lab `fire-hose`: a nozzle on a hose. Shows that the reaction force is the rate of
  momentum given to the water, F = ṁ v = ρAv², and why crews brace it. Recompute: ρAv². Check: the
  published nozzle-reaction formula. Numbers: hose size and nozzle pressure from a fire-service
  source.
- Problem families: recoil in two frames; Δv for a mass ratio; thrust against weight at lift-off; burn
  time and height; two stages. Conceptual: why a rocket works in vacuum.
- Traps: the exhaust speed taken as a ground speed; Δv linear in burned mass; gravity dropped; thrust
  taken as m a only; log base 10 for ln.
- Predictions: `rocket`: double the exhaust speed (Δv doubles); double the propellant mass (Δv less
  than doubles). `recoil`: throw both masses at once or in turn.

**Unit 7. Work and kinetic energy** (week 7; §2.4, §7.1 to §7.4, §8.2)

- Topics: the scalar product; kinetic energy; work by a constant and a variable force; the
  work–energy theorem in one, two and three dimensions; power; path dependence; conservative and
  non-conservative forces.
- `work-path`: a force field and three paths between the same two points. Shows that gravity and a
  spring do the same work along every path while friction does not, and that a force at a right
  angle to the motion does none. Recompute: closed-form line integrals. Check: numerical ∫F·dr along
  each path.
- `ramp-work`: a block pushed up and sliding down a ramp, with each force's work shown. Shows that the
  net work equals ΔK and that the normal force does none. Recompute: the sum of the works. Check:
  constant-acceleration kinematics for the speed.
- Application lab `car-cruise`: a car at highway speed. Shows that the power to hold speed grows with
  the cube of the speed, since drag is ∝ v², and that rolling resistance is the small part.
  Recompute: P = (½ρC_dAv² + C_rr mg) v. Check: integrate F·dr over a distance and divide by the
  time. Numbers: a published drag area and rolling-resistance coefficient.
- Problem families: work at an angle; work from a graph or a spring; the work–energy theorem for a
  speed, a distance or a force; average and instantaneous power. Conceptual: signs of work;
  whether a force does work.
- Traps: cos θ left out; the normal force's work on an incline taken as nonzero; kinetic energy with
  v, not v²; mg times the incline's length instead of its height; ½kx² written as kx².
- Predictions: `ramp-work`: double the speed (K ×4, stopping distance ×4). `work-path`: the same spring
  work along a longer path.

**Unit 8. Potential energy and conservation of energy** (week 8; §8.1, §8.3, §8.4, §13.3)

- Topics: potential energy of gravity, a spring and gravitation; the reference state; mechanical
  energy and its conservation; energy with friction; F = −dU/dx; stable and unstable equilibrium;
  reading a potential-energy diagram.
- `track` *(exists)*: bar charts of K, U and thermal energy for a car on a hilly track, with and
  without friction, and a vertical loop. Shows that the speed at a point depends on the height
  and not the path, that friction adds thermal energy f·d, and that a loop needs N ≥ 0 at the top.
  Recompute: energy conservation. Check: integrate Newton's equation along the track.
- `energy-diagram`: a one-dimensional U(x) with a total-energy line. Shows turning points, stable and
  unstable equilibria, F = −dU/dx, and escape over a barrier. Recompute: −dU/dx and the roots of
  E = U. Check: numerical integration of the motion, and the period from ∮ dx/v.
- Application lab `pumped-storage`: a pumped-hydro reservoir. Shows that stored energy is ρVgh and
  the round trip loses a fraction. Recompute: ρVgh. Check: integrate the work of lifting thin layers
  of water. Numbers: head, volume and capacity of a real station from a published source.
- Problem families: conservation with a spring and gravity; thermal energy from friction; a diagram
  read for turning points and speeds; the minimum height for a loop. Conceptual: the reference level;
  "used-up" energy.
- Traps: the reference level changing the answer; the spring compression's extra drop forgotten; mg
  times the path instead of the height; the sign of F = −dU/dx.
- Predictions: `track`: move the zero of U (speeds unchanged); two frictionless paths of the same
  height. `energy-diagram`: raise E past a barrier.

**Unit 9. Collisions** (week 9; §9.4, §9.5)

- Topics: momentum and kinetic energy in collisions; totally inelastic collisions; one-dimensional
  elastic collisions by relative velocity; restitution; two dimensions; the slingshot.
- `collide` *(exists)*: a one-dimensional collision from elastic to inelastic, with a moving-wall
  scenario for the slingshot. Shows that momentum is conserved for every e, that the kinetic energy lost
  grows as e falls, and that a light body can leave a heavy approaching one at v + 2V. Recompute: the
  closed form. Check: the centre-of-mass frame, and the energy lost ½μ(1 − e²)v_rel².
- `collide-2d`: an oblique collision of two discs. Shows that momentum is conserved by components and,
  for equal masses with one at rest in an elastic collision, the paths part at a right angle.
  Recompute: the closed form. Check: energy conservation (elastic) and the unchanged centre-of-mass
  velocity.
- `ballistic-pendulum`: a projectile embedding in a hanging block. Shows that momentum is conserved in
  the collision and energy in the swing, not the other way round. Recompute: v = (m + M)/m √(2gh).
  Check: integrate the swing, with the energy lost accounted for.
- Application lab `golf-driver`: club, ball and the limit on how bouncy the face may be. Shows that
  ball speed is (1 + e) m_c v_c/(m_c + m_b) and why the rules cap e. Recompute: the formula. Check:
  the centre-of-mass frame. Numbers: ball mass and the coefficient-of-restitution limit from the
  governing bodies' published rules.
- Problem families: totally inelastic; elastic by relative velocity; restitution given; two dimensions;
  the speed found from a swing; a given energy loss. Conceptual: which collisions are possible.
- Traps: elastic assumed when the bodies stick; momentum conserved by magnitude and not by component;
  signs of opposite velocities; e = 1 applied to an inelastic case.
- Predictions: `collide`: equal masses, one at rest, elastic (the velocities swap); totally inelastic
  (maximum loss). `ballistic-pendulum`: double the bullet speed (the height ×4).

**Unit 10. Rotation, moment of inertia and torque** (week 10; §2.4, §10.4 to §10.8)

- Topics: rigid bodies; kinetic energy of rotation; moments of inertia of a rod, disc and sphere;
  the parallel-axis theorem; the vector product; torque; torque from gravity; τ = Iα; internal
  torques cancel; massive pulleys; the no-slip condition; work and power in rotation.
- `rotor` *(exists)*: a disc or rod under a torque. Shows that α = τ/I and that mass farther out lowers
  α. Recompute: τ = Iα. Check: integrate θ and ω, and the work τθ against ½Iω².
- `inertia`: rod, ring, disc, sphere and a composite about any axis. Shows that I depends on how far
  the mass is from the axis, and the parallel-axis shift. Recompute: the tabulated forms and
  `parallelAxisMass`. Check: numerical integration of r² dm.
- `massive-pulley`: an Atwood machine whose pulley has inertia. Shows that the two tensions differ and
  a falls short of the massless case. Recompute: a = (m₂ − m₁)g/(m₁ + m₂ + I/R²). Check: energy
  conservation including the pulley's rotation.
- Application lab `flywheel`: a flywheel energy-storage unit. Shows that stored energy is ½Iω² and why a
  rim-heavy shape stores more per kilogram. Recompute: ½Iω². Check: the integral of τ dθ while
  spinning up. Numbers: mass, speed and stored energy of a real unit from a published datasheet.
- Problem families: I of composites; τ = Iα with a pulley; rotational energy; torque from gravity;
  a spinning-up wheel (constant α). Conceptual: which shape accelerates fastest under one torque.
- Traps: mass used for I; rod about its end taken as ML²/12; the parallel-axis term subtracted going
  away from the centre; the pulley treated as massless; torque without sin θ or the lever arm.
- Predictions: `rotor`: move mass outward (α falls); a ring and a disc of equal mass and radius under
  one torque.

**Unit 11. Angular momentum** (week 11; §11.2 to §11.4)

- Topics: angular momentum of a particle; its dependence on the origin; of circular motion; of a
  symmetric rigid body; torque as dL/dt; angular impulse; conservation; precession.
- `particle-l`: a particle passing a point on a straight line. Shows that L depends on the origin, and
  that on a straight line past the origin it is constant and equals p times the miss distance.
  Recompute: r × p. Check: the area swept per unit time, dA/dt = L/2m, integrated numerically.
- `spinner`: a stool with masses pulled in or out. Shows that L is conserved when no outside torque
  acts, so ω rises as I falls and the kinetic energy rises too. Recompute: L = Iω. Check: the work
  done pulling in, ∫F dr, equals the energy gained.
- `gyro`: a spinning wheel on a pivot. Shows that precession is the torque over the angular
  momentum, Ω = mgr/(Iω), slower for a faster spin. Recompute: the closed form. Check: integrate
  dL/dt = τ.
- Application lab `reaction-wheel`: a small satellite turning by spinning a wheel. Shows that the
  body turns the opposite way: I_b ω_b + I_w ω_w = 0, rates in the ground frame. Recompute: L conservation. Check: integrate
  the torque on each part. Numbers: a published small-satellite wheel datasheet.
- Problem families: L about different origins; conservation with a changing I; a particle striking a
  pivoted body; torque against a rate of change of L; precession rate. Conceptual: when L is
  conserved and when not.
- Traps: L = mvr when r and v are not perpendicular; kinetic energy assumed conserved because L is;
  a pivot's reaction torque ignored; the right-hand-rule direction.
- Predictions: `spinner`: pull the masses in (ω up, K up). `gyro`: double the spin (precession halves).

**Unit 12. Rolling** (week 12; §11.1)

- Topics: translation plus rotation; rolling without slipping; the contact point; friction on a rolling
  wheel; a yo-yo and a spool; kinetic energy and angular momentum of translation and rotation.
- `rolling`: a wheel with a traced rim point. Shows that the contact point is instantly at rest, the
  top moves at 2v, and the contact point accelerates toward the centre at v²/R. Recompute: v_P = v_cm +
  ω × r. Check: the numerical derivative of the cycloid x = R(θ − sin θ), y = R(1 − cos θ).
- `roll-race`: hoop, disc and sphere down an incline. Shows that mass and radius cancel, so only the
  shape decides, a = g sin θ/(1 + k²/R²). Recompute: the closed form. Check: energy, ½mv²(1 + k²/R²) =
  mgh, and a numerical run.
- `spool`: a spool pulled by a string at different angles. Shows that the spool rolls toward or away
  from the puller depending on the angle, with the change at cos θ = r/R (string leaving the bottom of the axle). Recompute: torque about the
  contact point. Check: ΣF = ma and Στ = Iα about the centre.
- Application lab `bowling-ball`: a ball skidding then rolling. Shows that a uniform sphere thrown with
  no spin ends at 5/7 of its speed whatever μ is, and μ only sets where it starts to roll. Recompute: the
  closed form. Check: a time-stepped skid. Numbers: ball mass and diameter limits from the sport's
  published rules.
- Problem families: speed at the bottom of an incline; the friction needed to roll; skid then roll;
  a pulled spool. Conceptual: the direction of friction on a rolling wheel; the race order.
- Traps: v = ωR used while slipping; friction at a rolling contact taken as μN; rotational energy
  left out; I about the contact point mixed with I about the centre.
- Predictions: `roll-race`: which reaches the bottom first (the sphere); double the radius (no change).

**Unit 13. Static equilibrium and elasticity** (no 8.01SC week; §12.1 to §12.4)

- Topics: the conditions ΣF = 0 and Στ = 0; choosing the axis; examples (ladders, beams, cables);
  stress, strain and elastic moduli; the elastic and plastic regions.
- `ladder`: a ladder against a wall, with a person on it. Shows that the friction needed does not
  depend on the ladder's weight or length, only on the angle and where the person stands. Recompute:
  ΣF = 0 and Στ = 0 (`statics`). Check: moments about a different point agree.
- `stress-strain`: a wire loaded past its yield. Shows a straight line to the yield, then permanent
  stretch. Recompute: σ = Eε and ΔL = FL/(AE). Check: the strain energy, the area under the
  curve, against ½FΔL.
- Application lab `tower-crane`: a crane with a counterweight. Shows how the load and counterweight
  moments balance, and the radius at which it tips. Recompute: the moment balance. Check: the same
  balance about the tipping edge. Numbers: a published load chart for a real crane.
- Problem families: a beam on two supports; a ladder; a sign hung by a cable and a strut; a wire's
  stretch; a tipping limit. Conceptual: where to put the axis; statically indeterminate cases.
- Traps: a force's lever arm forgotten; the wall's friction assumed; signs of torques; stress and force
  confused; Young's modulus units.
- Predictions: `ladder`: a heavier person higher up. `stress-strain`: unload after yield.

**Unit 14. Gravitation** (no week of its own; §13.1 to §13.5, with 8.01SC sessions 5.1 to 5.3, 23.5, 27.5)

- Topics: the universal law and g(r); gravitational potential energy and total energy; escape speed;
  circular orbits and their energy; Kepler's three laws.
- `orbit`: a satellite near a planet, with the launch speed the student sets. Shows circular speed
  √(GM/r), T ∝ r^(3/2), E = −GMm/2r, and an ellipse or escape as the speed grows. Recompute: the
  closed forms. Check: RK4 of the inverse-square motion, with L and E conserved.
- `kepler`: an ellipse with equal-area wedges. Shows equal areas in equal times and T² ∝ a³ across
  orbits. Recompute: the Kepler solution. Check: the area swept per step in the numerical orbit.
- Application lab `gps-satellite`: a navigation satellite. Shows the radius for a 12-hour orbit and
  that the speed does not depend on the satellite's mass. Recompute: T = 2π√(r³/GM). Check: the
  numerical orbit. Numbers: the published orbital altitude and period of the GPS constellation.
- Problem families: g at a height; orbital speed, period and energy; escape speed; Kepler's third law
  from data; energy change between orbits. Conceptual: weightlessness in orbit.
- Traps: r taken from the surface, not the centre; g held constant at altitude; escape speed confused
  with orbital speed (a factor of √2); "no gravity" in orbit.
- Predictions: `orbit`: double the radius (T ×2√2); double the satellite's mass (speed unchanged).

**Unit 15. Oscillations** (§15.1 to §15.6)

- Topics: simple harmonic motion; energy in it; the circular-motion picture; pendulums; damped
  oscillations; forced oscillations and resonance.
- `spring-mass`: a block on a spring, horizontal and vertical. Shows that the period depends on m and k
  and not on the amplitude, and gravity only moves the equilibrium. Recompute: T = 2π√(m/k). Check:
  RK4, with the period read from zero crossings.
- `pendulum`: a pendulum from small to large amplitude. Shows that the period is amplitude-independent
  only for small angles and grows as T ≈ T₀(1 + θ₀²/16). Recompute: the small-angle T₀. Check: the exact
  period by the arithmetic–geometric mean, against a simulation.
- `resonance`: a damped, driven oscillator. Shows a peak near the natural frequency whose width grows with
  the damping, and a phase that turns from 0 to π through it. Recompute: the steady-state amplitude and
  phase. Check: a time integration run to steady state.
- Application lab `car-suspension`: a quarter-car model. Shows why a damper is tuned near a damping ratio
  of a few tenths, and the ride at the body's natural frequency. Recompute: ω₀ and ζ. Check: the
  step response from a time integration. Numbers: a published sprung mass, spring rate and damper
  rate.
- Problem families: period from m and k; energy at a displacement; a pendulum's length from a
  period; a damped decay; the resonance amplitude. Conceptual: what changes the period.
- Traps: amplitude put in the period; ω and f mixed; degrees in a phase; the pendulum's period taken
  as depending on mass; the vertical spring's equilibrium forgotten.
- Predictions: `spring-mass`: double the mass (T ×√2); double the amplitude (T unchanged).
  `pendulum`: a large angle against a small one.

**Unit 16. Waves** (§16.1 to §16.6)

- Topics: travelling waves; the mathematics of a wave; the speed on a stretched string; energy and
  power; interference; standing waves and resonance.
- `traveling-wave`: a wave on a string with the tension and density set by the student. Shows that
  the speed is set by the medium, v = √(T/μ), not by the frequency or amplitude, and that each point
  only moves up and down. Recompute: v = √(T/μ). Check: a finite-difference solution of the wave
  equation, with the pulse's arrival time.
- `standing-wave`: two waves, or a string fixed at both ends. Shows constructive and destructive
  interference, nodes and antinodes, and f_n = nv/2L. Recompute: the sum and the mode formula. Check:
  the numerical modes of the string with fixed ends.
- Application lab `guitar-string`: a guitar string and its fret. Shows that pitch rises with tension
  and falls with a longer string, and why a fret shortens it. Recompute: f = (1/2L)√(T/μ). Check: the
  numerical fundamental. Numbers: a published scale length, string tension and linear density.
- Problem families: wave speed from a string's data; a wave equation read for k, ω, v; power from
  amplitude; the modes of a string; interference from a path difference. Conceptual: wave speed
  against particle speed.
- Traps: the particle speed taken as the wave speed; the frequency thought to change the speed; a
  fixed end's inversion forgotten; power ∝ A taken instead of A².
- Predictions: `traveling-wave`: four times the tension (v ×2). `standing-wave`: double the amplitude
  (power ×4).

**Unit 17. Sound** (§17.1 to §17.8)

- Topics: sound as a pressure wave; the speed of sound; intensity and the decibel; standing waves in
  pipes; musical sources; beats; the Doppler effect; shock waves.
- `intensity`: a point source in the open. Shows that intensity falls as 1/r², so each doubling of
  distance costs about 6 dB and doubling the power adds 3 dB. Recompute: β = 10 log₁₀(I/I₀). Check: the
  intensity integrated over a sphere gives back the power.
- `pipe-modes`: open and closed pipes, with a two-frequency beats scenario. Shows that a closed pipe has
  only odd harmonics and f₁ = v/4L, and that two close frequencies beat at |f₁ − f₂|. Recompute: the
  formulas, with v from the temperature. Check: the numerical modes of the wave equation, and the
  envelope of the summed sinusoids.
- `doppler`: a moving source or observer, with a Mach cone past the speed of sound. Shows that moving the
  source and moving the observer give different shifts, and the cone forms above Mach 1. Recompute:
  f′ = f(v ± v_o)/(v ∓ v_s). Check: a simulated pulse train, with the frequency read from the arrival
  times.
- Application lab `ultrasound-doppler`: a medical Doppler probe on a vessel. Shows that the shift gives the
  blood speed, with one speed of sound assumed for the whole tissue path. Recompute: the Doppler shift off a moving
  reflector. Check: the pulse-train simulation. Numbers: a published probe frequency and a typical
  flow speed.
- Problem families: the level from an intensity and back; a distance change; the modes of a pipe;
  beat frequency; a Doppler shift in the four cases; the Mach angle. Conceptual: why adding levels
  does not add decibels.
- Traps: two 60 dB sources taken as 120 dB (it is 63 dB); the speed of sound taken as fixed; a closed
  pipe given even harmonics; the Doppler sign swapped; 20 log used for an intensity.
- Predictions: `intensity`: double the distance (−6 dB); two identical speakers (+3 dB). `doppler`: the
  source approaching against the observer approaching at one speed.

**Unit 18. Fluids** (§14.1 to §14.7)

- Topics: density and pressure; gauge and absolute pressure; Pascal's principle and hydraulics;
  Archimedes' principle; continuity; Bernoulli's equation; viscosity and turbulence, lightly.
- `pressure-depth`: containers of different shapes and a hydraulic lift. Shows that pressure depends on
  depth and not on the shape, and a small force on a small piston balances a large one on a large one.
  Recompute: P = P₀ + ρgh and F₂/F₁ = A₂/A₁. Check: the force balance on a thin slice of fluid.
- `buoyancy`: a block floating or sinking. Shows that the buoyant force is the weight of the fluid
  displaced, and the submerged fraction is ρ_object/ρ_fluid. Recompute: Archimedes. Check: the pressure
  integrated over the body's surface.
- `bernoulli`: a pipe with a changing cross-section and height, and a draining tank. Shows that the
  speed rises and the pressure falls where the pipe narrows, and Torricelli's √(2gh). Recompute: continuity
  and Bernoulli. Check: the work–energy balance on a fluid parcel, and a numerical tank drain.
- Application lab `pitot-tube`: an aircraft airspeed probe. Shows that the pressure difference gives
  the airspeed, v = √(2ΔP/ρ). Recompute: the formula. Check: Bernoulli between the stagnation and static
  ports. Numbers: standard sea-level air density and published airspeed limits.
- Problem families: pressure at a depth; a hydraulic lift; a floating or sunken body; a speed or pressure
  from continuity and Bernoulli; a draining tank. Conceptual: which of two shapes has more bottom force.
- Traps: gauge and absolute pressure mixed; weight of the body used for the buoyant force; Bernoulli
  applied across a pump; density and specific gravity confused; psi and kPa.
- Predictions: `pressure-depth`: a wider container at the same depth (the same pressure).
  `bernoulli`: halve the pipe's area (speed ×2, pressure down).
