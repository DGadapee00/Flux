## What the textbook sets, in OCW's place (D23)

- **Units and order.** The book's chapter order. Chapters are grouped into 13 units below.
- **Difficulty.** The book's own tiers set the levels. Level 1 is a section problem. Level 2 is an
  additional problem. Level 3 is a challenge problem, with the hardest additional problems standing
  in for Ch 8, which has no challenge problems. Level 3 means harder than the worked examples, as
  the standard asks.
- **Printed answers.** Worked cases reproduce the book's worked examples and its answered
  exercises, by our own computation, and cite the section and the example's title. Where our answer
  differs from the printed one, the case says why. Statements are written fresh, and the book's figures
  and wording are never used.
- **Conceptual share.** The book has 376 conceptual questions. Flux writes its own choice, true/false
  and ordering items on the same ideas, each wrong option with its own `why`.

## Units

Every unit follows the book's chapters. Each lab says what it shows, what it recomputes, and the
second method that checks it. Every application lab is a real everyday or industrial device (D25),
taken from a worked example in the book so its numbers come with a citation, and its explainer ends
with **What the course model leaves out.** (D30). Labs marked *(exists)* are in
`courses/phy2048/src/labs/` as logic only.

Notes follow PHY 2049's structure (D32): parts with a legend, six or more worked examples each with a
**Show this in the lab** setup, and a `box out` where a section uses an idealisation. Where a
chapter has fewer than six book examples (Ch 15 has five), the notes write more of their own.

**Unit 1. Units, vectors and motion along a line** (Ch 1 to 3, 17 sections, 43 examples)

- Sections: §1.2 to §1.7 (§1.1 is left out), §2.1 to §2.4, §3.1 to §3.6. Ch 1 is one notes part on
  units, conversion, dimensional analysis, estimates, significant figures and problem solving.
- Topics: scalars and vectors; components and coordinate systems; the algebra of vectors; the scalar
  and vector products; position, displacement and velocity; average and instantaneous values;
  acceleration; constant acceleration; free fall; velocity and displacement from acceleration.
- `vectors`: two or three vectors the student drags, with their sum, components, scalar product and
  vector product. Shows that a resultant adds by components and not by magnitudes, that a·b is a
  projection (zero at a right angle), and that |a×b| is the area they span. Recompute: components.
  Check: the sum by the polygon (tip to tail), and the products against the angle formulas.
  A scenario uses the book's drone takeoff (§2.2).
- `kinematics-graphs`: linked x–t, v–t and a–t graphs, with a piecewise a(t) the student edits and a
  secant that shrinks to the tangent. Shows that velocity is the slope of x–t and the area under a–t,
  that the average velocity over a shrinking interval becomes the instantaneous one, and that
  distance and displacement part when v changes sign. Recompute: exact integration of each piece.
  Check: quadrature of the same a(t), and ∫|v| dt against the sum of |Δx| between zero crossings.
- Application lab `stopping-distance`: a car braking on dry and wet concrete, with a reaction time.
  Shows that stopping distance grows with the square of the speed, and that the reaction distance is
  the part that grows only with speed. Recompute: d = v²/(2a) + v t_r. Check: a time-stepped stop.
  Numbers: §3.4, "How Far Does a Car Go?" (30.0 m/s, 7.00 and 5.00 m/s²).
- Worked cases from: §1.3 "Converting Nonmetric Units to Metric"; §1.6 "Calculating Percent
  Uncertainty: A Bag of Apples"; §2.3 "Analytical Computation of a Resultant"; §3.4 "Cheetah Catching
  a Gazelle"; §3.5 "Free Fall of a Ball"; §3.6 "Motion of a Motorboat".
- Problem families: convert a derived quantity and round to the right figures; the vector sum or
  product from components or from magnitude and angle; which quantity is unknown in constant
  acceleration; a motion read from a graph or a table; a reversal of direction; free fall up and down.
  Conceptual: the sign of v and a together; a at the top of a toss; scalar against vector.
- Traps: average velocity as (v₀ + v)/2 when a varies; total distance given as net displacement;
  sine and cosine swapped when the angle is from the vertical; a·b taken as ab with no cos θ;
  km/h to m/s by ×3.6 instead of ÷3.6; too many significant figures in a product.
- Predictions: `vectors`: reverse one vector (the sum changes, the magnitude of the product does
  not); `kinematics-graphs`: the same Δv from a short large a and a long small one, and v = 0 at an
  instant (is a = 0?).

**Unit 2. Motion in two and three dimensions** (Ch 4, 5 sections, 14 examples)

- Sections: §4.1 to §4.5.
- Topics: displacement, velocity and acceleration vectors; projectile motion; uniform and nonuniform
  circular motion; relative motion in one and two dimensions.
- `projectile` *(exists)*: launch angle against range, from level ground and from a height. Shows
  that complementary angles give the same range on level ground and not from a height, and that the
  horizontal and vertical motions do not affect each other. Recompute: closed form with the quadratic
  landing time. Check: step integration of the same motion.
- `circular-motion`: a particle on a circle with a speed the student sets, steady or changing. Shows
  that the acceleration of a steady circular motion points at the centre and not along the motion,
  scales as v²/r, and gains a tangential part when the speed changes. Recompute: a_c = ω²r and
  a_t = αr. Check: the numerical second derivative of x(t) and y(t), projected on the radius and the
  tangent.
- `relative-motion`: a boat across a river, a plane in a wind, a car seen from a truck. Shows that the
  velocity you see depends on the frame, that heading and track differ, and that to reach a point you
  aim upstream. Recompute: v_ground = v_air + v_wind by components. Check: the polygon, and the heading
  from the sine rule.
- Application lab `wind-triangle`: a plane that must fly due north in a wind. Shows the heading that
  cancels the sideways drift and the ground speed that results. Recompute: the vector sum. Check: the
  sine rule. Numbers: §4.5, "Flying a Plane in a Wind" (300 km/h in still air, a 90 km/h wind from the
  northeast).
- Worked cases from: §4.3 "A Fireworks Projectile Explodes High and Away" and "Comparing Golf Shots";
  §4.4 "Creating an Acceleration of 1 g"; §4.5 "Motion of a Car Relative to a Truck".
- Problem families: range, height and time of flight; a launch to or from a height; a launch at an
  angle up or down a slope; the speed and acceleration in uniform circular motion from rpm or period;
  a total acceleration in nonuniform circular motion; a heading to reach a point in a wind or current.
  Conceptual: the direction of a at several points; a projectile at its top.
- Traps: the level-ground range formula used for a launch from a height; v² taken as v_x² only at the
  top; a pointing along the velocity in circular motion; degrees in ω; the wind added to the heading
  and not to the velocity.
- Predictions: `projectile`: 30° to 60° at one speed (range the same, height larger), and doubling
  the speed (range ×4, time ×2); `circular-motion`: doubling the speed at one radius (a ×4);
  `relative-motion`: a faster current against the same crossing time.

**Unit 3. Newton's laws and their applications** (Ch 5 and 6, 11 sections, 34 examples)

- Sections: §5.1 to §5.7, §6.1 to §6.4.
- Topics: forces; the three laws; mass and weight; common forces (normal, tension, spring);
  free-body diagrams; solving with Newton's laws (blocks, ropes, pulleys, elevators); friction;
  centripetal force; drag and terminal speed.
- `incline` *(exists)*: a block on an incline with friction. Shows that whether it slides depends on
  tan θ against μ_s and not on the mass, and that a = g(sin θ − μ_k cos θ) once it moves. Recompute:
  ΣF = ma. Check: integrate the motion, and the work of friction against the energy change.
- `atwood`: two masses over an ideal pulley, and a block pulled by a hanging mass. Shows that the
  tension is not the hanging weight while the system accelerates. Recompute: solve ΣF = ma for a and
  T. Check: ½(m₁ + m₂)v² against (m₂ − m₁)g h over a drop.
- `banked-curve`: a car on a flat and a banked, circular road. Shows the band of speeds that hold
  with friction and the ideal speed that needs none. Recompute: ΣF = ma in normal and tangential
  axes. Check: the same forces in x–y components; the residual ΣF − ma is zero.
- `drag`: a body falling with linear or quadratic drag, compared with free fall. Shows that the
  terminal speed does not depend on the starting speed, that the linear case reaches 63% of it in one
  time constant m/b, and that quadrupling the area halves a quadratic v_t. Recompute: the exponential
  and the tanh closed forms. Check: RK4 integration of m dv/dt = mg − F(v).
- Application lab `elevator-scale`: a bathroom scale in an elevator. Shows that the scale reads the
  normal force, m(g + a), not the weight, and reads the weight at constant speed. Recompute:
  ΣF = ma. Check: the same run in the elevator's frame with a pseudo-force. Numbers: §6.1, "What Does
  the Bathroom Scale Read in an Elevator?" (75.0 kg, 1.20 m/s²).
- Application lab `skydiver`: a skydiver falling spread-eagle and then under a canopy. Shows the
  terminal speed in each case and why the canopy slows it. Recompute: v_t = √(2mg/ρC_dA). Check: RK4
  to terminal speed. Numbers: §6.4, "Terminal Velocity of a Skydiver" (85 kg, 0.70 m²).
- Worked cases from: §5.7 "Two Blocks on an Inclined Plane"; §6.1 "Atwood Machine" and "Baggage
  Tractor"; §6.2 "Downhill Skier" and "Snowboarding"; §6.3 "What Is the Ideal Speed to Take a Steeply
  Banked Tight Curve?"; §6.4 "Effect of the Resistive Force on a Motorboat".
- Problem families: a free-body diagram turned into equations, solved for a, T, N or μ; an incline that
  holds or slides; connected bodies; a crate on an accelerating truck; a flat or banked curve; a
  loop; terminal speed. Conceptual: action–reaction pairs; ranking apparent weights; tension at rest
  against in motion.
- Traps: T = mg while accelerating; N = mg on an incline (it is mg cos θ); μ_k used to decide whether
  a block starts to slide; weight and the normal force named as an action–reaction pair; a = g at
  terminal speed; "centrifugal force" drawn on a free-body diagram in an inertial frame.
- Predictions: `incline`: doubling the mass at one angle (a unchanged); `atwood`: doubling both masses
  (a unchanged, T doubles); `banked-curve`: the same car at the ideal speed on ice; `drag`: four times
  the area (v_t halves), and double the mass (linear: v_t doubles; quadratic: ×√2).

**Unit 4. Work, energy and power** (Ch 7 and 8, 9 sections, 23 examples)

- Sections: §7.1 to §7.4, §8.1 to §8.4 (§8.5 is left out).
- Topics: work by a constant and a variable force and by a spring; kinetic energy; the work–energy
  theorem; power; potential energy of a system; conservative and non-conservative forces;
  conservation of energy; potential-energy diagrams and stability.
- `work-energy`: a block pushed up and sliding down a ramp, with each force's work shown. Shows that
  the net work equals ΔK, that the normal force does none, and that a force at a right angle to the
  motion does none. Recompute: the sum of the works. Check: constant-acceleration kinematics for the
  speed.
- `work-path`: a force field and three paths between the same two points. Shows that gravity and a
  spring do the same work along every path while friction does not. Recompute: closed-form line
  integrals. Check: numerical ∫F·dr along each path.
- `track` *(exists)*: bar charts of K, U and thermal energy for a car on a hilly track, with and
  without friction, and a loop-the-loop. Shows that the speed at a point depends on the height and
  not the path, that friction adds thermal energy f·d, and that a loop needs N ≥ 0 at the top.
  Recompute: energy conservation. Check: integrate Newton's equation along the track.
- `energy-diagram`: a one-dimensional U(x) with a total-energy line. Shows turning points, stable and
  unstable equilibria, F = −dU/dx, and escape over a barrier. Recompute: −dU/dx and the roots of
  E = U. Check: numerical integration of the motion, and the period from ∮ dx/v.
- Application lab `automotive-power`: a car climbing a grade at highway speed. Shows that the engine
  power is the force times the speed, and how much of it goes to climbing and how much to losses.
  Recompute: P = F v. Check: ΔE over a measured distance divided by the time. Numbers: §7.4,
  "Automotive Power Driving Uphill" (1200 kg, a 15% grade, 90 km/h, 25% lost).
- Worked cases from: §7.1 "Work Done by a Spring Force"; §7.3 "Loop-the-Loop" and "Determining a
  Stopping Force"; §7.4 "Pull-Up Power"; §8.1 "Gravitational Potential Energy of a Hiker"; §8.3
  "Simple Pendulum"; §8.4 "Quartic and Quadratic Potential Energy Diagrams".
- Problem families: work at an angle, from a graph or from a spring; the work–energy theorem for a
  speed, a distance or a force; average and instantaneous power; energy conservation with a spring and
  gravity; thermal energy from friction; a diagram read for turning points and speeds; the minimum
  height for a loop. Conceptual: the signs of work; the reference level; "used-up" energy.
- Traps: cos θ left out; the normal force's work on an incline taken as nonzero; kinetic energy with
  v, not v²; mg times the incline's length instead of its height; ½kx² written as kx²; the sign of
  F = −dU/dx; the reference level changing the answer.
- Predictions: `work-energy`: double the speed (K ×4, stopping distance ×4); `work-path`: the same
  spring work along a longer path; `track`: move the zero of U (speeds unchanged); `energy-diagram`:
  raise E past a barrier.

**Unit 5. Momentum and collisions** (Ch 9, 7 sections, 20 examples)

- Sections: §9.1 to §9.7.
- Topics: momentum; impulse; conservation of momentum; elastic, inelastic and perfectly inelastic
  collisions; collisions in two dimensions; the centre of mass; rocket propulsion.
- `impulse`: an F–t curve the student shapes. Shows that a short large force and a long small one
  with equal area give the same Δp, and that impulse is a vector. Recompute: the area under F(t).
  Check: integrate m dv/dt = F.
- `collide` *(exists)*: a one-dimensional collision from elastic to perfectly inelastic. Shows that
  momentum is conserved for every collision, that the kinetic energy lost grows as the bodies stick
  more, and that equal masses with one at rest swap velocities when elastic. Recompute: the closed
  form. Check: the centre-of-mass frame, and the energy lost ½μ(1 − e²)v_rel².
- `collide-2d`: an oblique collision of two discs. Shows that momentum is conserved by components
  and, for equal masses with one at rest in an elastic collision, the paths part at a right angle.
  Recompute: the closed form. Check: energy conservation (elastic) and the unchanged centre-of-mass
  velocity.
- `center-of-mass`: point masses, a rod, a hoop and a shell bursting in flight. Shows that the centre
  of mass keeps its parabola whatever the fragments do. Recompute: the weighted position. Check:
  numerical integration of x dm for the continuous body, and the burst's centre of mass against the
  parabola.
- `rocket`: a rocket burning at a steady rate, with and without gravity. Shows that Δv grows with
  ln(m₀/m_f) and not with the mass burned, and that gravity costs g t_b. Recompute:
  Δv = v_e ln(m₀/m_f) − g t_b. Check: numerical integration of m dv/dt = ṁ v_e − mg. A scenario uses
  the book's spacecraft thrust (§9.7).
- Application lab `crumple-zone`: a driver stopped by a seatbelt and an airbag. Shows that stopping
  the same Δp over a longer time cuts the average force, F = Δp/Δt. Recompute: Δp over the stopping
  time. Check: the work–energy view, F × stopping distance = ½mv². Numbers: §9.2, "The Benefits of
  Impulse" (27 m/s, 860 N, about 1 s against a longer stop).
- Application lab `crash-reconstruction`: a car striking a parked truck that then skids. Shows how
  a skid length gives the truck's speed after the crash, and the car's speed before it, from
  conservation of momentum. Recompute: v_truck = √(2μ g d), then momentum. Check: the work done by
  friction on the truck equals its kinetic energy. Numbers: §9.4, "Analyzing a Car Crash" (1200 kg,
  3000 kg, 10 m, μ = 0.62).
- Worked cases from: §9.2 "The Arizona Meteor Crater" and "The iPhone Drop"; §9.3 "Colliding Carts" and
  "Ice Hockey 1"; §9.4 "Ice Hockey 2"; §9.5 "Traffic Collision"; §9.6 "Center of Mass of the Earth-Moon
  System"; §9.7 "Thrust on a Spacecraft".
- Problem families: impulse from a graph; a bounce and a stop; momentum of a system by components;
  recoil; elastic by relative velocity; perfectly inelastic; a given energy loss; two dimensions; the
  centre of mass of discrete, continuous and composite bodies; Δv for a mass ratio; thrust against
  weight. Conceptual: equal momentum and unequal kinetic energy; which collisions are possible.
- Traps: a rebound impulse taken as mv instead of 2mv; momentum added as magnitudes; elastic assumed
  when the bodies stick; e = 1 applied to an inelastic case; the midpoint taken as the centre of mass
  of unequal masses; Δv linear in burned mass; log base 10 for ln.
- Predictions: `impulse`: the same area, a longer time (smaller peak force); `collide`: equal masses,
  one at rest, elastic (velocities swap), and perfectly inelastic (maximum loss); `center-of-mass`: a
  burst in flight (the centre of mass is unmoved); `rocket`: double the exhaust speed (Δv doubles) and
  double the propellant (Δv less than doubles).

**Unit 6. Fixed-axis rotation** (Ch 10, 8 sections, 19 examples)

- Sections: §10.1 to §10.8.
- Topics: rotational variables; constant angular acceleration; angular and translational quantities;
  moment of inertia and rotational kinetic energy; calculating moments of inertia and the
  parallel-axis theorem; torque; Newton's second law for rotation; work and power in rotation.
- `rotor` *(exists)*: a disc or rod under a torque. Shows that α = τ/I and that mass farther out lowers
  α. Recompute: τ = Iα. Check: integrate θ and ω, and the work τθ against ½Iω².
- `inertia`: rod, ring, disc, sphere and a composite about any axis. Shows that I depends on how far
  the mass is from the axis, and the parallel-axis shift. Recompute: the tabulated forms and
  `parallelAxisMass`. Check: numerical integration of r² dm.
- `massive-pulley`: an Atwood machine whose pulley has inertia. Shows that the two tensions differ and
  a falls short of the massless case. Recompute: a = (m₂ − m₁)g/(m₁ + m₂ + I/R²). Check: energy
  conservation including the pulley's rotation.
- Application lab `helicopter-rotor`: the blades of a rescue helicopter. Shows that the stored
  rotational energy is ½Iω² with I of a thin rod about one end, and how it compares with the
  helicopter's translational energy. Recompute: ½(4 × ⅓mL²)ω². Check: numerical integration of
  r² dm along a blade. Numbers: §10.4, "Calculating Helicopter Energies" (four blades of 4.00 m and
  50.0 kg, 300 rpm, a 1000 kg helicopter).
- Worked cases from: §10.1 "Wind Turbine"; §10.2 "Calculating the Acceleration of a Fishing Reel";
  §10.3 "Linear Acceleration of a Centrifuge"; §10.5 "Rod and Solid Sphere"; §10.6 "Calculating
  Torque"; §10.8 "Rotational Work: A Pulley".
- Problem families: a wheel spinning up or down (constant α); angular to linear quantities; I of
  composites; τ = Iα with a pulley; rotational energy; torque from gravity; rotational work and
  power. Conceptual: which shape accelerates fastest under one torque.
- Traps: mass used for I; a rod about its end taken as ML²/12; the parallel-axis term subtracted going
  away from the centre; the pulley treated as massless; torque without sin θ or the lever arm; rpm
  used as rad/s.
- Predictions: `rotor`: move mass outward (α falls); a ring and a disc of equal mass and radius under
  one torque.

**Unit 7. Rolling and angular momentum** (Ch 11, 4 sections, 10 examples)

- Sections: §11.1 to §11.4.
- Topics: rolling without slipping; rolling with slipping; angular momentum of a particle and of a
  rigid body; torque as dL/dt; conservation of angular momentum; precession of a gyroscope.
- `rolling`: a wheel, hoop, disc or sphere rolling down an incline, with a traced rim point. Shows that
  the contact point is instantly at rest, that mass and radius cancel so only the shape sets
  a = g sin θ/(1 + k²/R²), and that a sphere beats a disc beats a hoop. Recompute: the closed form
  and the friction needed. Check: energy, ½mv²(1 + k²/R²) = mgh, and a numerical run.
- `particle-l`: a particle passing a point on a straight line. Shows that L depends on the origin,
  and that on a straight line past the origin it is constant and equals p times the miss distance.
  Recompute: r × p. Check: the area swept per unit time, dA/dt = L/2m, integrated numerically.
- `spinner`: a stool with masses pulled in or out. Shows that L is conserved when no outside torque
  acts, so ω rises as I falls and the kinetic energy rises too. Recompute: L = Iω. Check: the work
  done pulling in, ∫F dr, equals the energy gained.
- `gyro`: a spinning wheel on a pivot. Shows that precession is the torque over the angular momentum,
  Ω = mgr/(Iω), slower for a faster spin. Recompute: the closed form. Check: integrate dL/dt = τ.
- Application lab `rover-wheel`: a wheel from the Curiosity rover rolling down a basin. Shows the
  speed at the bottom from energy, with the rotational share, and how slipping changes it. Recompute:
  v = √(2gh/(1 + k²/R²)). Check: a time-stepped roll. Numbers: §11.1, "Curiosity Rover" (a 25 cm
  wheel, 25 m of drop).
- Worked cases from: §11.1 "Rolling Down an Inclined Plane" and "Rolling Down an Inclined Plane with
  Slipping"; §11.2 "Angular Momentum of Three Particles"; §11.3 "Coupled Flywheels" and "Dismount from
  a High Bar"; §11.4 "Period of Precession".
- Problem families: speed at the bottom of an incline; the friction needed to roll; L about different
  origins; conservation with a changing I; a collision with a pivoted body; torque against a rate
  of change of L; precession rate. Conceptual: the direction of friction on a rolling wheel; when L is
  conserved and when not.
- Traps: v = ωR used while slipping; friction at a rolling contact taken as μN; rotational energy
  left out of a rolling body; L = mvr when r and v are not perpendicular; kinetic energy assumed
  conserved because L is; a pivot's reaction torque ignored.
- Predictions: `rolling`: which reaches the bottom first (the sphere), and double the radius (no
  change); `spinner`: pull the masses in (ω up, K up); `gyro`: double the spin (precession halves).

**Unit 8. Static equilibrium and elasticity** (Ch 12, 4 sections, 10 examples)

- Sections: §12.1 to §12.4.
- Topics: the conditions ΣF = 0 and Στ = 0; the centre of gravity; choosing the axis; examples
  (ladders, beams, cables, hinges); stress, strain and elastic moduli; elasticity and plasticity.
- `beam`: a board on two supports with a person who walks along it. Shows that the support forces
  trade off linearly, and that it tips when one support force reaches zero. Recompute: ΣF = 0 and
  Στ = 0. Check: the torque balance about each support gives the same forces.
- `ladder`: a ladder against a wall, with a person on it. Shows that the friction needed does not
  depend on the ladder's weight or length, only on the angle and where the person stands. Recompute:
  ΣF = 0 and Στ = 0 (`statics`). Check: moments about a different point agree.
- `stress-strain`: a wire loaded past its yield. Shows a straight line to the yield, then permanent
  stretch. Recompute: σ = Eε and ΔL = FL/(AE). Check: the strain energy, the area under the curve,
  against ½FΔL.
- Application lab `car-balance`: the weight a car puts on its front and rear wheels. Shows where the
  centre of mass sits from two axle loads, and how braking shifts load forward. Recompute: the moment
  balance about an axle. Check: the same balance about the other axle. Numbers: §12.1, "Center of
  Gravity of a Car" (a 2.5 m wheelbase, 52% of the weight on the front wheels).
- Worked cases from: §12.1 "A Breaking Tension"; §12.2 "The Torque Balance", "Forces in the Forearm" and
  "A Ladder Resting Against a Wall"; §12.3 "Compressive Stress in a Pillar" and "Stretching a Rod".
- Problem families: a beam on two supports; a ladder; a sign hung by a cable and a strut; a wire's
  stretch; a tipping limit; a hinge's reaction. Conceptual: where to put the axis; a determinate
  case against an indeterminate one.
- Traps: a force's lever arm forgotten; friction at the wall assumed; the signs of torques; stress
  and force confused; Young's modulus units; the weight placed at the wrong point.
- Predictions: `ladder`: a heavier person higher up; `stress-strain`: unload after yield.

**Unit 9. Gravitation** (Ch 13, 7 sections, 15 examples)

- Sections: §13.1 to §13.5 (§13.6 tidal forces and §13.7 Einstein's theory of gravity are left out).
- Topics: Newton's law of universal gravitation; gravitation near Earth's surface and above it;
  gravitational potential energy and total energy; escape speed; satellite orbits and their energy;
  Kepler's three laws.
- `orbit`: a satellite near a planet, with the launch speed the student sets. Shows circular speed
  √(GM/r), T ∝ r^(3/2), E = −GMm/2r, and an ellipse or escape as the speed grows. Recompute: the
  closed forms. Check: RK4 of the inverse-square motion, with L and E conserved.
- `kepler`: an ellipse with equal-area wedges. Shows equal areas in equal times and T² ∝ a³ across
  orbits. Recompute: the Kepler solution. Check: the area swept per step in the numerical orbit.
- Application lab `iss-orbit`: the International Space Station. Shows its orbital speed and period
  from its altitude, and that the speed does not depend on its mass. Recompute: v = √(GM/r),
  T = 2πr/v. Check: the numerical orbit. Numbers: §13.4, "The International Space Station".
- Worked cases from: §13.1 "A Collision in Orbit"; §13.2 "Masses of Earth and Moon" and "Zero Apparent
  Weight"; §13.3 "Escape from Earth"; §13.4 "Determining the Mass of Earth"; §13.5 "Orbit of Halley's
  Comet".
- Problem families: g at a height; orbital speed, period and energy; escape speed; Kepler's third
  law from data; the energy change between orbits. Conceptual: weightlessness in orbit.
- Traps: r taken from the surface, not the centre; g held constant at altitude; escape speed confused
  with orbital speed (a factor of √2); "no gravity" in orbit.
- Predictions: `orbit`: double the radius (T ×2√2), and double the satellite's mass (speed
  unchanged).

**Unit 10. Fluid mechanics** (Ch 14, 7 sections, 9 examples)

- Sections: §14.1 to §14.7. §14.7 (viscosity and turbulence) is covered by its two examples on flow
  rate and the Reynolds number, not by a lab.
- Topics: density and pressure; measuring pressure (gauge and absolute); Pascal's principle and
  hydraulics; Archimedes' principle and buoyancy; fluid dynamics and continuity; Bernoulli's
  equation; viscosity and turbulence.
- `pressure-depth`: containers of different shapes and a hydraulic lift. Shows that pressure depends
  on depth and not on the shape, and a small force on a small piston balances a large one on a
  large one. Recompute: P = P₀ + ρgh and F₂/F₁ = A₂/A₁. Check: the force balance on a thin slice of
  fluid.
- `buoyancy`: a block floating or sinking. Shows that the buoyant force is the weight of the fluid
  displaced, and the submerged fraction is ρ_object/ρ_fluid. Recompute: Archimedes. Check: the
  pressure integrated over the body's surface.
- `bernoulli`: a pipe with a changing cross-section and height, and a draining tank. Shows that the
  speed rises and the pressure falls where the pipe narrows, and Torricelli's √(2gh). Recompute:
  continuity and Bernoulli. Check: the work–energy balance on a fluid parcel, and a numerical tank
  drain.
- Application lab `brake-hydraulics`: a car's brake system. Shows that the master cylinder's pressure
  reaches every wheel cylinder, so the force is multiplied by the ratio of the areas. Recompute:
  F₂ = F₁(d₂/d₁)². Check: the work balance, F₁ x₁ = F₂ x₂ for incompressible fluid. Numbers: §14.3,
  "Calculating Force on Wheel Cylinders" (a 0.500 cm master piston, 2.50 cm wheel pistons, 500 N).
- Application lab `fire-hose`: a fire hose with a nozzle on a ladder. Shows the pressure at the nozzle
  from continuity, Bernoulli and the rise. Recompute: Bernoulli between hose and nozzle. Check: the
  energy balance per unit volume. Numbers: §14.6, "Calculating Pressure: A Fire Hose Nozzle" (6.40 cm
  hose, 40.0 L/s, 1.62×10⁶ N/m², a 10.0 m rise, a 3.00 cm nozzle).
- Worked cases from: §14.1 "What Force Must a Dam Withstand?"; §14.2 "Fluid Heights in an Open U-Tube";
  §14.4 "Calculating Average Density"; §14.5 "Calculating Fluid Speed through a Nozzle"; §14.7
  "Using Flow Rate: Turbulent Flow or Laminar Flow?".
- Problem families: pressure at a depth; a hydraulic lift; a floating or sunken body; a speed or pressure
  from continuity and Bernoulli; a draining tank; laminar against turbulent flow. Conceptual: which of
  two shapes has more bottom force.
- Traps: gauge and absolute pressure mixed; the body's weight used for the buoyant force; Bernoulli
  applied across a pump; density and specific gravity confused; psi and kPa; diameter used for area.
- Predictions: `pressure-depth`: a wider container at the same depth (the same pressure);
  `bernoulli`: halve the pipe's area (speed ×2, pressure down).

**Unit 11. Oscillations** (Ch 15, 6 sections, 5 examples)

- Sections: §15.1 to §15.6.
- Topics: simple harmonic motion; energy in it; the circular-motion picture; pendulums; damped
  oscillations; forced oscillations and resonance.
- `spring-mass`: a block on a spring, horizontal and vertical. Shows that the period depends on m and
  k and not on the amplitude, and gravity only moves the equilibrium. Recompute: T = 2π√(m/k).
  Check: RK4, with the period read from zero crossings.
- `pendulum`: a pendulum from small to large amplitude, and a rod swinging about one end. Shows that the
  period is amplitude-independent only for small angles and grows as T ≈ T₀(1 + θ₀²/16). Recompute: the
  small-angle T₀. Check: the exact period by the arithmetic–geometric mean, against a simulation.
- `resonance`: a damped, driven oscillator. Shows a peak near the natural frequency whose width grows
  with the damping, and a phase that turns from 0 to π through it. Recompute: the steady-state
  amplitude and phase. Check: a time integration run to steady state.
- Application lab `skyscraper-damper`: a pendulum on a tall building that cancels its sway. Shows that
  a pendulum tuned to the building's frequency moves against it, and how its length is set from the
  frequency. Recompute: T = 2π√(I/mgd) for a physical pendulum. Check: a simulation of the building and
  the pendulum together. Numbers: §15.4, "Reducing the Swaying of a Skyscraper" (a 0.50 Hz sway).
- Worked cases from: §15.1 "Determining the Frequency of Medical Ultrasound" and "Determining the
  Equations of Motion for a Block and a Spring"; §15.4 "Measuring Acceleration due to Gravity by the
  Period of a Pendulum".
- Problem families: the period from m and k; the energy at a displacement; a pendulum's length from a
  period; the equations of motion from initial conditions; a damped decay; the resonance amplitude.
  Conceptual: what changes the period.
- Traps: amplitude put in the period; ω and f mixed; degrees in a phase; the pendulum's period taken
  as depending on mass; the vertical spring's equilibrium forgotten.
- Predictions: `spring-mass`: double the mass (T ×√2), and double the amplitude (T unchanged);
  `pendulum`: a large angle against a small one.

**Unit 12. Waves** (Ch 16, 6 sections, 7 examples)

- Sections: §16.1 to §16.6.
- Topics: travelling waves; the mathematics of a wave; the speed on a stretched string; energy and
  power; interference; standing waves and resonance.
- `traveling-wave`: a wave on a string with the tension and density set by the student. Shows that the
  speed is set by the medium, v = √(T/μ), not by the frequency or amplitude, and that each point only
  moves up and down. Recompute: v = √(T/μ). Check: a finite-difference solution of the wave
  equation, with the pulse's arrival time.
- `standing-wave`: two waves, or a string fixed at both ends. Shows constructive and destructive
  interference, nodes and antinodes, and f_n = nv/2L. Recompute: the sum and the mode formula. Check:
  the numerical modes of the string with fixed ends.
- Application lab `guitar-string`: the high E and low E strings of a guitar. Shows how a heavier string
  needs a different tension for the same wave speed, and why pitch follows √(T/μ). Recompute:
  v = √(T/μ) and f = v/2L. Check: the numerical fundamental. Numbers: §16.3, "The Wave Speed of a
  Guitar String" (μ = 3.09×10⁻⁴ kg/m, 56.40 N, a low string about 20 times heavier).
- Worked cases from: §16.1 "Wave on a String"; §16.2 "Characteristics of a Traveling Wave on a String"
  and "Interference of Waves on a String"; §16.4 "Power Supplied by a String Vibrator"; §16.6
  "Standing Waves on a String".
- Problem families: the wave speed from a string's data; a wave function read for k, ω and v; the
  power from the amplitude; the modes of a string; interference from a path difference. Conceptual:
  wave speed against particle speed.
- Traps: the particle speed taken as the wave speed; the frequency thought to change the speed; a
  fixed end's inversion forgotten; power ∝ A taken instead of A².
- Predictions: `traveling-wave`: four times the tension (v ×2); `standing-wave`: double the amplitude
  (power ×4).

**Unit 13. Sound** (Ch 17, 8 sections, 8 examples)

- Sections: §17.1 to §17.8.
- Topics: sound as a pressure wave; the speed of sound; intensity and the decibel; standing waves in
  tubes; musical sources; beats; the Doppler effect; shock waves.
- `intensity`: a point source in the open. Shows that intensity falls as 1/r², so each doubling of
  distance costs about 6 dB and doubling the power adds 3 dB. Recompute: β = 10 log₁₀(I/I₀). Check: the
  intensity integrated over a sphere gives back the power.
- `pipe-modes`: open and closed tubes, with a two-frequency beats scenario. Shows that a closed tube has
  only odd harmonics and f₁ = v/4L, and that two close frequencies beat at |f₁ − f₂|. Recompute: the
  formulas, with v from the temperature. Check: the numerical modes of the wave equation, and the
  envelope of the summed sinusoids.
- `doppler`: a moving source or observer, with a Mach cone past the speed of sound. Shows that moving
  the source and moving the observer give different shifts, and the cone forms above Mach 1.
  Recompute: f′ = f(v ± v_o)/(v ∓ v_s). Check: a simulated pulse train, with the frequency read from
  the arrival times.
- Application lab `train-horn`: a train sounding its horn as it passes. Shows the higher pitch on the
  way in, the lower on the way out, and the size of the drop. Recompute: the Doppler shift in each
  direction. Check: the pulse-train simulation. Numbers: §17.7, "Calculating a Doppler Shift" (a 150 Hz
  horn, 35.0 m/s, 340 m/s).
- Worked cases from: §17.2 "Calculating Wavelengths"; §17.3 "Calculating Sound Intensity Levels" and
  "Changing Intensity Levels of a Sound"; §17.5 "Finding the Length of a Tube with a 128-Hz
  Fundamental"; §17.6 "Find the Beat Frequency Between Two Tuning Forks".
- Problem families: the level from an intensity and back; a distance change; the modes of a tube; the
  beat frequency; a Doppler shift in the four cases; the Mach angle. Conceptual: why adding levels does
  not add decibels.
- Traps: two 60 dB sources taken as 120 dB (it is 63 dB); the speed of sound taken as fixed; a closed
  tube given even harmonics; the Doppler sign swapped; 20 log used for an intensity.
- Predictions: `intensity`: double the distance (−6 dB), and two identical speakers (+3 dB);
  `doppler`: the source approaching against the observer approaching at one speed.
