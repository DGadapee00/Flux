1Requirement

Turn “make a tank” into requirements someone can verify.

The idea

A dome closes the end of a tank. A requirement is a measurable promise. Design pressure includes the demanding cases the system must survive; operating pressure describes normal use.

Walk through it

  1. Name the fluid, operating temperature, pressure range, envelope, service life and interfaces. Mark unknowns instead of guessing.
  2. Set the example pressure and diameter below. Change one at a time and watch how the seam loads change.
  3. Write how each requirement will be checked: calculation, measurement, inspection or test. Identify who owns each unresolved input.

Apparatus and readings

Start with units and load cases. The calculator models pressure membrane loads only. It does not cover supports, launch loads, buckling, fatigue or a complete vessel design.

Expected evidence: Every input has a unit, a reason and a planned verification. Increasing pressure or diameter increases the load the seams carry.

Investigate: “Strong enough” has no measurable limit. A pressure input without its failure cases can produce a convincing but incomplete sizing result.

The engineer decides

  • The design pressure: the highest the tank can see, with its failure cases counted.
  • The factors. NASA asks for 1.4 on ultimate strength, and a proof test at 1.05 times design pressure for a propellant tank.
  • How each requirement is verified: by analysis, by test or by inspection.
  • The weld class. A weld whose single failure loses the vehicle is Class A.

What goes wrong

A requirement nobody can test

It was written as a wish, such as “shall not leak”, with no rate, pressure or method.

How it is found: At the verification plan, when no test or inspection can be named against it.

A pressure that leaves out a failure

The design pressure took the normal case and not the stuck regulator or the warm tank.

How it is found: In review of the pressure system, before the wall is sized.

Worked example · Write a verifiable requirement

A team asks for a light tank with a 5 m diameter. It has written down a normal pressure but has not reviewed regulator faults, temperatures or service life. You have enough information to explore load sensitivity, but not to release a design.

  1. Replace “light” with a mass budget and its owner. Replace “holds pressure” with a pressure envelope, temperature envelope and leak criterion. Each requirement needs a verification method.
  2. For a pressure-only cylindrical barrel model, cut the cylinder along its length. Pressure pushes on the projected area, 2rL. Two cut walls each carry a line load N over length L: 2NL = p(2rL), so N = pr. This is a barrel-seam model, not the stress solution for a curved dome.
  3. At 200 kPa = 0.2 N/mm² and r = 2,500 mm, N = 500 N/mm. Raising pressure by 20% raises this load by 20%. That sensitivity does not establish the actual design pressure.

The paper

Requirements
Each with a number and a way to verify it.
Verification plan
Which test, analysis or inspection closes each requirement.

Ask on the floor

  • Where does the design pressure come from, and which failure sets it?
  • Which welds on this part are Class A?

What to produce

  • Requirement and units, including fluid, pressure and temperature envelopes
  • Source or owner of each input and the unresolved load cases
  • Verification method and required evidence for three requirements
  • Who may freeze the requirement and what would reopen it
Transfer the idea

The diameter stays fixed but a new load case raises design pressure. Which sizing, tooling, joint, inspection and test decisions must you revisit?

Review checklist

  • I distinguish operating pressure from the reviewed design envelope.
  • I show units and identify the limits of the pressure-only model.
  • I pair requirements with measurements, tests or analysis.
  • I name unresolved inputs and their decision owners.

2Design for it

Sketch a part that can be formed, joined, measured and inspected.

The idea

A gore is a curved panel, like a segment of an orange peel. A weld land is a thicker edge reserved for joining. A datum is the reference used to locate and measure a feature.

Walk through it

  1. Sketch the dome, ring, crown fitting and barrel interface. Label which seams must seal and which carry load.
  2. Compare a formed one-piece dome with a dome assembled from gores. Record tooling access, machine capacity, seams, inspection access and cost drivers.
  3. Mark weld lands, smooth thickness transitions, clamp space, tool entry and exit, and inspection access before detailing the drawing.

Apparatus and readings

Overlay the tool, fixture and inspection probe on the proposed seam. Check the whole path, including starts and stops.

Expected evidence: The welding tool can follow each seam, the fixture can support it, and the inspector can reach the required surfaces.

Investigate: A seam disappears behind the fitting or a sharp land transition concentrates stress. A model that looks complete may still be unbuildable.

The engineer decides

  • Seam shapes the tool can follow: straight or a steady curve, with room for the tool and the clamps.
  • The lands: their thickness and width, and how the land blends into the thinner skin.
  • Where each weld starts and stops, and what closes the exit.
  • What the drawing says: the weld class, the procedure, and the inspection each seam gets.
  • How few welds. Fewer panels, or a spun dome, put less weld in the part.

What goes wrong

A seam the tool cannot reach

The joint was drawn before the tool and the fixture were.

How it is found: At the first tooling review, or on the shop floor.

Thickness that changes along a seam

A fixed pin suits one thickness.

How it is found: When the schedule cannot be qualified. A retractable pin is one answer.

A land that ends in a sharp step

Stress concentrates where the thick land meets the thin skin.

How it is found: In the stress analysis, or as a crack starting there.

Worked example · Choose a build route with a trade study

A fictional supplier offers Route A: formed gores, edge machining and multiple welds, with setup cost 12,000 and recurring cost 8,000 per article. Route B uses a one-piece formed dome with setup cost 48,000 and recurring cost 4,000. Both still need interface joining, inspection and qualified capability.

  1. Separate feasibility from price. Check material condition, forming capacity, tool reach, joint access, metrology and inspection coverage first. An inexpensive route that cannot meet the requirements is not an option.
  2. Total cost in this teaching model is setup + quantity × recurring cost. At two articles, Route A costs 28,000 and Route B costs 56,000. At nine articles they tie at 84,000. This model leaves out scrap, qualification, delivery time and redesign.
  3. For a small demonstrator, record why you prefer Route A only if its technical evidence is acceptable. A larger order can change that choice. Draw the weld lands, datums, tool approach and clamp clearance before releasing panel drawings.
  4. Prepare a make-or-buy list: panel blanks, ring, fitting, forming service, machining, joining, fixture, inspection and test services. For each line record quantity, drawing revision, material condition, required certificate, supplier capability, lead time and incoming check. Budget qualification coupons, sacrificial stock, tooling and possible rework as well as finished parts.

The paper

Drawing
Seams, lands, weld class, procedure and inspection callouts.
Design review record
What welding, tooling and inspection asked for, and what changed.

Ask on the floor

  • What would the welding engineer change about this drawing?
  • Which seam is hardest to reach, clamp or inspect?

What to produce

  • Feasible routes and the requirement that rules each in or out
  • Quantity, cost assumptions, capacity and supplier questions
  • Chosen route, rejected route and what would change the choice
  • Sketch or sketch description of seams, datums, clamps and inspection access
Transfer the idea

Make an unpressurized card mock-up over a bowl or form. Mark panel identities, seams, the ring and fitting. Use a pencil as the joining tool: can it approach every seam while another hand holds the panel? Record one access problem and revise the layout. The mock-up demonstrates sequence and access, not strength.

Review checklist

  • I check technical feasibility before comparing price.
  • I separate setup cost from recurring cost.
  • I include tool, fixture and inspection access in the design.
  • I name a change in requirements that could reverse my decision.

3Material and process

Choose material and joining process together, then explain how you would characterize them.

The idea

An alloy is a controlled mixture of metals. Temper describes its processing condition. Yield is the start of permanent deformation; ultimate strength is the maximum tensile stress reached in a test. A coupon is a small test specimen.

Walk through it

  1. Compare the reference 2195 alloy with an alternative using density, strength at service temperature, availability, formability, joining and cost. The reference choice is not automatically your choice.
  2. Trace the plate certificate to its alloy, temper and lot. Identify which properties are supplier values and which are qualified design values.
  3. Plan tensile coupons of the parent plate and welded joint in relevant directions and temperatures. Compare yield, ultimate strength and elongation, including result spread.

Apparatus and readings

A prepared coupon sits in grips. A load cell measures force; an extensometer measures extension over a known gauge length. Convert force to stress using the original cross-sectional area and extension to strain using the gauge length.

Expected evidence: A traceable specimen, valid setup and usable stress-strain curve support separate yield, ultimate and ductility results for the plate and joint.

Investigate: Grip slip, an incorrect specimen area or an untraceable coupon makes the result suspect. A plate certificate does not prove the strength of a weld.

The engineer decides

  • The alloy. 2195 is about 30% stronger and 5% less dense than the 2219 it replaced, and it resists fracture at cryogenic temperature.
  • The process. Stir welding joins the metal without melting it, so it avoids the cracks and pores that come with solidifying.
  • What the weld will be worth. In 2195 plate a stir weld averaged 58.7 ksi, against 44 ksi for a fusion weld.
  • Traceability: every plate keeps its lot number from the mill to the finished dome.

What goes wrong

A weld far weaker than the plate

Heat undoes the precipitates that give the alloy its strength. Melting does it most.

How it is found: In the tensile tests that qualify the weld procedure.

Plate from the wrong lot or temper

The stock was not checked against its certificate when it arrived.

How it is found: At receiving inspection, or much later as low test results.

Worked example · Turn a tensile reading into a material decision

A plate brochure looks strong. The part, however, contains formed and welded material used at a different temperature from the brochure test. You need evidence for those conditions, not a material name alone.

  1. Compare candidate materials using mass, strength, fracture behavior at the service temperature, forming, joining, availability and traceability. The historical dome uses 2195 and friction stir welding; treat that as a case study rather than a universal prescription.
  2. A fictional tensile coupon starts 10 mm wide and 3 mm thick, so its original area is 30 mm². A 9,000 N load corresponds to engineering stress 9,000 / 30 = 300 N/mm² = 300 MPa. A 0.5 mm extension over a 50 mm gauge length is strain 0.01, or 1%.
  3. That single point is not yield strength or ultimate strength. Yield needs a defined method on the stress-strain curve; ultimate is the maximum engineering stress. A valid characterization plan controls specimen location, direction, condition, temperature, calibration, sample population and result spread. A batch average is not automatically a design allowable.
  4. Alloy choice also changes the supply plan. Ask whether the required stock form, temper, thickness and lot certification are available, whether the forming route changes the material condition, and whether suitable joint qualification exists. If an alternate alloy is easier to buy, repeat the linked material, forming, joint and inspection review rather than substituting on name alone.

The paper

Material certificate
The mill’s record of alloy, temper, lot and test results.
Lot traceability
The record that ties each gore to the plate it was cut from.

Ask on the floor

  • Why this alloy and not 2219, and what did the change cost in weldability?
  • How is a plate’s lot number carried onto the part?

What to produce

  • Material and process candidates, service conditions and tradeoffs
  • Coupon location, orientation, condition, temperature and lot identity
  • Measurements needed for stress, strain, yield, ultimate and elongation
  • Evidence required to establish joint design values and who approves them
Transfer the idea

A welded coupon has a lower maximum load than a plate coupon of the same original area. Explain what that changes in the joint design, and why increasing plate strength alone may not solve it.

Review checklist

  • I convert load to stress using original area and retain units.
  • I distinguish a curve point, yield, ultimate and a design allowable.
  • I characterize the welded condition and relevant service conditions.
  • I trace specimens to lots and account for variation.

4Gore panels

Follow raw stock into a curved panel with controlled wall and weld lands.

The idea

Forming changes shape. Machining removes material. A tolerance is the permitted variation in a feature; nominal size is the intended value.

Walk through it

  1. Plan blanks, forming tooling and the forming route with the supplier. Preserve identification when cutting each blank.
  2. Plan CNC machining and workholding to leave the skin and the thicker weld lands. Model the stock and the fixture before making cuts.
  3. Measure the panel against the drawing: profile, wall thickness, lands and edge location. Compare actual readings with the stated tolerances.

Apparatus and readings

Forming tooling gives the panel its curve. The mill follows a programmed path to remove stock. A thickness gauge and profile measurement check the result against drawing references; the historical dome used photogrammetry for shape.

Expected evidence: Measured thickness and profile meet the drawing, and weld lands remain continuous. The inspection map shows where each measurement was taken.

Investigate: A panel looks smooth but has a thin spot, a springback error or a damaged land. Appearance and the CNC program are not dimensional evidence.

The engineer decides

  • The land thickness, from the load the seam carries and the strength the weld keeps.
  • Which check governs. This weld yields at 36.6 ksi and breaks at 58.7 ksi, so not yielding at proof sets the land before strength at 1.4 times pressure does.
  • Gores or one piece. NASA has also spun a dome from a single blank and welded it only to its ring.
  • How the curve is measured. NASA used photogrammetry on this dome.

What goes wrong

A land too thin

It was sized on the plate’s strength, not the weld’s.

How it is found: In the stress analysis, or at proof if it was missed.

Gores that do not meet

Springback and machining stresses moved the edges.

How it is found: At fit-up, as a gap or a step between panels.

Worked example · Plan stock, forming, machining and metrology

A panel must end with a thin skin and thicker weld lands. You cannot treat the nominal CAD shape as evidence that every location has enough metal left.

  1. Receiving checks material condition, certificate and lot identity. Cutting creates labeled blanks. Forming changes curvature and can leave springback or local thinning. Check the formed shape before spending time on final machining.
  2. CAM turns a reviewed CAD model into toolpaths. A setup locates the panel from drawing datums, supports it without unacceptable distortion and keeps clamps out of the cutter path. A qualified operator proves the program and checks tool and work offsets. Machine capacity must cover the actual work envelope and fixture.
  3. Choose the forming route with the specialist: a die or press route shapes a blank with tooling; spinning forms a rotating blank progressively. These routes have different capacity, material-flow, tooling and qualification needs. The mill does not replace forming by default. For either route, ask what stock, heat treatment, intermediate checks and final property evidence are needed before buying a blank.
  4. Use a measurement map after machining. In a fictional drawing, a land is 6.00 ± 0.15 mm, so its interval is 5.85 to 6.15 mm. A nominal reading is not enough if another location is thin. Near a boundary, apply the inspection plan’s uncertainty and decision rule instead of rounding a reading into tolerance.

The paper

Drawing
The land’s thickness and width, and the class of each weld.
In-process inspection record
The measured thickness and contour of each gore.

Ask on the floor

  • How thick is the land against the skin, and which check set it?
  • How is the contour of a formed gore measured?

What to produce

  • Stock identity, blank plan, forming route and checks after forming
  • Machining sequence, datums, workholding and remaining weld stock
  • Measurement locations, instruments, calibration and drawing limits
  • Thin-spot or profile departure, containment and review owner
Transfer the idea

A measured profile looks wrong while the part is clamped but recovers after release. What should the manufacturing and metrology teams investigate before changing the CNC toolpath?

Review checklist

  • I preserve part identity from stock through the panel.
  • I explain forming, CNC machining and measuring as distinct operations.
  • I check local thickness and profile rather than appearance alone.
  • I consider uncertainty and the agreed acceptance rule near a limit.
