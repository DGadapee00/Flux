Part I

Engines, plants, heat pumps and what heat is worth

1Exergy: what heat is worth

The first law counts energy; the second says how much of it can become work. That amount, against the surroundings at \(T_0\), is exergy.

Derivation

The most work heat \(Q\) at \(T\) can give runs a Carnot engine that rejects to \(T_0\):

\[ X = Q\left(1 - \frac{T_0}{T}\right). \]

A stream that cools from \(T\) to \(T_0\) as it gives up its heat runs one engine per slice, \(dX = (1 - T_0/T')\,c\,dT'\), which sums to \(Q[1 - T_0\ln(T/T_0)/(T - T_0)]\). Any irreversibility destroys exergy, \(X_\text{destroyed} = T_0S_\text{gen}\).

Worked example · furnace heat for a room

With surroundings at 283 K, a kilojoule at 1500 K is worth \(1 - 283/1500 = 0.81\) kJ of work. Delivered to a room at 295 K it generates \(1/295 - 1/1500 = 2.72\) J/K of entropy and destroys \(283 \times 0.00272 = 0.77\) kJ: 95% of its potential.

Common mistake

Treating every kilojoule alike. At 320 K a kilojoule is worth 0.12 kJ of work; at the surroundings' temperature, nothing, though the energy is all still there.

2Otto and Diesel cycles

Air-standard cycles model an engine as a fixed mass of air heated and cooled, which is enough to compare them.

Derivation

Both compress isentropically by \(r = v_1/v_2\), so \(T_2 = T_1r^{k-1}\). Otto adds heat at constant volume and Diesel at constant pressure, until the volume has grown by the cutoff ratio \(r_c\). Then

\[ \eta_\text{Otto} = 1 - \frac{1}{r^{\,k-1}}, \qquad \eta_\text{Diesel} = 1 - \frac{1}{r^{\,k-1}}\,\frac{r_c^{\,k} - 1}{k\,(r_c - 1)}. \]

The bracket is above 1 for \(r_c > 1\).

Worked example · both at r = 10

With \(k = 1.4\): \(\eta_\text{Otto} = 1 - 10^{-0.4} = 60.2\%\). With cutoff 2, the bracket is \((2^{1.4} - 1)/(1.4 \times 1) = 1.71\), so \(\eta_\text{Diesel} = 1 - 0.398 \times 1.71 = 53.4\%\). At \(r = 18\) the Diesel gives 63.2%.

Common mistake

Calling the Diesel the more efficient cycle. At the same compression ratio it is less efficient; it wins because compressing air alone lets it use a ratio near 18, where a petrol engine would knock.

3The Rankine cycle

A steam plant pumps water to boiler pressure, boils and superheats it, expands it through a turbine and condenses it. Pumping a liquid costs far less work than the turbine gives.

Derivation

Steady-flow energy balances on each part give the work and heat per kilogram. With states 1 (condenser outlet), 2 (pump outlet), 3 (turbine inlet) and 4 (turbine outlet),

\[ \eta = \frac{(h_3 - h_4) - (h_2 - h_1)}{h_3 - h_2}, \qquad w_\text{pump} \approx v_1(p_2 - p_1). \]

The turbine expands isentropically, \(s_4 = s_3\), so a higher boiler pressure at the same \(T_3\), which lowers \(s_3\), leaves state 4 further inside the dome: wetter.

Worked example · 6 MPa and 500 °C

With the condenser at 10 kPa: \(h_1 = 191.8\) kJ/kg, the pump adds 6.1 kJ/kg, \(h_3 = 3423\) kJ/kg, and the turbine exhaust is 83% dry. The efficiency is 38.4%. At 15 MPa it is 41%, with the exhaust 76% dry.

Common mistake

Taking a higher boiler pressure as free efficiency. It wets the turbine exhaust, and below about 88% dryness droplets erode the last blades. Reheating the steam partway through the turbine fixes it.

4Vapour compression and the heat pump

A vapour-compression cycle moves heat uphill: it evaporates a refrigerant where it is cold and condenses it where it is warm, and a compressor does the lifting.

Derivation

The refrigerant leaves the evaporator as saturated vapour (1), is compressed (2), condenses (3) and is throttled at constant enthalpy, \(h_4 = h_3\). Per kilogram, the heat delivered is \(h_2 - h_3\) and the work \(h_2 - h_1\):

\[ \text{COP}_\text{HP} = \frac{h_2 - h_3}{h_2 - h_1} \le \frac{T_H}{T_H - T_L}. \]

A colder outdoor coil means a lower evaporating pressure, a bigger pressure ratio, and more work for each kilojoule delivered.

Worked example · outdoors at 7 °C

With the model refrigerant evaporating at −1 °C and condensing at 35 °C, the compressor does 32.9 kJ/kg and the condenser delivers 205 kJ/kg: COP 6.2. The Carnot limit for 21 °C indoors and 7 °C outdoors is 21.

Common mistake

Taking a heat pump's COP as a fixed property. At −10 °C outdoors the same machine gives 4.1, and the Carnot limit itself has fallen from 21 to 9.5.
