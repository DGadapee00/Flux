Part I

Ideal-gas mixtures

1Mole and mass fractions

A mixture can be described by how many molecules of each gas it holds or by how much each gas weighs. The two descriptions agree only when the molecules weigh the same.

Derivation

A mass \(m_i\) of a gas with molar mass \(M_i\) is \(N_i = m_i/M_i\) moles. The mole fraction and the mass fraction are

\[ y_i = \frac{N_i}{N}, \qquad x_i = \frac{m_i}{m}. \]

Since \(m_i = N_i M_i\) and \(m = N M\), with \(M = m/N\) the mixture's molar mass, they are tied by \(y_i = x_i\,M/M_i\). A gas heavier than the average has a smaller share of the molecules than of the mass.

Worked example · nitrogen and carbon dioxide

1 kg of N₂ (28.0 g/mol) and 1 kg of CO₂ (44.0 g/mol). The amounts are \(1/0.0280 = 35.7\) mol and \(1/0.0440 = 22.7\) mol. CO₂ is half the mass, \(x = 0.5\), but

\[ y_{\mathrm{CO_2}} = \frac{22.7}{35.7 + 22.7} = 0.389 \]

of the molecules.

Common mistake

Reading a mass fraction as a mole fraction, or the reverse. Convert through the molar masses every time.

2Partial pressures

An ideal gas's molecules do not feel one another. In a mixture each gas fills the whole vessel at the mixture's temperature, as if the others were not there.

Derivation

Each gas alone in volume \(V\) at \(T\) would push with \(p_i = N_i \bar R T/V\). The pressures add, Dalton's law, to \(p = N\bar R T/V\), so

\[ p_i = y_i\,p. \]

The mole fraction sets a gas's share of the pressure. The mass fraction does not.

Worked example · a little hydrogen

1 kg of N₂ and 0.1 kg of H₂ (2.02 g/mol) in 1 m³ at 300 K. The hydrogen is \(0.1/0.00202 = 49.6\) mol against 35.7 mol of nitrogen, so \(y_{\mathrm{H_2}} = 0.582\). The total is \(85.3 \times 8.314 \times 300 = 213\) kPa, and hydrogen's share is

\[ p_{\mathrm{H_2}} = 0.582 \times 213 = 124\ \text{kPa}. \]

Split by mass, it would be \(0.0909 \times 213 = 19.3\) kPa, more than six times too little.

Key idea

Pressure counts molecules. A light gas with a small share of the mass can carry most of the pressure.

3Mixture properties

A mixture of ideal gases is itself an ideal gas, with properties averaged from its parts. The weights depend on what the property is measured per.

Derivation

A per-mole quantity averages by mole fraction, a per-kilogram one by mass fraction:

\[ M = \sum_i y_i M_i = \frac{m}{N}, \qquad R = \frac{\bar R}{M}, \qquad c_p = \sum_i x_i\,c_{p,i}. \]
Worked example · the mixture’s M, R and c_p

The 1 kg of N₂ and 1 kg of CO₂ above: \(M = 2/(35.7 + 22.7) = 34.2\) g/mol, so \(R = 8.314/0.0342 = 243\) J/(kg·K). At 300 K, \(c_p\) is 1038 J/(kg·K) for N₂ and 846 for CO₂, and with half the mass each

\[ c_p = 0.5 \times 1038 + 0.5 \times 846 = 942\ \text{J/(kg·K)}. \]
Common mistake

Averaging the molar masses by mass fraction. That gives 36.0 g/mol here, not 34.2, because it overcounts the heavy CO₂.

Part II

Reacting and moist air

4Combustion and flame temperature

Burning a fuel rearranges atoms; the energy released is the difference in enthalpies of formation between reactants and products.

Derivation

Air is taken as \(\mathrm{O_2} + 3.76\,\mathrm{N_2}\). Stoichiometric methane takes two \(\mathrm O_2\); with a fraction \(\lambda\) of that air,

\[ \mathrm{CH_4} + 2\lambda(\mathrm{O_2} + 3.76\,\mathrm{N_2}) \to \mathrm{CO_2} + 2\,\mathrm{H_2O} + 2(\lambda - 1)\mathrm{O_2} + 7.52\lambda\,\mathrm{N_2}. \]

With no heat lost, the products' enthalpy, each \(\bar h\) counting its enthalpy of formation, equals the reactants': \(\sum_\text{P} N_i\bar h_i(T_\text{ad}) = \sum_\text{R} N_i\bar h_i(298\ \text{K})\). That fixes the adiabatic flame temperature \(T_\text{ad}\).

Worked example · stoichiometric methane

One mole of CH₄ with 2 mol O₂ and 7.52 mol N₂ releases 803 kJ, and it must heat 10.52 mol of products. The balance gives \(T_\text{ad} = 2326\) K. The air–fuel ratio is \(2 \times 4.76 \times 28.97/16.04 = 17.1\) by mass.

Common mistake

Thinking more air makes a hotter flame. At 150% air the same 803 kJ must heat 14.3 mol of products, and the flame falls to 1789 K.

5Moist air and the cooling coil

Atmospheric air is dry air plus a little water vapour, treated as an ideal-gas mixture. How much vapour it holds sets how it behaves when cooled.

Derivation

With vapour partial pressure \(p_v\) in total pressure \(p\), the humidity ratio, mass of vapour per mass of dry air, is \(\omega = 0.622\,p_v/(p - p_v)\), and the relative humidity is \(\phi = p_v/p_g(T)\). Per kilogram of dry air,

\[ h = 1.005\,T + \omega\,(2501 + 1.82\,T)\ \text{kJ/kg}, \quad T \text{ in °C}. \]

Cooled at constant \(\omega\), air reaches saturation at its dew point, where \(p_g(T_d) = p_v\). Cooled further, vapour condenses.

Worked example · a summer day

Air at 30 °C and 60%: \(p_g = 4.25\) kPa, \(p_v = 2.55\) kPa, \(\omega = 16.0\) g/kg, dew point 21.4 °C. Cooled to 12 °C, it leaves saturated with \(\omega = 8.7\) g/kg: 7.3 g of water drips out per kilogram of air, and the coil removes 36.7 kJ/kg.

Common mistake

Thinking an air conditioner only cools. Once the air passes its dew point it also dries: about half the heat the coil removes above goes into condensing water.
