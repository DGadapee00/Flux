Part I

Switching

1The time constant

A circuit with one capacitor or one inductor and DC sources settles exponentially after every switching. Only three numbers are needed: where it starts, where it is heading, and how fast.

Derivation

Replace everything the capacitor sees with its Thévenin equivalent. Then \(C\,dv/dt = (V_\text{th} - v)/R_\text{th}\), whose solution is

\[ v(t) = v(\infty) + \big[v(0^+) - v(\infty)\big]\,e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad \tau = R_\text{th}C, \]

with \(v(\infty) = V_\text{th}\). For an inductor the same holds for its current, with \(\tau = L/R_\text{th}\). \(R_\text{th}\) is the resistance across the element's terminals with the sources switched off, so it changes whenever a switch changes the circuit.

Worked example · switched in and out

12 V with \(R_1 = 1\) kΩ feeds node A through a switch; \(R_2 = 2\) kΩ runs from A to ground, and a 10 µF capacitor hangs from A behind \(R_3 = 200\) Ω. Closed, the capacitor sees \(200 + 1000 \parallel 2000 = 867\) Ω, so \(\tau = 8.67\) ms, heading for \(12 \times 2/3 = 8\) V. After 40 ms it has reached 7.92 V. Then the switch opens: the capacitor now sees \(200 + 2000\) Ω, and discharges with \(\tau = 22\) ms.

Common mistake

Taking \(\tau = R_1 C\), with the source's resistor alone. That gives 10 ms here, and it is wrong both ways: closed, \(R_2\) and \(R_3\) also shape what the capacitor sees; open, \(R_1\) is not connected at all.

2What carries over a switch

A capacitor's voltage cannot jump, since that would take an infinite current. An inductor's current cannot jump, since that would take an infinite voltage. These fix the starting value \(x(0^+)\) after every switching.

Derivation

\(i_C = C\,dv_C/dt\) and \(v_L = L\,di_L/dt\). A finite current keeps \(v_C\) continuous, and a finite voltage keeps \(i_L\) continuous. The other quantity can jump: the capacitor's current, and the inductor's voltage, change at once to whatever the new circuit demands. In steady DC, a capacitor acts as a break and an inductor as a wire, which gives \(x(\infty)\).

Worked example · opening on an inductor

Swap the capacitor for a 10 H inductor. Closed, it heads for \(8/867 = 9.23\) mA and reaches 8.94 mA by 40 ms. When the switch opens, that current must keep flowing, and its only path is \(R_2 + R_3 = 2.2\) kΩ: the inductor's voltage jumps to \(-8.94 \times 2.2 = -19.7\) V, more than the 12 V supply, and the current decays with \(\tau = 4.55\) ms.

Key idea

Find \(x(0^+)\) from what cannot jump, \(x(\infty)\) from the DC circuit, and \(\tau\) from the Thévenin resistance the element sees in the new switch position.

3Second-order circuits

With an inductor and a capacitor together, energy swings between them. A resistor drains it; how fast decides whether the response rings.

Derivation

Kirchhoff's laws give a second-order equation with natural frequency \(\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}\) and decay rate \(\alpha\):

\[ \ddot y + 2\alpha\dot y + \omega_0^2 y = \omega_0^2 y_\infty, \qquad \alpha_\text{series} = \frac{R}{2L}, \quad \alpha_\text{parallel} = \frac{1}{2RC}. \]

With \(\zeta = \alpha/\omega_0\): below 1 the response rings at \(\omega_d = \omega_0\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\) and overshoots by \(e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\); at 1 it is critically damped; above 1 it creeps.

Worked example · L = 10 mH, C = 1 µF

\(\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{10^{-8}} = 10\,000\) rad/s. In series, \(\zeta = (R/2)\sqrt{C/L} = R/200\): 20 Ω gives \(\zeta = 0.1\) and a 73% overshoot; 200 Ω is critical. In parallel, \(\zeta = (1/2R)\sqrt{L/C} = 50/R\): 50 Ω is critical, and 2 kΩ gives \(\zeta = 0.025\).

Common mistake

Assuming more resistance always means more damping. In a parallel RLC a larger resistor draws less current from the tank, so it damps less: \(\zeta\) goes as \(1/R\).
