Part I

Phasors

1Phasors and impedance

Once a sine source has run for a while, every voltage and current in a linear circuit is a sine at the same frequency. Each is fixed by two numbers, an amplitude and a phase, which one complex number holds: its phasor.

Derivation

Write \(v(t) = \operatorname{Re}\big(\mathbf V e^{j\omega t}\big)\). A time derivative then becomes multiplication by \(j\omega\), so \(v_L = L\,di/dt\) becomes \(\mathbf V_L = j\omega L\,\mathbf I\), and \(i_C = C\,dv/dt\) becomes \(\mathbf V_C = \mathbf I/(j\omega C)\). Each part acts as a resistor with a complex value, its impedance:

\[ Z_R = R, \qquad Z_L = j\omega L, \qquad Z_C = \frac{1}{j\omega C}. \]

Ohm's law, series and parallel rules and node analysis all carry over. Kirchhoff's voltage law holds for the phasors: \(\mathbf V_R + \mathbf V_L + \mathbf V_C = \mathbf V\).

Worked example · a 3-4-5 RC circuit

5 V at 1 kHz across 1 kΩ and a capacitor with \(X_C = 1/(\omega C) = 1333\) Ω. Then

\[ |Z| = \sqrt{1000^2 + 1333^2} = 1667\ \Omega, \qquad I = \frac{5}{1667} = 3\ \text{mA}, \]

so \(|V_R| = 3\) V and \(|V_C| = 4\) V. The current leads the source by \(\arctan(1333/1000) = 53.1^\circ\).

Common mistake

Adding amplitudes round a loop. 3 V and 4 V make 5 V here because the two voltages peak a quarter cycle apart. Add the phasors, head to tail.

2Resonance

\(Z_L\) points up the imaginary axis and \(Z_C\) down it. At one frequency they are equal and cancel.

Derivation

In series, \(Z = R + j(\omega L - 1/\omega C)\). It is smallest, and purely resistive, at

\[ \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}, \qquad f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}. \]

There the current is \(V/R\), and each of \(\mathbf V_L\) and \(\mathbf V_C\) has amplitude \(Q V\), with \(Q = \omega_0 L/R\). They cancel each other, so the source sees only \(R\).

Worked example · a series RLC at resonance

\(R = 10\) Ω, \(L = 10\) mH, \(C = 2.533\) µF: \(f_0 = 1.000\) kHz and \(\omega_0 L = 62.8\) Ω. A 5 V source drives \(I = 5/10 = 500\) mA, and

\[ |V_L| = |V_C| = 0.5 \times 62.8 = 31.4\ \text{V}, \]

six times the source, in opposite phase.

Key idea

A series resonant circuit can put many times the source voltage across its parts. Rate the capacitor for \(QV\), not \(V\).

Part II

Frequency response

3Filters

A circuit's frequency response is how it treats a sine at each frequency: how much it passes and how much it delays. A signal that is not a sine is a sum of sines, and each is treated on its own.

Derivation

For R in series with C, the output across C is a voltage divider of impedances:

\[ H(j\omega) = \frac{1/(j\omega C)}{R + 1/(j\omega C)} = \frac{1}{1 + j\omega RC}, \qquad |H| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (\omega RC)^2}}, \qquad \angle H = -\arctan(\omega RC). \]

At the corner, \(f_c = 1/(2\pi RC)\), \(|H| = 1/\sqrt 2\). Well above it \(|H|\) falls as \(1/f\). A ±1 V square wave at \(f_0\) is \(\tfrac{4}{\pi}\sum \sin(n\omega_0 t)/n\) over odd \(n\), so its \(n\)-th harmonic comes out cut by \(|H(jn\omega_0)|\) and delayed by \(-\angle H/(n\omega_0)\) in time.

Worked example · a corner at 1 kHz

A 1 kHz square wave through a filter with its corner at 1 kHz. The fundamental passes 0.707 and is delayed 125 µs. The 3rd harmonic passes 0.316 and is delayed only 66.3 µs. In the input the 3rd harmonic is a third of the fundamental; in the output it is 0.149 of it, and out of step. The output is a new shape: rounded, with sloping sides.

Worked example · a corner at 200 Hz

Move the corner to 200 Hz. In each half period the capacitor charges only a little before the input flips: the output ramps up and down between ±0.304 V, nearly a triangle. Its 3rd harmonic is 0.113 of its fundamental, close to the triangle's 1/9.

Common mistake

Picturing a filter as a volume knob that makes the signal smaller. It cuts and delays each frequency differently, so it changes the shape. Only a pure sine keeps its shape through it.

4AC power and power factor

With a sine voltage across it, a load's power swings every half cycle. When its current lags or leads the voltage, part of each cycle sends energy back to the source.

Derivation

With \(v = \sqrt2 V\cos\omega t\) and \(i = \sqrt2 I\cos(\omega t - \theta)\), the product averages over a cycle to

\[ P = VI\cos\theta, \qquad Q = VI\sin\theta, \qquad S = VI = \sqrt{P^2 + Q^2}, \]

in rms values. \(P\) does the work; \(Q\), the reactive power, flows into the load's inductance and back each cycle. \(\cos\theta\), the power factor, is the share of \(VI\) that does work. A capacitor across the load draws \(Q_C = V^2\omega C\) the other way, so the line need only carry \(\sqrt{P^2 + (Q - Q_C)^2}/V\).

Worked example · a motor at 0.6

A 5 kW motor on 240 V at power factor 0.6 lagging: \(\theta = 53.1^\circ\), \(Q = 5 \tan 53.1^\circ = 6.67\) kvar, \(S = 8.33\) kVA, so the line carries \(8333/240 = 34.7\) A. Through 0.2 Ω of wire, that loses \(34.7^2 \times 0.2 = 241\) W.

Worked example · correcting it

A capacitor of \(Q/(V^2\omega) = 6667/(240^2 \times 377) = 307\) µF across the motor supplies its reactive power locally. The line now carries only \(5000/240 = 20.8\) A, and the wire loss falls to 86.8 W.

Common mistake

Taking volts times amps as the power used. At power factor 0.6, \(VI\) is 8.33 kVA while the work is 5 kW; the extra current does no work but still heats the wires.
