Part I

Superposition

1Superposition

A circuit of resistors and sources is linear. Its node equations are \(G\mathbf v = \mathbf i_s\), and the sources appear only on the right. So the answer to several sources is the sum of the answers to each one alone.

Derivation

Split the source vector by source: \(\mathbf i_s = \mathbf i_{s,1} + \mathbf i_{s,2}\). Then

\[ \mathbf v = G^{-1}\mathbf i_s = G^{-1}\mathbf i_{s,1} + G^{-1}\mathbf i_{s,2}. \]

Each term is the circuit with one source on and the other at zero. A voltage source at zero holds no voltage: it becomes a wire. A current source at zero passes no current: it becomes a break. Its series resistor, and every dependent source, stays.

Worked example · two sources

12 V with 2 Ω feeds node 1, 4 Ω runs from node 1 to ground, 2 Ω joins node 1 to node 2, the 4 Ω load hangs from node 2, and 6 V with 4 Ω feeds node 2. With 6 V a wire, node 2 sees 4 Ω ∥ 4 Ω = 2 Ω; with the 2 Ω that makes 4 Ω, which in parallel with the 4 Ω shunt is 2 Ω. The 12 V source sees 4 Ω in all and drives 3 A. Half goes towards node 2 and half of that through the load: \(I_{L1} = 0.75\) A. By the same steps with 12 V a wire, the 6 V source gives \(I_{L2} = 0.469\) A. Both on:

\[ I_L = 0.75 + 0.469 = 1.219\ \text{A}. \]
Common mistake

Removing the switched-off voltage source and its resistor, leaving a gap. Zero volts is a wire: the source's resistor still runs to ground.

2Power does not superpose

Power goes as the square of current, and the square of a sum is not the sum of the squares.

Derivation \[ P_L = R_L\,(I_{L1} + I_{L2})^2 = P_1 + P_2 + 2R_L\,I_{L1}I_{L2}. \]

The cross term is positive when the two parts push the same way and negative when they oppose. Add the currents first, then find the power.

Worked example · powers that do not add

In the circuit above, 12 V alone puts \(0.75^2 \times 4 = 2.25\) W into the load and 6 V alone \(0.469^2 \times 4 = 0.879\) W. Their sum is 3.13 W. With both on the load takes

\[ 1.219^2 \times 4 = 5.94\ \text{W}, \]

nearly twice as much.

Worked example · currents that cancel

Reverse the right-hand source and raise it to 9.6 V. Alone it gives \(-0.75\) A through the load, 2.25 W, the same power as the 12 V source alone. The powers alone sum to 4.5 W, but together the currents cancel and the load takes nothing.

Key idea

Voltages and currents superpose. Power, a product of the two, does not.
