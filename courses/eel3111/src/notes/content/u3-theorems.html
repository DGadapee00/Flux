Part I

Superposition

1Superposition

A circuit of resistors and sources is linear. Its node equations are \(G\mathbf v = \mathbf i_s\), and the sources appear only on the right. So the answer to several sources is the sum of the answers to each one alone.

Derivation

Split the source vector by source: \(\mathbf i_s = \mathbf i_{s,1} + \mathbf i_{s,2}\). Then

\[ \mathbf v = G^{-1}\mathbf i_s = G^{-1}\mathbf i_{s,1} + G^{-1}\mathbf i_{s,2}. \]

Each term is the circuit with one source on and the other at zero. A voltage source at zero holds no voltage: it becomes a wire. A current source at zero passes no current: it becomes a break. Its series resistor, and every dependent source, stays.

Worked example · two sources

12 V with 2 Ω feeds node 1, 4 Ω runs from node 1 to ground, 2 Ω joins node 1 to node 2, the 4 Ω load hangs from node 2, and 6 V with 4 Ω feeds node 2. With 6 V a wire, node 2 sees 4 Ω ∥ 4 Ω = 2 Ω; with the 2 Ω that makes 4 Ω, which in parallel with the 4 Ω shunt is 2 Ω. The 12 V source sees 4 Ω in all and drives 3 A. Half goes towards node 2 and half of that through the load: \(I_{L1} = 0.75\) A. By the same steps with 12 V a wire, the 6 V source gives \(I_{L2} = 0.469\) A. Both on:

\[ I_L = 0.75 + 0.469 = 1.219\ \text{A}. \]
Common mistake

Removing the switched-off voltage source and its resistor, leaving a gap. Zero volts is a wire: the source's resistor still runs to ground.

2Power does not superpose

Power goes as the square of current, and the square of a sum is not the sum of the squares.

Derivation \[ P_L = R_L\,(I_{L1} + I_{L2})^2 = P_1 + P_2 + 2R_L\,I_{L1}I_{L2}. \]

The cross term is positive when the two parts push the same way and negative when they oppose. Add the currents first, then find the power.

Worked example · powers that do not add

In the circuit above, 12 V alone puts \(0.75^2 \times 4 = 2.25\) W into the load and 6 V alone \(0.469^2 \times 4 = 0.879\) W. Their sum is 3.13 W. With both on the load takes

\[ 1.219^2 \times 4 = 5.94\ \text{W}, \]

nearly twice as much.

Worked example · currents that cancel

Reverse the right-hand source and raise it to 9.6 V. Alone it gives \(-0.75\) A through the load, 2.25 W, the same power as the 12 V source alone. The powers alone sum to 4.5 W, but together the currents cancel and the load takes nothing.

Key idea

Voltages and currents superpose. Power, a product of the two, does not.

Part II

Equivalent circuits

3Thévenin and Norton equivalents

Take the load out of a linear network and look into the two terminals it left. Whatever is inside, the terminal voltage falls on a straight line as the load draws more current.

Derivation

By superposition, the terminal voltage is the sum of a part from the sources inside, with the load drawing nothing, and a part from the load's current \(I\) alone, with the sources switched off. The first is the open-circuit voltage \(V_\text{th}\). The second is \(-I R_\text{th}\), where \(R_\text{th}\) is the resistance seen with every independent source switched off. So

\[ V = V_\text{th} - I R_\text{th}. \]

That is one source in series with one resistor, Thévenin's form. Shorting the terminals gives the other intercept, \(I_N = V_\text{th}/R_\text{th}\): one current source beside the same resistor, Norton's form.

Worked example · the network from its load

The two-source network of section 1, with the load taken out. Switch both sources off and the terminals see 4 Ω in parallel with 2 Ω plus 2 Ω ∥ 4 Ω:

\[ R_\text{th} = 4 \parallel 3.33 = 1.82\ \Omega. \]

Short the terminals: the 6 V source sends 1.5 A through its 4 Ω, and the 12 V source sends 2.4 A through the 2 Ω, so \(I_N = 3.9\) A and \(V_\text{th} = 3.9 \times 1.82 = 7.09\) V. On the bench, a 10 Ω load reads 6.0 V and a 1 Ω load 2.52 V: the line through those two points gives the same pair.

Common mistake

Switching off a dependent source to find \(R_\text{th}\). It stays in; with one inside, push a test current into the terminals and read the voltage.

4Maximum power is not efficiency

A load \(R_L\) on the equivalent draws \(I = V_\text{th}/(R_\text{th} + R_L)\). Its power \(I^2 R_L\) is zero for a short and for an open, so it peaks between.

Derivation \[ P_L = \frac{V_\text{th}^2 R_L}{(R_\text{th} + R_L)^2}, \qquad \frac{dP_L}{dR_L} = 0 \ \text{ at } \ R_L = R_\text{th}, \qquad P_\text{max} = \frac{V_\text{th}^2}{4R_\text{th}}. \]

At the peak the same current runs through \(R_\text{th}\), which takes as much power as the load. The equivalent's efficiency is \(\eta = R_L/(R_L + R_\text{th})\): 50% at the match, rising towards 100% as the load grows.

Worked example · a matched load

The network above with \(R_L = 1.82\) Ω takes \(7.09^2/(4 \times 1.82) = 6.91\) W, and 6.91 W heats \(R_\text{th}\): 50%. The real sources put out 38.4 W, because the network wastes power inside, in the 4 Ω shunt above all. A 20 Ω load takes only 2.11 W, but at 92% efficiency in the equivalent.

Key idea

Match the load to get the most power from a weak source, such as an antenna. Make the load much larger than \(R_\text{th}\) to waste little, as every power supply does. The equivalent copies the terminals, not the losses inside.
