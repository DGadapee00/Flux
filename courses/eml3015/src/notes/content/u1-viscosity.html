Viscosity and shear

Part I

Shear

1Viscosity: stress from a velocity gradient

A fluid sticks to every surface it touches. Drag a plate at speed \(U\) over a thin layer of thickness \(h\), with a still wall below, and the fluid's speed runs from 0 at the wall to \(U\) at the plate. Each layer slides over the one beneath it.

Sliding layers rub. The stress between them is proportional to how fast the speed changes across the layer, and the constant is the viscosity \(\mu\).

Derivation \[ \tau = \mu\,\frac{du}{dy}, \qquad \text{and with a straight-line profile } \frac{du}{dy} = \frac{U}{h}, \text{ so } \tau = \mu\,\frac{U}{h}. \]

The force on a plate of area \(A\) is \(\tau A\).

Worked example · water under a plate

A plate moves at 0.5 m/s over a 2 mm layer of water, with \(\mu = 1.0016\times10^{-3}\) Pa·s. The stress is \(1.0016\times10^{-3} \times 0.5/0.002 = 0.250\) Pa. On a 0.1 m² plate that is a force of 0.025 N.

Common mistake

Taking viscosity as how heavy a fluid is. Mercury is 13.5 times denser than water, but its viscosity is only 1.5 times bigger, so under the same plate the stress is only 1.5 times bigger: 0.38 Pa.

Key idea

The stress depends on the gradient \(U/h\), not on the speed alone. Halve the gap and the stress doubles.

2Momentum spreads in from the wall

Start the plate from rest. At first only the fluid touching it moves. The moving layer drags the next, and motion spreads into the gap. This is diffusion of momentum, and it obeys the same equation as heat spreading through a rod.

Derivation \[ \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \nu\,\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2}, \qquad \nu = \frac{\mu}{\rho}. \]

The kinematic viscosity \(\nu\) sets how fast motion spreads. Across a gap \(h\) the slowest part of the start-up dies away on a time \(h^2/(\pi^2\nu)\). The profile is within 1% of its straight line after \(t_1 = \dfrac{h^2}{\pi^2\nu}\ln\dfrac{200}{\pi}\).

Worked example · water settles

For water, \(\nu = 1.002\times10^{-6}\) m²/s. Across a 2 mm gap, \(t_1 = \dfrac{(0.002)^2}{\pi^2 \times 1.002\times10^{-6}}\ln\dfrac{200}{\pi} = 1.68\) s.

Common mistake

Expecting a thick fluid to respond slowly. Glycerol is 1,400 times more viscous than water and only 26% denser, so its \(\nu\) is about 1,100 times bigger. The same gap settles in 1.5 ms, where water takes 1.7 s.

What the model leaves out

The fluid is Newtonian and the flow laminar, with no edges or end walls. Honey, paint and blood change viscosity as they are sheared.
