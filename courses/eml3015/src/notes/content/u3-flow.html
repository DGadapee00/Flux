Part I

Particles

1Acceleration following a particle

A flow field gives the velocity at each point and time, \(\mathbf v(x, y, z, t)\). Newton's law is about a particle, which moves through that field. Its velocity changes for two reasons: the field changes in time where the particle is, and the particle moves to a place where the field is different.

Derivation

Along the particle's path \(x(t)\), the chain rule gives, in one dimension,

\[ \frac{Du}{Dt} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + u\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}, \]

and in three, \(D\mathbf v/Dt = \partial\mathbf v/\partial t + (\mathbf v\cdot\nabla)\mathbf v\). The first term is the local part, the second the convective part.

Worked example · a particle leaving the nozzle

Water enters a nozzle 0.5 m long at a steady 1 m/s, and the area falls in a straight line to a quarter. Then \(u = 1/(1 - 0.75\,x/0.5)\). At the exit \(u = 4\) m/s and \(\partial u/\partial x = u \times 0.75/(0.5 \times 0.25) = 24\) s⁻¹, so the particle accelerates at \(4 \times 24 = 96\) m/s², nearly ten g.

Common mistake

Taking steady flow to mean no acceleration. Steady means nothing changes at a fixed point. Every particle in a steady nozzle still speeds up, because it moves into the narrow end.

Key idea

Double the speed and the convective acceleration goes up four times: both \(u\) and \(\partial u/\partial x\) double.

2Time along a path

To follow a particle, integrate its speed along the path. Where the flow is fast the particle covers ground quickly; dots placed at equal times spread out.

Derivation

From \(dt = dx/u\) with \(u = u_0/(1 - c\,x/L)\),

\[ T = \int_0^L \frac{1 - c\,x/L}{u_0}\,dx = \frac{L}{u_0}\left(1 - \frac{c}{2}\right). \]

If the inflow ramps up as \(u_0(1 + rt)\), the path separates the same way: \(x - c\,x^2/(2L) = u_0(t + r t^2/2)\).

Worked example · crossing the nozzle

For the same nozzle, \(c = 0.75\), so \(T = (0.5/1)(1 - 0.375) = 0.313\) s.

Common mistake

Averaging the inlet and exit speeds. The particle spends most of its time near the slow inlet, so the time is longer than \(L\) over the average speed.

Part II

Pressure

3Euler's equation across a bend

With friction left out, the only forces on a particle are pressure and weight. Newton's law with the material derivative is Euler's equation. Across curved streamlines it says how much the pressure must rise to turn the flow.

Derivation \[ \rho\,\frac{D\mathbf v}{Dt} = -\nabla p + \rho\,\mathbf g. \]

A particle on a streamline of radius \(r\) accelerates toward the centre at \(u^2/r\). In the horizontal plane, the normal part of Euler's equation is \(\partial p/\partial r = \rho u^2/r\). For a free vortex, \(u = K/r\), with \(K\) set by the flow rate \(Vw = K\ln(r_2/r_1)\):

\[ p_2 - p_1 = \frac{\rho K^2}{2}\left(\frac{1}{r_1^2} - \frac{1}{r_2^2}\right). \]
Worked example · a water elbow

Water at a mean 2 m/s turns through a bend 0.2 m wide with centre-line radius 0.3 m, so \(r_1 = 0.2\) and \(r_2 = 0.4\) m. Then \(K = 0.4/\ln 2 = 0.577\) m²/s, the inner wall moves at \(K/r_1 = 2.89\) m/s, and the outer wall carries \(\tfrac{998}{2}(0.577)^2(25 - 6.25) = 3.12\) kPa more than the inner.

Common mistake

Taking the pressure as even across a stream. That holds only where the streamlines are straight. A flow that turns needs a pressure that rises toward the outside of the turn.

What the model leaves out

Friction. In a real pipe bend the slow fluid near the walls cannot resist the same pressure difference, so it drifts inward and sets up a pair of swirls downstream of the bend.

4Mass in, mass out

Mass is neither made nor lost. For any fixed region of a flow, the mass flowing out through its sides each second must equal the rate the mass inside falls.

Derivation

For a region of area \(A\) and boundary \(s\), and then by the divergence theorem for a vanishing region,

\[ \oint \rho\,\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf n\,ds = -\frac{d}{dt}\iint \rho\,dA \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf v) = 0. \]

Where the density cannot change, as in water at ordinary speeds, this says \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf v = 0\): what flows in flows out.

Worked example · air squeezed by a piston

Air at 1.2 kg/m³ moves as \(u = -2x\) s⁻¹ toward a closed end. A square 0.3 m on a side takes in 0.468 kg/s per metre of depth and lets out 0.252: a net 0.216 kg/s stays. The density inside rises at \(0.216/0.09 = 2.4\) kg/m³ each second.

Common mistake

Reading a speeding-up flow as one that gains or loses mass. Water entering a corner speeds up in one direction and slows in the other; in and out still balance.

5Pressure-driven flow in a pipe

Push a liquid down a long straight pipe and, far from the inlet, every particle moves straight along at its own steady speed. Nothing accelerates, so Navier–Stokes keeps only two terms: the pressure gradient and viscous shear.

Derivation

With \(\mathbf v = u(r)\,\hat{\mathbf x}\), continuity holds and \((\mathbf v\cdot\nabla)\mathbf v = 0\). The axial part of Navier–Stokes, with \(G = \Delta p/L\), is

\[ 0 = G + \frac{\mu}{r}\frac{d}{dr}\Big(r\frac{du}{dr}\Big). \]

Integrate twice. Keep \(u\) finite on the axis and set \(u(R) = 0\) at the wall (no slip):

\[ u = \frac{G}{4\mu}\,(R^2 - r^2), \qquad Q = \int_0^R u\,2\pi r\,dr = \frac{\pi R^4 G}{8\mu}, \qquad \tau_\text{wall} = \frac{GR}{2}. \]

The profile is a parabola. Its peak grows as \(R^2\), and the area it fills grows as \(R^2\), so the flow grows as \(R^4\). Between two plates a gap \(h\) apart the same steps give \(q = Gh^3/(12\mu)\) per unit width.

Worked example · olive oil in a 4 mm tube

Olive oil (\(\mu = 0.0562\) Pa·s, \(\rho = 920\) kg/m³) flows down a tube of radius 2 mm under 10 kPa per metre:

\[ Q = \frac{\pi(0.002)^4(10^4)}{8(0.0562)} = 1.12\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^3/\text{s} = 1.12\ \text{mL/s}. \]

The mean speed is \(Q/\pi R^2 = 0.089\) m/s, so \(Re = \rho V D/\mu = 5.8\): well inside laminar flow. A tube of twice the radius carries 16 times as much, 17.9 mL/s.

Common mistake

Using the formula without checking the Reynolds number. Water under 1 kPa per metre in a 1 cm pipe would, by the formula, move at 3.1 m/s, with \(Re \approx 31{,}000\). Above about 2300 the flow is turbulent and carries far less.

6Models and similitude

A law of physics cannot depend on the units it is written in. That one fact says which combinations of the variables can matter, and when a small model behaves like the full-size thing.

Derivation

A ship's drag depends on \(V\), \(L\), \(\rho\), \(\mu\) and \(g\): five variables in three base dimensions, mass, length and time. The dimension matrix has rank 3, so the drag coefficient can depend on only \(5 - 3 = 2\) independent groups:

\[ \mathrm{Re} = \frac{VL}{\nu}, \qquad \mathrm{Fr} = \frac{V}{\sqrt{gL}}. \]

Re compares inertia with viscous force, Fr inertia with gravity. A model at scale \(\lambda = L_m/L_p\) matches Fr when \(V_m = V_p\sqrt\lambda\). Its Reynolds number is then

\[ \frac{\mathrm{Re}_m}{\mathrm{Re}_p} = \lambda^{3/2}\,\frac{\nu_p}{\nu_m}, \]

so matching both needs \(\nu_m = \lambda^{3/2}\nu_p\).

Worked example · a 1/25 ship model

A 100 m ship at 10 m/s, tested with a 4 m model. Matching Fr gives \(V_m = 10\sqrt{1/25} = 2\) m/s. In water the model's Reynolds number is \((1/25)^{3/2} = 1/125\) of the ship's: \(8.0\times10^6\) against \(1.0\times10^9\). Matching both would need \(\nu_m = 1.0\times10^{-6}/125 = 8.0\times10^{-9}\) m²/s, fourteen times thinner than mercury.

Common mistake

Running the model at the ship's own speed. Its waves then match a ship five times faster: the Froude number is \(\sqrt{25}\) times too big.

7Boundary layers

A stream sliding along a plate sticks to it at the surface and keeps its full speed a little way off. Between the two is a thin layer where viscosity matters. Outside it, the flow behaves as if it had none.

Derivation

In the layer, Navier–Stokes simplifies because it is thin: changes across it are far faster than changes along it. Prandtl's boundary-layer equations follow, and on a flat plate they have a solution of one shape, stretched as the layer grows. With \(u/U = f'(\eta)\) and \(\eta = y\sqrt{U/\nu x}\),

\[ f''' + \tfrac12 f f'' = 0, \qquad f(0) = f'(0) = 0, \quad f'(\infty) = 1. \]

Solved numerically, it gives \(f'' = 0.332\) at the wall and \(f' = 0.99\) at \(\eta = 4.91\). So

\[ \delta = \frac{4.91\,x}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Re}_x}}, \qquad \tau_w = \frac{0.332\,\rho U^2}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Re}_x}}, \qquad \mathrm{Re}_x = \frac{Ux}{\nu}. \]

The layer grows as \(\sqrt x\) and thins as \(1/\sqrt U\). It stays laminar to about \(\mathrm{Re}_x = 5\times10^5\) on a smooth plate.

Worked example · air along a 1 m plate

Air at 5 m/s, \(\nu = 1.54\times10^{-5}\) m²/s. At the trailing edge \(\mathrm{Re}_L = 5(1)/1.54\times10^{-5} = 3.25\times10^5\), still laminar, and

\[ \delta = \frac{4.91(1)}{\sqrt{3.25\times10^5}} = 8.6\ \text{mm}. \]

Halfway along it is \(8.6/\sqrt2 = 6.1\) mm. At 10 m/s the layer turns turbulent at \(x = 5\times10^5\nu/U = 0.77\) m.

Common mistake

Scaling the layer with distance or speed directly. It goes as \(\sqrt{x}\) and \(1/\sqrt{U}\): twice the speed thins it by \(\sqrt2\), not by half.

8A system passing through a box

Newton's laws are written for a fixed lump of matter, a system. In a pump or a nozzle the matter keeps moving, so it is easier to watch a fixed region, a control volume. The Reynolds transport theorem turns a law for the system into one for the box.

Derivation

Take the system to be the water in the box at time \(t\). A moment \(dt\) later it has moved on: it has left a sliver at the inlet face and filled a sliver past the outlet face. So its property \(B\) is what the box holds then, minus the inlet sliver, plus the outlet sliver. For one-dimensional flow with \(b\) per kilogram, each sliver holds \(\rho b\,uA\,dt\), and

\[ \frac{dB_\text{sys}}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\int_\text{box}\rho b\,dV + \big(\rho b\,uA\big)_\text{out} - \big(\rho b\,uA\big)_\text{in}. \]

With \(b = 1\) it is the mass balance. With \(b = u\) the left side is the net force on the water, by Newton's second law. In three dimensions the flux term becomes \(\int_\text{CS}\rho b\,(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf n)\,dA\).

Worked example · a steady nozzle

Water at 10 L/s flows steadily through a duct whose area falls from 0.02 m² to 0.005 m² over 2 m. A box holds the duct from 0.75 m to 1.25 m, where the areas are 0.014375 m² and 0.010625 m². The speeds are \(u = Q/A = 0.696\) m/s in and 0.941 m/s out. Nothing in the box changes, so with \(b = u\)

\[ F = \rho Q\,(u_\text{out} - u_\text{in}) = 1000(0.01)(0.941 - 0.696) = 2.46\ \text{N}. \]

The walls and the pressure push the water in the box forward with 2.46 N, though the flow is steady.

Common mistake

Treating the box as the system. In steady flow nothing in the box changes, yet the water passing through it speeds up and needs a force. And when dye drifts into the box, the box gains dye while the water, which carries its dye with it, gains none.

9Momentum on a control volume

With momentum for the property in the transport theorem, the force on whatever is inside a control volume is the momentum flux out minus the momentum flux in. A jet turned by a vane is the plainest case.

Derivation

Ride with a vane moving away at \(U\). The flow is steady, the water arrives at \(w = V - U\) and, with no friction and the same pressure all round, leaves at \(w\) turned through \(\theta\). Each second \(\dot m = \rho A w\) passes, so the force on the water is

\[ \mathbf F_\text{water} = \dot m\,(\mathbf w_\text{out} - \mathbf w_\text{in}) = \rho A w^2\,(\cos\theta - 1,\ \sin\theta), \]

and the vane feels the opposite: \(F_x = \rho A w^2(1 - \cos\theta)\) along the jet. The relative speed enters twice, so half of it is a quarter of the force.

Worked example · a jet on a turning vane

A 2 cm jet at 20 m/s: \(A = 3.14\times10^{-4}\) m² and \(\dot m = 998(3.14\times10^{-4})(20) = 6.27\) kg/s. A fixed vane turning it 180° feels

\[ F_x = 6.27(20)(1 - \cos 180^\circ) = 250.8\ \text{N}, \]

twice what a 90° vane feels. Running away at 10 m/s, it feels a quarter: 62.7 N.

Common mistake

Using the jet's own speed for a moving vane. The water meets the vane at \(V - U\), and only that much arrives each second: the force goes as \((V - U)^2\).

10Head loss in pipes

Friction at the wall turns some of a pipe flow's pressure into heat. As a height of fluid, that is the head loss, and it decides the pump a system needs.

Derivation

Dimensional analysis says the pressure drop over a length \(L\) depends on \(L/D\), \(\mathrm{Re}\) and the wall's relative roughness \(\varepsilon/D\). Darcy–Weisbach puts it as

\[ h_L = f\,\frac{L}{D}\,\frac{V^2}{2g}. \]

In laminar flow the Poiseuille profile gives \(f = 64/\mathrm{Re}\) exactly, so \(h_L \propto V\) and roughness does not enter. In turbulent flow Colebrook's equation, \(1/\sqrt f = -2\log_{10}\big(\varepsilon/3.7D + 2.51/(\mathrm{Re}\sqrt f)\big)\), gives an \(f\) that changes slowly with \(\mathrm{Re}\) and levels off in a rough pipe, so \(h_L\) grows nearly as \(V^2\). The Moody chart plots it.

Worked example · water in 5 cm steel

2 L/s of water (\(\nu = 1.0\times10^{-6}\) m²/s) through 10 m of 5 cm commercial steel, \(\varepsilon = 0.045\) mm. \(V = 1.02\) m/s and \(\mathrm{Re} = 50{,}930\): turbulent. Colebrook gives \(f = 0.0237\), so

\[ h_L = 0.0237\times\frac{10}{0.05}\times\frac{1.02^2}{2(9.81)} = 0.2505\ \text{m}. \]

At twice the flow the loss is 3.68 times as much; in a 1 mm-rough pipe, 3.97 times.

Common mistake

Using the Fanning friction factor in Darcy's equation. Fanning's is a quarter of Darcy's: laminar Fanning is \(16/\mathrm{Re}\). Check which a chart or formula gives.

11Pipe networks and pumps

A real system strings pipes together: in series, in parallel, branching to several outlets, with a pump to drive it all. Two rules and two curves settle it.

Derivation

Pipes in series carry one flow and add their losses. Pipes in parallel run between the same two points, so they lose the same head, and their flows add:

\[ h_{L,A}(Q_A) = h_{L,B}(Q_B), \qquad Q_A + Q_B = Q. \]

Each \(h_L = f(L/D)V^2/2g\) grows roughly as \(Q^2 L/D^5\), so a short or wide pipe takes more than its share of area suggests. The system needs a head that rises with flow, the lift plus every loss; a pump gives a head that falls with flow. They run where the two curves cross.

Worked example · two pipes to one shower

A pump lifts water through a 20 mm trunk to a junction, then through a 15 mm pipe 10 m long (A) and a 10 mm bypass 2 m long (B) in parallel to a shower 6 m up. Both lose the same 4.56 m, which splits the flow 0.429 L/s through A and 0.355 L/s through B: 55% through A, not the 69% its area would give. The pump runs at 0.784 L/s and 32.7 m.

Worked example · the tap opens

Open a downstairs tap at the junction halfway. The pump slides down its curve to 1.08 L/s, and the trunk's loss climbs to 11.9 m. The junction's head falls to 9.85 m, and the shower, which must still climb 6 m, gets only 0.327 L/s.

Common mistake

Splitting parallel flows evenly or by area. The split is whatever makes the losses equal; work it from \(h_L\), and remember every branch shares the trunk upstream.

Part IV

Bodies in a stream

12Drag and the drag crisis

A body in a stream feels a force along the flow, drag, and across it, lift. Both scale with the dynamic pressure \(\tfrac12\rho V^2\) and an area.

Derivation

Dimensional analysis leaves the force divided by \(\tfrac12\rho V^2A\) a function of the Reynolds number alone, for a given shape:

\[ F_D = \tfrac12\,C_D(\text{Re})\,\rho V^2 A, \qquad F_L = \tfrac12\,C_L\,\rho V^2 A. \]

For a sphere \(C_D\) is near 0.4 to 0.5 over \(10^3 < \text{Re} < 2 \times 10^5\). Near \(3 \times 10^5\) the boundary layer turns turbulent, clings longer to the back, and the wake narrows: \(C_D\) drops to about 0.1. Roughness brings that crisis to lower Re.

Worked example · a football at 15 m/s

A 220 mm ball at 15 m/s: \(\text{Re} = 15 \times 0.22/1.5 \times 10^{-5} = 2.2 \times 10^5\), \(C_D = 0.38\), \(A = 0.038\) m². So \(F_D = \tfrac12 \times 0.38 \times 1.2 \times 225 \times 0.038 = 2.0\) N. At 25 m/s, past the crisis, it is 1.5 N.

Common mistake

Assuming drag always rises with speed. Across the drag crisis \(C_D\) falls faster than \(V^2\) rises. Dimples on a golf ball cause the crisis at its own speeds, which is why it flies further than a smooth one.
