Pressure on walls and in moving tanks

Part I

Walls

1The force on a flat gate

In a still liquid the gauge pressure grows with depth, \(p = \rho g h\). On a flat gate each strip feels the pressure at its own depth. Adding the strips gives a force equal to the pressure at the gate's centroid times its area.

Derivation

Measure \(y\) along the gate's plane from the line where that plane meets the surface. A strip at \(y\) is at depth \(y\sin\theta\), so

\[ F = \int \rho g\, y \sin\theta\; dA = \rho g \sin\theta \int y\, dA = \rho g\, y_c \sin\theta\, A = \rho g\, h_c A. \]

The integral \(\int y\,dA\) is \(y_c A\) by the definition of the centroid.

Worked example · a gate 3 m down

A vertical gate 2 m tall and 1 m wide holds back water, \(\rho = 998\) kg/m³, with its top edge 3 m below the surface. Its centroid is at \(h_c = 3 + 1 = 4\) m, so \(F = 998 \times 9.81 \times 4 \times 2 = 78.3\) kN.

Common mistake

Using the depth of the top or bottom edge. The pressure at the centroid is the gate's average pressure only because the pressure grows in a straight line with depth.

Key idea

The force depends on depth, not on how much liquid is behind the gate. A thin slot of water 4 m deep pushes as hard as a lake 4 m deep.

2Where the force acts

The lower part of a gate feels more pressure than the upper part, so the force acts below the centroid. Where exactly is set by taking moments about the surface line.

Derivation \[ F\, y_{cp} = \int y\, p\, dA = \rho g \sin\theta \int y^2\, dA = \rho g \sin\theta\,(I_{xc} + y_c^2 A), \]

and dividing by \(F = \rho g \sin\theta\, y_c A\) gives \(y_{cp} = y_c + I_{xc}/(y_c A)\). For a rectangle \(I_{xc} = bL^3/12\), so the force acts \(L^2/(12 y_c)\) below the centroid, along the gate.

Worked example · where the gate's force acts

For the gate 3 m down, \(y_c = 4\) m and \(L = 2\) m, so \(y_{cp} = 4 + 4/48 = 4.08\) m. The force acts 8 cm below the centroid. Hinged at its top edge, the gate needs \(F (y_{cp} - 3)/2 = 42.4\) kN at its bottom edge to stay shut.

Common mistake

Placing the force two thirds of the way down every time. That holds only when the top edge is at the surface, where the pressure rises from zero in a triangle. Lower the gate and the force moves toward the centroid.

Key idea

Deep down the pressure on a gate is nearly even, and the force acts close to the centroid. Near the surface the load leans on the bottom edge.

Part II

Moving tanks

3A tank that accelerates

Once a liquid has settled and moves with its tank, no layer slides over another, so there is no shear. Pressure alone must push each parcel along with the tank and hold it up against gravity.

Derivation

Newton's second law on a small block of liquid, with only pressure and weight acting, gives

\[ -\nabla p + \rho\,\mathbf g = \rho\,\mathbf a \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \nabla p = \rho\,(\mathbf g - \mathbf a). \]

Isobars lie square to \(\mathbf g - \mathbf a\). The free surface is one of them, so for a forward acceleration \(a_x\) and an upward one \(a_z\) it tilts by \(\tan\theta = a_x/(g + a_z)\).

Worked example · water in a truck

A tank 1 m long holds water 0.45 m deep. The truck speeds up at 3 m/s². The surface tilts by \(\arctan(3/9.81) = 17.0°\) and pivots about the middle, so the back wall rises \((3/9.81)(0.5) = 15.3\) cm. The floor pressure at the back is \(998 \times 9.81 \times 0.603 = 5.90\) kPa.

Common mistake

Expecting the water to pile up at the front, as if thrown forward. The tank pushes the water forward, and only higher pressure at the back can do that. The water climbs the back wall.

Key idea

Speed does nothing. A tank at a steady 25 m/s keeps a level surface; only acceleration tilts it.

4A tank that spins

Spin a round tank until the liquid turns with it. Each parcel now moves in a circle, and its acceleration points to the axis with size \(\omega^2 r\).

Derivation

In the radial direction \(\partial p/\partial r = \rho\,\omega^2 r\), and vertically \(\partial p/\partial z = -\rho g\). Along the surface \(dp = 0\), so

\[ \frac{dz}{dr} = \frac{\omega^2 r}{g} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad z = \frac{\omega^2 r^2}{2g} + C. \]

No liquid is added, so the rest level sits halfway between centre and rim: the centre drops \(\omega^2R^2/(4g)\) and the rim rises the same.

Worked example · water spun at 6 rad/s

A tank of radius 0.3 m holds water 0.45 m deep. At 6 rad/s the surface climbs \(36 \times 0.09/(2 \times 9.81) = 16.5\) cm from centre to rim, so the centre drops 8.26 cm. The floor pressure at the rim is \(998 \times 9.81 \times 0.533 = 5.21\) kPa.

Common mistake

Taking the whole climb \(\omega^2R^2/(2g)\) as the drop at the centre. The same water fills the tank, so the centre falls half the climb and the rim rises the other half.

What the model leaves out

The liquid has settled and turns or moves with the tank. Sloshing on the way there, waves and surface tension at the walls are left out.
