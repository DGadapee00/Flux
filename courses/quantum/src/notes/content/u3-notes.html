1Model and interpretation

Key relation
\[T=\left[1+\frac{V_0^2\sinh^2(\kappa a)}{4E(V_0-E)}\right]^{-1}\]

A finite barrier can transmit a nonzero probability current even when the incident energy is below its height. The state inside the barrier is evanescent. Reflection and transmission follow matching of the wavefunction and its derivative.

Work the model

  1. Check that 0 < E < V₀ for the below-barrier formula used here.
  2. Calculate κ = √[2m(V₀ − E)]/ħ with consistent energy and length units.
  3. Match ψ and ψ′ at both interfaces. The lab also solves those four complex equations independently of the closed-form transmission formula.
  4. For equal potentials and masses on the two exterior sides, reflected and transmitted probability-current fractions sum to one. In a different setup, amplitude squares need velocity factors.
Worked example · reason through the setup

For the classroom choice E = 2 eV, V₀ = 5 eV and width 0.25 nm, the lab computes the exact rectangular-barrier transmission and compares it with direct complex boundary matching. Increase the width by a small amount and observe the strong reduction in transmission. Use the readout rather than a large-barrier approximation when the barrier is thin.

Common mistake

Tunneling does not require the particle to borrow enough energy to cross the barrier.

Common mistake

An exponential-only estimate omits interface factors and can be poor for a thin barrier.

3Limits and further study

What the model leaves out

One-dimensional, time-independent, single-particle scattering with a rectangular barrier and equal exterior potentials. It omits absorption and many-body effects.

Worked reflection · transfer the idea

Keep E/V₀ fixed and increase the barrier width. Explain what happens to transmission and why R + T is a useful check.

Write a prediction, identify the quantity held fixed and test the prediction in the lab. Name one additional physical effect that would require a different model.
