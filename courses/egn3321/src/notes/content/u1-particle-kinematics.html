01Units and significant figures

A force in pounds and a length in feet have to become newtons and metres before they enter an SI equation. One foot is exactly 0.3048 m. One pound-force is one pound-mass times 9.80665 m/s².

Why

The pound-force is defined with standard gravity, 9.80665 m/s². A pound-mass is exactly 0.45359237 kg. Multiply them: one pound-force is 4.4482 N. Multiply a force by that factor. Multiply an arm by 0.3048. The product is the moment, in newton-metres.

Worked example · 50 lbf through 4 ft

A 50 lbf force has a 4 ft arm. The force is 222 N, to three figures. The arm is 1.219 m, four figures, because the first digit is 1. The moment is 271 N·m.

The US customary mass unit is the slug. It is the mass that one pound-force accelerates at 1 ft/s². Newton's second law fixes it: \(1\ \text{slug} = 1\ \text{lbf}\cdot\text{s}^2/\text{ft}\). In SI that is 14.594 kg.

Worked example · a car's mass in slugs

A car weighs 2000 lb. Its mass is \(W/g = 2000/32.174 = 62.2\) slug, using standard gravity in ft/s². In SI, the same car is \(2000 \times 0.45359\) = 907 kg.

Check by converting the other way: \(62.16 \times 14.594 = 907.2\) kg. The two routes agree.

Report three significant figures. When the first significant digit is 1, keep four. Count from the first non-zero digit.

Worked example · three figures

The product \(3 \times 5 \times \pi / 7\) is 6.73198 before rounding. The first digit is 6, so three figures give 6.73.

An equation is dimensionally homogeneous when every added term measures the same kind of quantity. \(v = v_0 + at\) is homogeneous: \(at\) is (m/s²)(s), which is m/s. \(v = v_0 + a\) is not. A velocity is being added to an acceleration. Checking dimensions catches a dropped \(t\) before any number is computed.

Common mistake

The pound-force uses 9.80665 m/s². Projectile motion in this unit uses \(g = 9.81\) m/s². A student who uses one of those values for both jobs changes the third figure. Hibbeler Statics §1.3 and Dynamics §12.6.

Key

Convert first. Then round. Three figures, or four when the leading digit is 1. Check dimensions before numbers.

What the model leaves out

The conversion uses the exact foot and the defined pound-force. It leaves out a local value of \(g\). A scale that reads pounds already used standard gravity. Measurement uncertainty is also left out: three figures is a reporting rule, not a statement about how well a quantity is known.

02Rectilinear motion

On a straight line, velocity is the slope of \(s(t)\). Acceleration is the slope of \(v(t)\). The other direction is an area.

\[ v = \frac{ds}{dt}, \qquad a = \frac{dv}{dt}. \]
Why

If \(a\) is constant, \(dv/dt = a\) integrates to \(v = v_0 + at\). Then \(ds/dt = v_0 + at\) integrates to \(s = s_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac12 at^2\).

When \(a\) is known against \(s\), time is in the way. The chain rule removes it: \(a = dv/dt = (dv/ds)(ds/dt) = v\,dv/ds\). So \(a\,ds = v\,dv\). With constant \(a\), integrate both sides from the start: \(a(s - s_0) = \tfrac12(v^2 - v_0^2)\).

\[ \begin{aligned} v &= v_0 + at,\\ s &= s_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac12 at^2,\\ v^2 &= v_0^2 + 2a(s - s_0). \end{aligned} \]

The third equation is the one to use when time is neither given nor asked for. It follows from the first two by eliminating \(t\), as well as from \(a\,ds = v\,dv\).

Worked example · a reversal

A particle starts at the origin with \(v_0 = 4\) m/s and \(a = -2\) m/s². At \(t = 5\) s the velocity is \(-6\) m/s and the position is \(-5\) m. It stopped at \(t = 2\) s, at \(s = 4\) m. The path out and back is 13 m.

Worked example · stopping from 60 mph

A car at 60 mph brakes at a steady 20 ft/s². First convert: 60 mph is 88 ft/s. The time equation gives \(t = 88/20 = 4.40\) s to stop. The velocity–position equation, with \(v = 0\), gives \(d = v_0^2/(2a) = 88^2/40 = 194\) ft.

Check in SI. 88 ft/s is 26.82 m/s, and 20 ft/s² is 6.096 m/s². The distance is \(26.82^2/12.19 = 59.0\) m, which is 194 ft.

When \(a\) depends on \(s\), separate the variables in \(a\,ds = v\,dv\) and integrate. For \(a = ks\), a particle that passes the origin at \(v_0\) has speed \(\sqrt{v_0^2 + ks^2}\).

Worked example · a pull toward the origin

Let \(a = -4s\), in m/s² with \(s\) in m. The particle passes the origin at 2 m/s. Integrate \(v\,dv = -4s\,ds\): \(\tfrac12 v^2 - 2 = -2s^2\), so \(v^2 = 4 - 4s^2\). The particle stops where \(v = 0\): at \(s = 1\) m. It then returns, so the motion is an oscillation between \(\pm 1\) m.

A resistance that grows with speed gives the other common law, \(a = -kv\). It slows a particle without ever reversing it.

Why a resistance slows linearly with distance

Put \(a = -kv\) into \(a\,ds = v\,dv\): \(-kv\,ds = v\,dv\). The speed cancels from both sides, which is the whole trick. What is left is \(dv = -k\,ds\). Integrate from the origin, where the speed is \(v_0\):

\[ v = v_0 - ks. \]

The speed falls by the same amount for every metre travelled. It reaches zero at \(s = v_0/k\), and the particle stops there for good.

In time the story looks different. From \(dv/dt = -kv\), the speed decays as \(v = v_0 e^{-kt}\). It never quite reaches zero, yet the distance it covers adds up to the finite \(v_0/k\). Both statements are true. The distance law is linear; the time law is exponential.

Worked example · a boat coasting to a stop

A small boat cuts its motor at 6 m/s. Water resistance gives \(a = -0.5v\), with \(k = 0.5\) s⁻¹. After 4 m it is moving at \(v = 6 - 0.5(4) = 4\) m/s. It stops after \(6/0.5 = 12\) m.

Check in time. The speed halves when \(e^{-0.5t} = 0.5\), at \(t = \ln 2/0.5 = 1.39\) s. The distance by then is \(\int_0^t 6e^{-0.5\tau}\,d\tau = 12(1 - 0.5) = 6\) m. The distance law agrees: \(v = 6 - 0.5(6) = 3\) m/s, half the start.

Common mistake

Distance is 13 m. Displacement is \(-5\) m. Reporting the absolute value of the displacement as the distance drops the leg after the stop. Hibbeler §12.2.

Key

Displacement is the net change in \(s\). Distance adds the lengths of the pieces, including the piece after the velocity changes sign. Use \(a\,ds = v\,dv\) when time is not in the question.

In the Rectilinear lab, the plot shows \(a\), \(v\), and \(s\) against time. Move the probe through the stop and watch the distance and the displacement separate.

What the model leaves out

Constant acceleration leaves out drag, which grows with speed, and a brake whose grip fades as it heats. Either makes \(a\) a function of \(v\), which needs \(dt = dv/a(v)\) instead. A curve needs the normal acceleration of section 5.

03Erratic graphs

When the motion comes as a graph rather than a formula, read it with the same two definitions. The slope of an \(s\)–\(t\) graph is \(v\). The slope of a \(v\)–\(t\) graph is \(a\). Areas run the other way.

Why

Integrate \(a = dv/dt\) over an interval: \(\Delta v = \int a\,dt\), the area under the \(a\)–\(t\) graph. Integrate \(v = ds/dt\): \(\Delta s = \int v\,dt\), the area under the \(v\)–\(t\) graph. On a step where \(a\) is constant, \(v\) is linear. Its area is the width times the average of the end velocities. That trapezoid is exact, not an estimate.

An \(a\)–\(t\) graph made of steps has a constant acceleration on each step. The area of a step is that step's change in velocity. Then step the position forward with the average velocity on the interval.

Worked example · three steps

Start from rest. Accelerate at 2 m/s² for 3 s, then at \(-1\) m/s² for 2 s, then at 0 for 1 s. The velocity ends at 4 m/s. The position ends at 23 m.

Why

The first area is \(2 \times 3 = 6\) m/s, so \(v = 6\) m/s and \(s = 9\) m. The next area is \(-2\) m/s, so \(v = 4\) m/s and \(s = 19\) m. The last step holds \(v\) and adds 4 m. End at 23 m.

A graph need not be steps. If \(a\) is linear in time, \(v\) is a parabola and \(s\) a cubic. Integrate term by term.

Worked example · a ramp in acceleration

Let \(a = 1 + 0.5t\) m/s², with \(v_0 = 2\) m/s and \(s_0 = 0\). Then \(v = 2 + t + 0.25t^2\) and \(s = 2t + 0.5t^2 + t^3/12\). At \(t = 2\) s: \(a = 2\) m/s², \(v = 5\) m/s, and \(s = 4 + 2 + 0.667 = 6.67\) m.

A \(v\)–\(s\) graph gives acceleration through \(a = v\,dv/ds\): the height times the slope. On \(v = 2s + 1\), at \(s = 1\) m, \(v = 3\) m/s and \(dv/ds = 2\) s⁻¹. So \(a = 6\) m/s².

Common mistake

The slope of a \(v\)–\(s\) graph is not the acceleration. On \(v = 2s + 1\) the slope is 2 everywhere, but the acceleration is \(v\) times it, which grows with \(v\). It is 6 m/s² at \(s = 1\) m and 10 m/s² at \(s = 2\) m. Hibbeler §12.3.

Key

Slopes give the next derivative down the chain. Areas give the change one step up. On a \(v\)–\(s\) graph, multiply the slope by the height.

A graph also answers questions without a number. Read its shape first.

  • A straight \(v\)–\(t\) line has a constant slope. The acceleration is constant, and the \(s\)–\(t\) graph is a parabola.
  • Where the \(v\)–\(t\) graph crosses zero, the particle stops and turns. The \(s\)–\(t\) graph is flat there: a peak or a trough.
  • The \(s\)–\(t\) graph bends upward where \(a > 0\) and downward where \(a < 0\). Its bend says nothing about which way the particle moves.
  • A jump in the \(a\)–\(t\) graph is a corner in the \(v\)–\(t\) graph. The \(s\)–\(t\) graph stays smooth.
Worked example · a ball thrown up

A ball leaves the hand at 12 m/s, straight up. Its \(v\)–\(t\) graph is a straight line from 12 m/s, falling at 9.81 m/s². It crosses zero at \(t = 12/9.81 = 1.22\) s. That is the top. The area of the triangle above the axis is \(\tfrac12(1.22)(12) = 7.34\) m, the height climbed. The same height from \(v^2 = v_0^2 - 2gh\) is \(144/19.62 = 7.34\) m.

The \(s\)–\(t\) graph is a parabola that bends downward the whole time: going up, at the top, and coming down. The acceleration never changes sign.

In the Rectilinear lab, the Two constant accelerations scenario is a two-step graph. The Ramp scenario is the linear acceleration above. A fourth-order Runge–Kutta step gives the same \(s\) and \(v\) as the integrals.

What the model leaves out

A step graph leaves out the rounding between corners. Real acceleration cannot jump: a jump would need an infinite rate of change of force. The area rule still holds for a smooth graph. Integrate \(a\) over the interval, then step \(s\) with the area under \(v\).

04Projectile motion

Horizontal velocity stays constant when the only acceleration is gravity. Vertical velocity changes at \(g = 9.81\) m/s². Resolve the launch velocity, then treat the two axes separately.

Why

The acceleration vector is \((0, -g)\). Each component is a constant-acceleration problem from section 2. Horizontally \(a_x = 0\), so \(x = v_0\cos\theta\,t\). Vertically \(y = v_0\sin\theta\,t - \tfrac12 g t^2\). Eliminate \(t\) between them:

\[ y = x\tan\theta - \frac{g\,x^2}{2v_0^2\cos^2\theta}. \]

The path is a parabola. On level ground it returns to \(y = 0\) at \(x = v_0^2\sin 2\theta / g\). That range is largest at \(\theta = 45^\circ\).

Worked example · level ground

Launch at 20 m/s, 30° above the horizontal, from level ground. The range is 35.3 m. The maximum height is 5.10 m. The time of flight is 2.04 s.

Why

\(v_x = 17.32\) m/s and \(v_y = 10.00\) m/s. The vertical velocity is zero at the top, so the height is \(v_y^2 / (2g)\). Flight time is \(2v_y / g\). Range is \(v_x\) times that time. The range formula agrees: \(400\sin 60^\circ / 9.81 = 35.3\) m.

Worked example · off a cliff

A ball leaves a 20 m cliff horizontally at 15 m/s. Vertically it starts from rest, so \(20 = \tfrac12(9.81)t^2\) gives \(t = 2.02\) s. In that time it travels \(15 \times 2.02 = 30.3\) m out. At impact its vertical speed is \(9.81 \times 2.02 = 19.8\) m/s. Its speed is \(\sqrt{15^2 + 19.8^2} = 24.8\) m/s.

Check with energy: \(v^2 = 15^2 + 2(9.81)(20) = 617\), so \(v = 24.8\) m/s. The two routes agree.

Worked example · a punt in feet

A ball is kicked at 80 ft/s, 50° above level ground. In US units \(g = 32.2\) ft/s². The components are \(v_x = 51.4\) ft/s and \(v_y = 61.3\) ft/s. The flight time is \(2v_y/g = 3.81\) s, the range \(51.4 \times 3.81 = 196\) ft, and the peak \(v_y^2/(2g) = 58.3\) ft.

Check in SI. 80 ft/s is 24.38 m/s, and \(24.38^2\sin 100^\circ/9.81 = 59.7\) m, which is 196 ft.

At the top of the flight, the vertical velocity is zero. Nothing else is. The horizontal velocity is still \(v_0\cos\theta\), so the speed is at its least but not zero. The acceleration is still \(g\), straight down.

Common mistake

"At the top the ball stops, so its acceleration is zero." Both halves are wrong. In the level-ground example the ball still moves at 17.3 m/s at the top. Its acceleration is 9.81 m/s² downward, as it is everywhere else on the path. If \(a\) were zero at the top, the ball would keep its velocity and never come down.

The top of the arc is also a curve, so section 5 applies to it. There the velocity is horizontal and \(g\) is perpendicular to it. All of \(g\) is normal acceleration, so \(g = v_x^2/\rho\).

Worked example · the bend at the top

For the 20 m/s, 30° launch, \(v_x = 17.32\) m/s. At the top, \(\rho = v_x^2/g = 300/9.81 = 30.6\) m. At the launch point the speed is 20 m/s, and the normal part of \(g\) is \(g\cos 30^\circ = 8.50\) m/s². There \(\rho = 400/8.50 = 47.1\) m. The path is most sharply curved at its top.

Check from the parabola. With \(y = x\tan\theta - g x^2/(2v_0^2\cos^2\theta)\), \(|y''| = g/(v_0^2\cos^2\theta) = 9.81/300 = 0.0327\) m⁻¹. At the top \(y' = 0\), so \(\rho = 1/0.0327 = 30.6\) m. The two routes agree.

Common mistake

The 45° rule and the range formula assume the landing height equals the launch height. From the cliff, a 45° launch is not the longest throw, and \(v_0^2\sin 2\theta/g\) does not apply. Solve the vertical equation for the actual landing height. Hibbeler §12.6.

Key

Two independent problems share one clock. Horizontal: constant velocity. Vertical: constant acceleration \(-g\). Solve for \(t\) in the axis that has enough information, then use it in the other.

What the model leaves out

This projectile leaves out drag and wind. With drag, the horizontal acceleration is not zero, and the two axes no longer separate: drag on each axis depends on the total speed. It also leaves out the variation of \(g\) with height and the curvature of the ground. Over a few hundred metres neither matters.

05Normal and tangential components

The tangential piece \(a_t\) changes the speed. The normal piece \(a_n = v^2 / \rho\) turns the velocity. They are perpendicular. The magnitude is the root of the sum of the squares.

Why

Write the velocity as speed times direction: \(\vec{v} = v\,\hat{u}_t\). Differentiate with the product rule: \(\vec{a} = \dot{v}\,\hat{u}_t + v\,\dot{\hat{u}}_t\).

The unit tangent keeps length 1, so its rate of change is perpendicular to it, along \(\hat{u}_n\). It turns at the rate the path turns: \(d\theta/dt = (d\theta/ds)(ds/dt) = v/\rho\). So \(\dot{\hat{u}}_t = (v/\rho)\,\hat{u}_n\), and the second term is \((v^2/\rho)\,\hat{u}_n\).

\[ \vec{a} = \dot{v}\,\hat{u}_t + \frac{v^2}{\rho}\,\hat{u}_n, \qquad |\vec{a}| = \sqrt{a_t^2 + a_n^2}. \]

For a graph \(y = f(x)\), the radius of curvature is

\[ \rho = \frac{(1 + y'^2)^{3/2}}{|y''|}. \]

The unit normal points toward the concave side. A positive \(y''\) points it upward. On a circle, \(\rho\) is the radius. On a straight line, \(y'' = 0\) and \(\rho\) is infinite, so \(a_n = 0\).

Worked example · a parabola

The path is \(y = \tfrac{1}{2} x^2\). At \(x = 2\) m the speed is 3 m/s and \(a_t = 0\). Then \(y' = 2\) and \(y'' = 1\), so \(\rho = 11.18\) m. The normal acceleration is 0.805 m/s². That is also \(|a|\).

Worked example · speeding up through a bend

The path is \(y = 0.4x^2\). At \(x = 1.2\) m, \(y' = 0.96\) and \(y'' = 0.8\), so \(\rho = 1.9216^{3/2}/0.8 = 3.33\) m. The speed is 4 m/s and rising at 1.5 m/s². Then \(a_n = 16/3.33 = 4.81\) m/s², and \(|a| = \sqrt{1.5^2 + 4.81^2} = 5.03\) m/s².

Worked example · braking in a curve

A car on a 100 m radius curve travels at 25 m/s and brakes at 2 m/s². The normal part is \(25^2/100 = 6.25\) m/s², toward the centre. The tangential part is 2 m/s², backward. The total is \(\sqrt{6.25^2 + 2^2} = 6.56\) m/s². The tyres must supply all of it.

Worked example · a wavy track

The path is \(y = 0.5\sin x\), in metres. At \(x = 1\) m, \(y' = 0.5\cos 1 = 0.270\) and \(y'' = -0.5\sin 1 = -0.421\) m⁻¹. Then \(\rho = (1.0730)^{3/2}/0.421 = 2.64\) m. The second derivative is negative, so the track is concave down there and the normal points down and toward the inside of the crest.

At 3 m/s with \(a_t = 2\) m/s², \(a_n = 9/2.64 = 3.41\) m/s² and \(|a| = \sqrt{2^2 + 3.41^2} = 3.95\) m/s². At the crest, \(x = \pi/2\), the slope is zero and \(\rho = 1/0.5 = 2\) m. The level-path shortcut \(\rho = 1/|y''|\) holds only there.

Which way does the acceleration point? Sketch the two pieces and add them.

  • At constant speed, \(a_t = 0\). The acceleration points straight into the concave side of the path, toward the centre of curvature.
  • Speeding up through a bend, \(a_t\) points forward. The acceleration leans ahead of the normal.
  • Braking through a bend, \(a_t\) points backward. The acceleration leans behind the normal.
  • On a straight path, \(a_n = 0\). The acceleration lies along the path, forward or back.

The angle between the acceleration and the normal is \(\arctan(a_t/a_n)\). For the braking car above, that is \(\arctan(2/6.25) = 17.7^\circ\) behind the line to the centre.

Common mistake

Acceleration is not tangent to the path. In the parabola example \(a_t = 0\), and \(a_n = 0.805\) m/s². A student who reports \(a = a_t\) misses the turn. Hibbeler §12.7.

Key

\(a_t\) changes speed. \(a_n\) changes direction. Either one can be zero while the other is not.

In the Path lab, the gold arrow is \(a_t\) and the normal arrow points to the concave side. A numerical second derivative gives the same \(\rho\) as the formula.

What the model leaves out

The curvature formula is for a plane curve \(y = f(x)\). A space curve needs a binormal direction and a different radius. The model also takes \(a_t\) as given, rather than from a force. Unit 2 supplies the force.

06Relative motion, translating axes

Axes fixed to A, and not rotating, see B by subtraction. The positions are not needed for the relative velocity or the relative acceleration.

Why

Positions add as vectors: \(\vec{r}_B = \vec{r}_A + \vec{r}_{B/A}\). Differentiate in time. Because the axes on A translate and do not rotate, their unit vectors are constant, and each term differentiates directly. One derivative gives the velocities. A second gives the accelerations.

\[ \vec{v}_{B/A} = \vec{v}_B - \vec{v}_A, \qquad \vec{a}_{B/A} = \vec{a}_B - \vec{a}_A. \]
Worked example · two velocities

A moves at \((3, 1)\) m/s. B moves at \((1, 4)\) m/s. Then \(\vec{v}_{B/A} = (-2, 3)\) m/s. The relative speed is 3.61 m/s. That direction is neither velocity.

Worked example · crossing a river

A boat moves at 3 m/s relative to the water, pointed straight across. The river flows at 1.5 m/s. The boat's ground velocity is the sum: \((1.5, 3)\) m/s. Its speed over ground is 3.35 m/s, at 26.6° downstream from straight across.

To land directly opposite, point upstream by \(\varphi\) with \(3\sin\varphi = 1.5\), so \(\varphi = 30^\circ\). The crossing speed drops to \(3\cos 30^\circ = 2.60\) m/s.

Worked example · relative acceleration

At a junction, car A speeds up eastward at 2 m/s² while car B speeds up northward at 1 m/s². Seen from A, B accelerates at \(\vec{a}_{B/A} = (0, 1) - (2, 0) = (-2, 1)\) m/s². Its size is 2.24 m/s². An observer in A sees B pull away north-westward, although B never moves west.

Worked example · rain on a windscreen

Rain falls straight down at 8 m/s. A car drives east at 20 m/s. Relative to the car, the rain moves at \(\vec{v}_{r/c} = (0, -8) - (20, 0) = (-20, -8)\) m/s. Its speed is 21.5 m/s. It streaks backward at \(\arctan(8/20) = 21.8^\circ\) below the horizontal, although on the ground it falls straight down.

The driver sees the rain come almost head-on. A passenger standing still sees it fall vertically. Both are right: each measures the rain against their own axes.

Relative motion is a choice of observer, not a different physics. Pick the frame in which the question is simplest. To find when two ships come closest, work in one ship's frame: the other moves in a straight line at the relative velocity, and the closest point is the foot of a perpendicular. To find where a dropped package lands, work on the ground: it leaves the plane with the plane's velocity and falls as a projectile.

Worked example · closest approach

Ship B starts 10 km east of ship A. Relative to A, B moves at \((-6, 8)\) km/h, a relative speed of 10 km/h. In A's frame B travels a straight line. The time of closest approach is \(-(\vec{r}\cdot\vec{v})/v^2 = 60/100 = 0.6\) h. Then B sits at \((10 - 3.6,\ 4.8) = (6.4, 4.8)\) km from A, a distance of 8.0 km.

Check with the perpendicular: the distance from A to the line is \(|r_x v_y - r_y v_x|/v = 80/10 = 8.0\) km. The two routes agree.

Common mistake

Subtracting speeds instead of vectors. In the first example, \(|\vec{v}_B| - |\vec{v}_A| = 4.12 - 3.16 = 0.96\) m/s. The relative speed is 3.61 m/s. Speeds subtract only when the velocities are parallel. Hibbeler §12.10.

Key

Subtract the vectors. Do not subtract the speeds and then invent a direction.

In the Relative lab, drag either velocity. The relative arrow is drawn from A, and its components are the differences.

What the model leaves out

These axes translate with A and do not rotate. A rotating frame adds terms this section does not carry: a Coriolis term and a centripetal term. Rotating axes stay out of the course. The model also treats A and B as points with no size.

07Application: a sounding rocket

A sounding rocket flies straight up. Its motor burns for a few seconds and then the rocket coasts. The whole flight is rectilinear motion in two pieces, each with a constant acceleration. Nothing beyond this unit is needed to find how high it goes.

During the burn the net acceleration is the thrust less the weight, over the mass: \(a = T/m - g\). Treat the mass as constant for now; Unit 3 lets it fall as propellant burns. After burnout only gravity acts, and the acceleration is \(-g\).

Why the apogee splits into two heights

From rest, with constant \(a\) for \(t_b\) seconds, section 2 gives the state at burnout:

\[ v_{bo} = a\,t_b, \qquad h_{bo} = \tfrac12 a\,t_b^2. \]

Then the rocket coasts with \(a = -g\) until its speed is zero. Time is not needed, so use \(v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\,\Delta s\) with \(v = 0\):

\[ \Delta h_{\text{coast}} = \frac{v_{bo}^2}{2g}. \]

The apogee is the sum, \(h_{\max} = h_{bo} + v_{bo}^2/2g\). It is reached at \(t = t_b + v_{bo}/g\).

Section 3 gives the same number another way. The \(v\)–\(t\) graph is a triangle: a line rising to \(v_{bo}\), then a line falling at slope \(-g\) to zero. Its area is \(\tfrac12 v_{bo}(t_b + v_{bo}/g)\), which expands to the same sum.

Worked example · a student rocket

A student rocket accelerates at a net 49 m/s², about five times \(g\), for 2.5 s. At burnout \(v_{bo} = 49 \times 2.5 = 122.5\) m/s and \(h_{bo} = \tfrac12(49)(2.5)^2 = 153\) m.

The coast adds \(122.5^2/(2 \times 9.81) = 765\) m. The apogee is 918 m, reached 15.0 s after launch. The motor is out after the first 17% of the climb. The rest is momentum.

The area of the \(v\)–\(t\) triangle checks it: \(\tfrac12(122.5)(14.99) = 918\) m.

Worked example · thrust and weight

A 12 kg rocket has a motor that gives 600 N for 3.0 s. The net acceleration is \(600/12 - 9.81 = 40.2\) m/s². At burnout it is moving at \(40.19 \times 3 = 121\) m/s, at a height of \(\tfrac12(40.19)(9) = 181\) m.

The coast adds \(120.6^2/19.62 = 741\) m, for an apogee of 922 m. Leaving out the weight would give \(a = 50\) m/s² and an apogee of 1,500 m: an error of 60%.

Worked example · a long gentle burn

The same burnout speed can come from a gentler burn: 20 m/s² for 6 s reaches 120 m/s, about the student rocket's speed. But it reaches it higher, at \(\tfrac12(20)(36) = 360\) m. The coast adds \(120^2/19.62 = 734\) m, and the apogee is 1,094 m.

Same burnout speed, higher burnout, higher apogee. In this model, with no drag, a longer burn wins. A real rocket that burns long and slowly pays a gravity loss: it holds itself up against \(g\) for longer. Here that is already inside the net \(a\).

Worked example · a hop test

A test vehicle hovers just above its own weight: a net 3 m/s² for 4 s. It reaches 12 m/s at 24 m, coasts up another \(12^2/19.62 = 7.34\) m, and peaks at 31.3 m. With the engine out, it falls back to the pad 7.75 s after lift-off.

The fall from 31.3 m takes \(\sqrt{2(31.34)/9.81} = 2.53\) s, and the peak came at \(4 + 12/9.81 = 5.22\) s. Their sum is 7.75 s.

Common mistake

Two slips come up often. The first is stopping at burnout: 153 m for the student rocket, when the apogee is 918 m. The second is subtracting \(g\) from an acceleration that is already net, or forgetting it in \(T/m\). Read whether the acceleration given is the thrust's alone or the net one. Hibbeler §12.2–12.3.

Key

Split the flight where the acceleration changes. The coast height depends only on the burnout speed: \(v_{bo}^2/2g\). The area under \(v\)–\(t\) is the height.

In the Boost lab, move the burn time and the acceleration. The shaded triangle under \(v\)–\(t\) is the apogee, and the column on the left shows where burnout and apogee sit.

What the model leaves out

Drag is the big omission. At 120 m/s the air pushes back hard, and a real rocket of this size reaches perhaps two-thirds of these heights. The model also keeps the mass constant, when a solid motor may burn a fifth of the rocket's mass; Unit 3 adds that. Thrust is not constant either: a real motor ramps up and tails off. The area rule still holds for any thrust curve. Wind and weathercocking tilt the path, which makes it a projectile problem.
