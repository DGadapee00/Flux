Rigid-body kinematics

A rigid body in the plane has translation and one rotation. The instantaneous centre is the point that is at rest right now. It moves as the body moves. Each relation below comes from one fact: the distance between two points of a rigid body never changes. The numbers are recomputed.

Part I

Rotation about an axis

01Fixed-axis rotation

A point on a body that rotates about a fixed axis moves on a circle. Its speed is the radius times \(\omega\). Its tangential acceleration is the radius times \(\alpha\).

Why

A point at radius \(r\) travels the arc \(s = r\theta\), with \(\theta\) in radians. Differentiate: \(v = r\dot\theta = r\omega\). Differentiate again: \(a_t = r\alpha\). The point also moves on a circle, so Unit 1's normal acceleration applies: \(a_n = v^2/r = r\omega^2\), toward the axis.

With constant \(\alpha\), the angle obeys the same equations as a particle on a line: \(\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t\), \(\theta = \theta_0 + \omega_0 t + \tfrac12\alpha t^2\), and \(\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha\Delta\theta\).

\[ v = r\omega, \qquad a_t = r\alpha, \qquad a_n = r\omega^2. \]
Worked example · a flywheel spins up

A flywheel starts from rest and speeds up at 3 rad/s² for 4 s. Then \(\omega = 12\) rad/s, and it has turned 24 rad, which is 3.82 revolutions. A point 0.5 m out moves at 6 m/s. Its tangential acceleration is 1.5 m/s². Its normal acceleration is \(0.5 \times 144 = 72\) m/s², and the normal part dominates.

Worked example · a grinder winds down

A grinder at 3000 rpm is switched off and coasts to rest in 20 s at a steady rate. In rad/s, \(\omega_0 = 314\). So \(\alpha = -314/20 = -15.7\) rad/s². The angle turned is the average speed times the time, \(157 \times 20 = 3140\) rad, which is 500 revolutions.

Two wheels joined by a belt or by meshing teeth share one speed at the contact. That links their angular speeds.

Why

Points in contact share one arc speed, \(r\omega\). An open belt keeps the sense of rotation. External gears push back, so the sense reverses. Differentiating \(r_1\omega_1 = r_2\omega_2\) gives the same ratio for \(\alpha\).

Worked example · an open belt

An open belt joins pulleys of radii 0.15 m and 0.3 m. The driver turns at 20 rad/s. The belt speed is 3 m/s. The driven pulley turns at 10 rad/s, in the same sense.

Worked example · external gears

External gears have pitch radii 0.1 m and 0.25 m. The driver turns at 8 rad/s and speeds up at 2 rad/s². The driven gear turns at −3.2 rad/s and at −0.8 rad/s².

Worked example · a motor pulley in feet

A motor turns a pulley of 3 in radius at 1800 rpm. In radians per second, \(\omega = 1800 \times 2\pi/60 = 188.5\) rad/s. The radius is 0.25 ft, so the belt runs at 47.1 ft/s. The ratio of radii carries over unchanged in any unit.

Common mistake

Turning the ratio upside down. The contact speed is shared, so the larger wheel turns slower. For the open belt, \(\omega_2 = (0.15/0.3)(20) = 10\) rad/s. Inverted, it gives 40 rad/s. A belt leaves the sense alone; external gears reverse it. Hibbeler §16.3.

Common mistake

Multiplying rpm by a radius. \(v = r\omega\) needs \(\omega\) in rad/s. For the motor pulley, \(1800 \times 0.25 = 450\) is not a speed in any unit. Convert first: 188.5 rad/s, and 47.1 ft/s.

What the model leaves out

The belt does not slip, and the gear teeth stay in mesh. The model leaves out belt creep, the small slip as a loaded belt stretches around the pulley, and a tooth leaving contact. It also treats the axis as fixed, with no bearing play.

02Absolute motion analysis

Write the coordinate you want in terms of one angle. Differentiate that equation. The velocity comes out of the first derivative, and the acceleration out of the second.

Why

The rod closes the linkage: \(\sin\phi = (r/\ell)\sin\theta\). The slider coordinate is \(x = r\cos\theta + \ell\cos\phi\). Time derivatives of \(x\) are the slider velocity and acceleration.

Differentiate the closure: \(\cos\phi\,\dot\phi = (r/\ell)\cos\theta\,\dot\theta\). That gives \(\dot\phi\). Then \(\dot x = -r\sin\theta\,\omega - \ell\sin\phi\,\dot\phi\).

Worked example · mid stroke

A crank of 0.2 m turns at 10 rad/s. The rod is 0.8 m. At 90° the slider is at 0.775 m. Its velocity is −2 m/s.

Worked example · fully extended

The same crank at 0° puts the slider at 1 m. The velocity is 0. The crank and the rod are in one line.

Worked example · at 45°

The same crank at 45°. The closure gives \(\sin\phi = 0.25\sin 45^\circ = 0.177\), so \(\phi = 10.2^\circ\). The slider is at \(0.141 + 0.787 = 0.929\) m. The rod turns at \(\dot\phi = 0.25\cos 45^\circ \times 10/\cos\phi = 1.80\) rad/s. The slider velocity is \(-1.414 - 0.8(0.177)(1.80) = -1.67\) m/s.

The crank's own term, \(-r\omega\sin\theta\), is \(-1.41\) m/s. The rod's swing adds the rest. At 90° the rod term vanishes, which is why the mid-stroke speed is exactly \(r\omega\).

Worked example · the ladder by geometry

A 4 m ladder makes angle \(\theta\) with the floor. Its foot is at \(x = 4\cos\theta\) and its top at \(y = 4\sin\theta\). With the foot moving out at 2 m/s at \(\theta = 60^\circ\): \(\dot x = -4\sin\theta\,\dot\theta = 2\), so \(\dot\theta = -0.577\) rad/s. Then \(\dot y = 4\cos\theta\,\dot\theta = -1.155\) m/s. Section 4 finds the same numbers another way.

Common mistake

Write the position, then differentiate. Do not start from the acceleration formula. And differentiate every angle that changes: dropping \(\dot\phi\) at 45° gives −1.41 m/s instead of −1.67 m/s. Hibbeler §16.4.

In the Slider-crank lab, sweep the crank angle. The readout's velocity and acceleration come from the derivatives, and a finite difference of the position agrees.

What the model leaves out

One position equation is enough when the linkage has one degree of freedom. The model leaves out a flexible rod and a joint with clearance. Mechanisms with two loops need two closure equations, solved together.

A fixed axis fixes the path of every point. Part II handles a body whose axis moves, and finds the velocity of one point from another.

Part II

Relative motion in a body

03Relative velocity

Two points of a rigid body differ by a rotation. The velocity of B is the velocity of A plus \(\omega\) crossed with the vector from A to B.

Why

Positions add: \(\vec r_B = \vec r_A + \vec r_{B/A}\). A rigid body keeps the length of \(\vec r_{B/A}\) fixed, so that vector can only turn. A vector of fixed length turning at \(\omega\) changes at \(\omega\times\vec r_{B/A}\), perpendicular to itself. Differentiate the sum.

\[ \vec v_B = \vec v_A + \omega\times\vec r_{B/A}. \]

Any point of the body can serve as A. Choose the one whose velocity is known completely, such as a pin on a crank or a point on a guide. Choose B where one direction is known, such as a slider. Each vector equation gives two scalar equations, so it can find two unknowns: usually \(\omega\) and one speed.

Worked example · a bar

Point A moves at (1, 0.5) m/s. Point B is 0.4 m to the right and 0.3 m up. \(\omega\) is 2 rad/s counterclockwise. The velocity of B is (0.4, 1.3) m/s.

Worked example · the ladder by relative velocity

The same 4 m ladder at 60°. Take A at the foot, moving at (2, 0) m/s, and B at the top, which can only move vertically. Then \(\vec r_{B/A} = (-2, 3.46)\) m. The cross product gives \(\omega\mathbf{k}\times\vec r_{B/A} = (-3.46\omega, -2\omega)\).

B has no horizontal velocity, so \(2 - 3.46\omega = 0\), and \(\omega = 0.577\) rad/s. Then \(v_{B,y} = -2(0.577) = -1.155\) m/s. Geometry and relative velocity agree.

Worked example · a wheel's top, relatively

A wheel of radius \(R\) rolls with its centre at \(v\) and \(\omega = -v/R\) (clockwise). Take A at the centre. The top is at \(\vec r_{B/A} = (0, R)\). Then \(\omega\mathbf{k}\times(0, R) = (-\omega R, 0) = (v, 0)\). So the top moves at \(v + v = 2v\). The bottom, at \((0, -R)\), gets \(v - v = 0\). Section 4 reaches both from the instantaneous centre.

Common mistake

Dropping the minus sign in \(\omega\mathbf{k}\times(x, y) = (-\omega y,\ \omega x)\). For the bar, the rotation adds \((-0.6, 0.8)\) m/s. With the sign lost it adds \((0.6, 0.8)\), and B moves at \((1.6, 1.3)\) m/s instead of \((0.4, 1.3)\). That arrow is not perpendicular to the bar, which a rigid bar forbids. Hibbeler §16.5.

Common mistake

Leaving out \(\vec v_A\). The rotation term is B's velocity relative to A. On its own it gives \((-0.6, 0.8)\) m/s for the bar, the velocity B would have if A were pinned.

Key

Positive \(\omega\) is counterclockwise: \(\omega\mathbf{k}\times(x, y) = (-\omega y,\ \omega x)\). Each constraint, such as a guide or a pin, gives one scalar equation.

What the model leaves out

The body is rigid, so the distance from A to B is fixed. A body that bends would need another term in the relative velocity. The equation also assumes plane motion: one \(\omega\), along \(\mathbf{k}\).

04Instantaneous centre

The instantaneous centre is the point of the body, or of its extension, whose velocity is zero at this instant. Every other point moves perpendicular to the line drawn from the centre, with speed \(\omega\) times that distance.

Why

Put A at the instantaneous centre, where \(\vec v_A = 0\). The relative velocity equation leaves \(\vec v_B = \omega\times\vec r_{B/IC}\). So every point moves perpendicular to its line from the centre, at \(\omega\) times its distance. Turned around, the perpendiculars to two known velocities meet at the centre.

Worked example · a sliding ladder

A 4 m ladder leans at 60° to the floor. The foot moves out at 2 m/s. The centre is the corner across from the ladder. \(\omega\) is 0.577 rad/s. The top falls at 1.155 m/s.

The foot is \(4\sin 60^\circ = 3.46\) m below the centre, so \(\omega = 2/3.46\). The top is \(4\cos 60^\circ = 2\) m from it, so its speed is \(2\omega\).

Worked example · a shallower ladder

The same ladder at 30°, with the same foot speed, has \(\omega = 1\) rad/s. The top falls at 3.46 m/s. A shallower ladder drops the top faster.

Worked example · the contact point

A wheel of radius 0.4 m rolls at 2 m/s. The contact point is the instantaneous centre. \(\omega\) is −5 rad/s. The top moves at 4 m/s.

Worked example · the middle of the ladder

The ladder's midpoint, at 60°, is 2 m from the instantaneous centre, the half-diagonal of the rectangle the ladder spans. It moves at \(2 \times 0.577 = 1.155\) m/s, perpendicular to that line. Every point on the ladder is at a different distance, so every point has a different speed.

Worked example · a four-bar

A four-bar has a 1 m crank pinned at the origin, a 2.5 m coupler, and a 2 m rocker pinned 3 m away along the ground. The crank is at 60° and turns at 2 rad/s, so its tip A, at (0.5, 0.866) m, moves at 2 m/s perpendicular to the crank. The coupler's other end, B, is at (2.74, 1.98) m, and it moves perpendicular to the rocker.

Extend the crank line and the rocker line. They meet at (2.44, 4.22) m, the coupler's instantaneous centre. A is 3.88 m from it, so the coupler turns at \(2/3.88 = 0.516\) rad/s, clockwise. B is 2.26 m from it, so B moves at \(0.516 \times 2.26 = 1.17\) m/s. The rocker turns at \(1.17/2 = 0.583\) rad/s.

Common mistake

Swapping the distances. On the ladder at 60°, the foot is 3.46 m from the centre and the top 2 m. Dividing the foot speed by 2 m gives \(\omega = 1\) rad/s, and the top then falls at 3.46 m/s instead of 1.155 m/s. Measure each distance from the centre to that point. Hibbeler §16.6.

Key

Draw the lines through two points, each perpendicular to that point's velocity. They meet at the centre. If they are parallel, the body is translating, and \(\omega = 0\).

In the Centre lab, move the foot speed or the angle. The centre stays at the corner of the rectangle, and the speeds scale with the distances to it.

What the model leaves out

The instantaneous centre is at rest only at this instant. It is not a pin. Using it as a fixed axis at a later time is a different problem. It gives velocities only: the instantaneous centre's own acceleration is not zero, so it cannot be used for accelerations.

Another view. MIT OCW 8.01SC (Fall 2016), 35.5 Contact Point of a Wheel Rolling Without Slipping.

05Relative acceleration

The acceleration of B adds three pieces: the acceleration of A, the tangential piece \(\alpha\times r\), and the centripetal piece \(-\omega^2 r\).

Why

Differentiate \(\mathbf{v}_B = \mathbf{v}_A + \omega\times\mathbf{r}\). The product rule gives two new pieces. One is \(\dot\omega\times\mathbf{r} = \alpha\times\mathbf{r}\), tangent to the relative position. The other is \(\omega\times(\omega\times\mathbf{r})\), which in the plane is \(-\omega^2\mathbf{r}\), toward A.

\[ \vec a_B = \vec a_A + \alpha\times\vec r - \omega^2\vec r. \]
Worked example · the outer dead centre

The 0.2 m crank and 0.8 m rod are fully extended. \(\omega\) is 10 rad/s and \(\alpha\) is 0. The slider speed is 0. The acceleration is −25 m/s².

Worked example · the inner dead centre

Folded, the same crank still has speed 0. The acceleration is 15 m/s², outward along the slider.

Worked example · the slider at 45°

At 45° the slider's acceleration is −14.2 m/s². A centred second difference of the position, \(\omega^2\,x''(\theta)\), gives the same −14.22 m/s².

Worked example · the ladder's top

Keep the ladder's foot at a steady 2 m/s. At 60°, \(\dot\theta = -0.577\) rad/s. A steady foot means \(\ddot x = 0\), and differentiating \(\dot x = -4\sin\theta\,\dot\theta\) gives \(\ddot\theta = -\cos\theta\,\dot\theta^2/\sin\theta = -0.192\) rad/s². Then \(\ddot y = 4(-\sin\theta\,\dot\theta^2 + \cos\theta\,\ddot\theta) = -1.54\) m/s². The top speeds up as it falls, though the foot does not.

Common mistake

Zero velocity does not mean zero acceleration. The \(-\omega^2 r\) term remains at a dead centre. Hibbeler §16.7.

Common mistake

Taking the crank pin's acceleration for the slider's. At the outer dead centre the pin accelerates at \(r\omega^2 = 20\) m/s² toward the crank centre. The slider's is 25 m/s². The rod turns too, at \(\omega_{AB} = r\omega/\ell = 2.5\) rad/s, and its \(\omega_{AB}^2\ell = 5\) m/s² adds the rest.

In the Slider-crank lab, set the crank to 0° and then to 180°. The speed reads zero both times. The acceleration does not.

Another view. MIT OCW 8.01SC (Fall 2016), 35.3 Rolling Wheel in the Ground Frame; 2.003SC (Fall 2011), Lecture 2, Velocity, Acceleration, and Rotational Motion.

06Rotating axes

A pin that slides in the slot of a turning rocker is not a point of the rocker. Its motion is easiest to describe from axes fixed to the rocker: the pin is at distance \(\rho\) along the slot and slides at \(v_{rel}\). Those axes turn at \(\Omega\), so their unit vectors change in time, and that adds a term no translating frame has.

Why

Let the axes x–y turn at \(\Omega\) with origin A. Each unit vector changes as \(d\hat\imath/dt = \vec\Omega\times\hat\imath\). For B at \(\vec r = x\hat\imath + y\hat\jmath\) in those axes, \(d\vec r/dt = (\dot x\hat\imath + \dot y\hat\jmath) + \vec\Omega\times\vec r = \vec v_{rel} + \vec\Omega\times\vec r\).

Apply the same rule to \(\vec v_{rel}\) and to \(\vec\Omega\times\vec r\). The first gives \(\vec a_{rel} + \vec\Omega\times\vec v_{rel}\). The second gives \(\dot{\vec\Omega}\times\vec r + \vec\Omega\times(\vec v_{rel} + \vec\Omega\times\vec r)\). The term \(\vec\Omega\times\vec v_{rel}\) appears twice.

\[ \begin{aligned} \vec v_B &= \vec v_A + \vec\Omega\times\vec r + \vec v_{rel},\\ \vec a_B &= \vec a_A + \dot{\vec\Omega}\times\vec r + \vec\Omega\times(\vec\Omega\times\vec r) + 2\vec\Omega\times\vec v_{rel} + \vec a_{rel}. \end{aligned} \]

\(2\vec\Omega\times\vec v_{rel}\) is the Coriolis acceleration. It needs both turning and sliding. With the origin fixed and B on a line through it, this is Unit 1's polar result again: \(a_\theta = r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta\).

Worked example · the shaper at 30°

A 0.1 m crank turns at a steady 4 rad/s about C, 0.25 m above the rocker's pivot O. At 30° the pin is at \((0.0866, 0.300)\) m, so \(\rho = 0.312\) m. The crank gives the pin 0.4 m/s at right angles to the crank, and 1.6 m/s² toward C.

Across the slot the pin's velocity is \(0.288\) m/s, so \(\Omega = 0.288/0.312 = 0.923\) rad/s. Along it, \(v_{rel} = 0.277\) m/s. The Coriolis term is \(2(0.923)(0.277) = 0.512\) m/s².

Across the slot the pin's acceleration is \(1.109\) m/s², and it equals \(\rho\dot\Omega + 2\Omega v_{rel}\). So \(\dot\Omega = (1.109 - 0.512)/0.312 = 1.91\) rad/s².

Worked example · the top of the stroke

Turn the crank to 90°. The pin is straight above C, moving straight across the slot, so \(v_{rel} = 0\) and the Coriolis term vanishes. The rocker turns at \(\Omega = 0.4/0.35 = 1.14\) rad/s, and \(\dot\Omega = 0\): this is the rocker's fastest forward swing.

Worked example · the quick return

At 270° the pin is below C, only 0.15 m from O. The same 0.4 m/s swings the rocker back at \(0.4/0.15 = 2.67\) rad/s. The rocker moves forward slowly while the pin is above C and returns fast while it is below. A shaper cuts on the slow stroke and returns quickly.

Worked example · a rod on a moving cart

A rod is pinned to a cart that accelerates at 2 m/s² along the rod's present direction. The rod turns at 3 rad/s and speeds up at 2 rad/s². A collar 0.8 m out slides along it at a steady 0.5 m/s.

Along the rod: \(2 + 0 - 3^2(0.8) = -5.2\) m/s². Across it: \(2(0.8) + 2(3)(0.5) = 4.6\) m/s². The cart's acceleration adds unchanged; the Coriolis term is 3 m/s² of the 4.6.

Worked example · walking on a turntable

A playground turntable turns at 1 rad/s. A 30 kg child walks straight out at 1 m/s and is 2 m from the centre. The Coriolis term is \(2(1)(1) = 2\) m/s², ahead in the sense of the turn, beside 2 m/s² of centripetal pull inward. The platform must push the child sideways with \(30 \times 2 = 60\) N: that is the sideways shove a child feels walking out.

Common mistake

Leaving out \(2\vec\Omega\times\vec v_{rel}\). For the shaper at 30° that gives \(\dot\Omega = 1.109/0.312 = 3.55\) rad/s² instead of 1.91. Hibbeler §16.8.

Common mistake

Giving the rocker the crank's angular velocity. They are different bodies. The rocker turns at the part of the pin's velocity across the slot divided by \(\rho\): 0.923 rad/s, not 4 rad/s. Hibbeler §16.8.

In the Coriolis lab, play the crank and watch the purple curve against the dashed one: they part most where the pin slides fastest. At 90° and 270° the two meet, because the pin does not slide there.

What the model leaves out

Everything stays in one plane, so \(\vec\Omega\) keeps its direction. In three dimensions \(\vec\Omega\) can tilt, and \(\dot{\vec\Omega}\) then has a part that changes the axis as well as the rate. The pin is also a point with no clearance in the slot.

Part III

Rolling, gears and an application

07Rolling without slip

A wheel that rolls without slip has its contact point instantaneously at rest. That point is the instantaneous centre. The centre of the wheel moves at \(v = R\omega\), and the top moves at \(2v\).

Why

No slip means the contact point matches the ground, so its velocity is zero. That point is the instantaneous centre. The wheel centre is one radius away, and the top is two.

No slip also ties distance to angle. A wheel that turns through \(\theta\) lays down an arc \(R\theta\) of its rim, so its centre moves \(s = R\theta\). Differentiate twice: \(v_G = R\omega\) and \(a_G = R\alpha\).

Worked example · the contact point

A wheel of radius 0.4 m rolls at 2 m/s. The contact point has speed 0. Its acceleration is 10 m/s² toward the centre.

Worked example · the top point

The point at the top of that wheel moves at 4 m/s. Its acceleration is 10 m/s² toward the centre.

Worked example · a side point

The point level with the centre moves at 2.83 m/s. The acceleration is still 10 m/s² toward the centre.

Worked example · a wheel speeding up

Let the same wheel's centre accelerate at 1 m/s², so \(\alpha = 2.5\) rad/s². At the contact point the tangential pieces cancel: \(a_G\) forward and \(\alpha R\) backward. Only the 10 m/s² upward remains. At the top they add: 2 m/s² forward and 10 m/s² down, a total of 10.2 m/s².

Worked example · a bicycle wheel in feet

A bicycle rolls at 15 mph, which is 22 ft/s, on wheels of 13 in radius. \(\omega = 22/(13/12) = 20.3\) rad/s, or 194 rpm. The top of each tyre moves at 44 ft/s, while the bottom is at rest on the road.

Worked example · a planet gear

A planet gear of radius 0.1 m rolls around the outside of a fixed sun gear of radius 0.2 m. The arm carrying the planet's centre turns at 3 rad/s. The planet's centre is 0.3 m from the sun's centre, so it moves at 0.9 m/s. The planet touches the fixed sun at its instantaneous centre, 0.1 m from its own centre. So the planet spins at \(0.9/0.1 = 9\) rad/s, three times the arm.

Common mistake

The contact point is instantaneously at rest. It is not a fixed point, so it still has acceleration \(v^2/R\) toward the centre. Hibbeler §16.6 and §16.7.

Key

Speed from the contact point: zero at the ground, \(v\) at the centre, \(2v\) at the top. Acceleration of a rim point: \(v^2/R\) toward the centre, plus the \(\alpha\) terms when the wheel speeds up.

In the Rolling lab, move the point around the rim. The speed traces the distance from the contact point, and the acceleration size stays at \(v^2/R\) while the wheel rolls steadily.

What the model leaves out

No slip is a constraint on the velocity. It does not say whether friction can enforce that constraint. The force question belongs to kinetics, in Unit 6. The wheel and the ground are also rigid: a real tyre flattens at the contact, so its rolling radius is a little less than its free radius.

Another view. MIT OCW 8.01SC (Fall 2016), 35.4 Rolling Without Slipping, Slipping, and Skidding, 35.2 Rolling Wheel in the Center of Mass Frame.

08Gear trains

A small electric motor runs best fast. The machine it drives often wants a lower speed, or two shafts at different speeds. A gear train between them sets each speed. It is section 1's contact rule, applied mesh by mesh.

Why

Gears that mesh have the same tooth size, the module \(m\). The pitch circle of a gear with \(N\) teeth has circumference \(N\) tooth spacings, so its radius is \(r = mN/2\). At a mesh the pitch circles roll on each other: \(r_1\omega_1 = r_2\omega_2\). The module cancels, and \(\omega_2/\omega_1 = N_1/N_2\). An external mesh reverses the sense.

Two gears keyed to one shaft share one \(\omega\), not one pitch-line speed. So a compound train multiplies its stage ratios:

\[ \frac{\omega_4}{\omega_1} = \frac{N_1}{N_2}\cdot\frac{N_3}{N_4}. \]

An idler meshes on both sides. Its teeth appear once on top and once below, and cancel: \(\omega_4/\omega_1 = N_1/N_4\).

In the Gear train lab, pinion 1 is on the motor. Gear 2 is on shaft 2. Gear 3 rides on the same shaft and drives gear 4 on the output shaft. All four gears have a 2 mm module, so a 20-tooth pinion has a 20 mm pitch radius.

Worked example · a two-stage reduction

The motor spins pinion 1, 20 teeth, at 30,000 rpm. Gear 2 has 60 teeth, so shaft 2 turns at \(30{,}000 \times 20/60 = 10{,}000\) rpm, the other way. Gear 3 has 25 teeth and gear 4 has 50, so the output turns at \(10{,}000 \times 25/50 = 5{,}000\) rpm. Two reversals: it turns the same way as the motor.

The pinion's pitch radius is 20 mm. In rad/s, \(\omega_1 = 30{,}000 \times 2\pi/60 = 3{,}142\) rad/s. The teeth of mesh 1–2 move at \(0.020 \times 3{,}142 = 62.8\) m/s. At mesh 3–4 they move at \(0.025 \times 1{,}047 = 26.2\) m/s.

Worked example · a second stage that steps up

Swap the second stage: gear 3 has 40 teeth and gear 4 has 20. Shaft 2 still turns at 10,000 rpm. The output turns at \(10{,}000 \times 40/20 = 20{,}000\) rpm. One train sets two shaft speeds, one below the motor's and one above shaft 2's. At mesh 3–4 the teeth move at \(0.040 \times 1{,}047 = 41.9\) m/s.

Worked example · an idler

Now gear 2, 40 teeth, meshes with pinion 1 and with gear 4, 50 teeth. Gear 2 turns at \(30{,}000 \times 20/40 = 15{,}000\) rpm. Gear 4 turns at \(15{,}000 \times 40/50 = 12{,}000\) rpm. That is \(30{,}000 \times 20/50\): the idler's 40 teeth cancel.

Check with the pitch lines. Gear 2's pitch radius is 40 mm, and \(15{,}000\) rpm is \(1{,}571\) rad/s. Its teeth move at \(0.040 \times 1{,}571 = 62.8\) m/s at both meshes. An idler passes one pitch-line speed straight through.

Worked example · bigger teeth

Go back to the two-stage reduction and double the module to 4 mm. Every radius doubles, every ratio stays, and the output still turns at 5,000 rpm. The pinion's radius is now 40 mm, so its teeth move at \(0.040 \times 3{,}142 = 126\) m/s. Bigger teeth carry more load, but they move faster at the same rpm.

Common mistake

Matching pitch-line speeds across a shaft. Gears 2 and 3 share \(\omega\), not \(r\omega\). In the reduction, gear 2's teeth move at 62.8 m/s and gear 3's at 26.2 m/s. Carrying 62.8 m/s on to gear 4 would give \(62.8/0.050 = 1{,}257\) rad/s, or 12,000 rpm, instead of 5,000. Hibbeler §16.3.

Key

Across a mesh, match \(r\omega\), so \(\omega\) goes as \(1/N\). Along a shaft, match \(\omega\). Count the external meshes for the sense.

What the model leaves out

The model treats each mesh as two pitch circles rolling without slip. It leaves out backlash, the play between teeth, and the twist of a shaft under load. It says nothing about torque: which gear carries the load, and how fast the motor can spin the train up, is Unit 6. Real teeth have involute profiles, cut so the speed ratio holds steady while each pair of teeth is in contact. The pitch circles stand in for that.

09Application: a road bike's drivetrain

A road bike turns pedalling into speed with a chain. Take a Shimano 105 groupset: a 50/34-tooth crankset with 172.5 mm cranks and an 11–28 cassette. Its cogs have 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 17, 19, 21, 23, 25 and 28 teeth. The chain has the ISO 606 pitch, 12.7 mm. The tyre is a 700×25, a 25 mm tyre on a 622 mm rim, about 672 mm across.

Derivation

A chain is a belt with teeth. Every link that leaves the chainring enters the cog. In one crank turn, \(N_f\) links leave the ring, so \(N_f\) teeth of the cog pass: the cog turns \(N_f/N_r\) times. The cog is fixed to the wheel's hub, so they share one \(\omega\):

\[ n_w = \frac{N_f}{N_r}\,n_c, \qquad v = \pi D\,n_w, \qquad v_{chain} = N_f\,p\,n_c . \]

Each \(n\) is in turns per second. Relative to the frame, the rim moves at \(v\). Section 7 says the same rim rolls on the road, so the bike moves at \(v\) too.

The chain wraps a sprocket as a polygon of side \(p\), not a circle. Its links sit on the pitch circle, \(r = p/[2\sin(\pi/N)]\). While one tooth carries the chain, its line runs along a chord, at \(\omega r\cos\phi\), with \(\phi\) from \(-\pi/N\) to \(\pi/N\). Averaged over the tooth, that is \(\omega N p/2\pi\): the tooth count again.

Worked example · cruising, 34/17

The rider pedals at 90 rpm in the small ring and the 17-tooth cog. The ratio is \(34/17 = 2\), so the wheel turns at 180 rpm, 3 turns a second. One turn carries the bike \(\pi(0.672) = 2.111\) m. The speed is \(3 \times 2.111 = 6.33\) m/s, or 22.8 km/h.

The chain moves \(34 \times 0.0127 = 0.432\) m per crank turn, so \(0.432 \times 1.5 = 0.648\) m/s. That is a tenth of the bike's speed.

Worked example · the same ratio on the big ring

Shift to 50/25. The ratio is again 2, so the wheel still turns at 180 rpm and the bike still rides at 22.8 km/h. Only the chain changes: 50 links per crank turn instead of 34, so it moves at 0.953 m/s.

The bike's speed depends on the ratio alone. The sprocket sizes set the chain's speed. For the same power, a faster chain pulls with less tension. That is a Unit 6 question.

Worked example · top gear, 50/11

At 100 rpm in 50/11, the wheel turns \(100 \times 50/11 = 454.5\) rpm. The bike moves at \(2.111 \times 454.5/60 = 16.0\) m/s, or 57.6 km/h. The chain moves at \(50 \times 0.0127 \times 100/60 = 1.06\) m/s, a fifteenth of the bike's speed.

The 11-tooth cog's pitch radius is \(12.7/[2\sin(\pi/11)] = 22.5\) mm. The average-radius rule, \(Np/2\pi\), gives 22.2 mm. That 1.4% is the polygon. The chain surges by \(1 - \cos(\pi/11) = 4\%\) at every tooth.

Worked example · climbing, 34/28

At 80 rpm in 34/28, the wheel turns \(80 \times 34/28 = 97.1\) rpm. The bike climbs at \(2.111 \times 97.1/60 = 3.42\) m/s, or 12.3 km/h.

One crank turn now carries the bike \(2.111 \times 34/28 = 2.56\) m. In 50/11 it carries it 9.60 m. The groupset spans a factor of 3.74.

Worked example · the cadence for 30 km/h

Run the chain backwards. In 50/17, 30 km/h is 8.33 m/s. The wheel turns \(8.33/2.111 = 3.95\) times a second, 237 rpm. The crank turns \(17/50\) as fast: 80.5 rpm.

Common mistake

Turning the ratio upside down. The small cog turns faster than the ring, not slower. In 34/17 the wheel turns twice per crank turn, not half.

Common mistake

Using the rim's 622 mm. The road touches the tyre, not the rim. For 34/17 at 90 rpm, 622 mm gives 21.1 km/h instead of 22.8 km/h: 7% slow.

Key

The chain carries one speed from ring to cog. The cog and the wheel share one \(\omega\). The bike's speed is \(\pi D\,(N_f/N_r)\,n_c\), whatever the sprocket sizes.

In the Bike lab, the three scenarios are the first, third and fourth examples. Play it slowly to watch the pedal, the chain and the spokes. The chart shows all 22 gears: a gear is a line through the origin, and its slope is its ratio.

What the model leaves out

The chain's speed is an average; the polygon makes it surge at every tooth. The tyre's diameter is the rim plus twice its width. A real tyre is a little shorter than it is wide, and it flattens under the rider, so it rolls a few millimetres smaller. The model says nothing about torque, the rider's power, or the 2% or so the chain loses.

10Formula sheet

  • \(v = r\omega\), \(a_t = r\alpha\), \(a_n = r\omega^2\). Constant \(\alpha\): \(\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha\Delta\theta\).
  • Open belt, same sense: \(r_1\omega_1 = r_2\omega_2\). External gears flip the sign.
  • Gears: \(r = mN/2\), so \(\omega_2/\omega_1 = N_1/N_2\). A compound train multiplies its stages. An idler's teeth cancel.
  • Chain drive: \(\omega_r/\omega_f = N_f/N_r\); chain speed \(N_f\,p\,n_f\). A wheel on the cog's axle moves its rim at \(\omega_r D/2\).
  • \(\vec v_B = \vec v_A + \omega\times\vec r_{B/A}\).
  • \(\vec a_B = \vec a_A + \alpha\times\vec r - \omega^2\vec r\).
  • The instantaneous centre is where the perpendiculars to two velocities meet. Use it for velocities only.
  • Rolling without slip: contact speed 0, top speed \(2v\), rim acceleration \(v^2/R\) toward the centre. \(a_G = R\alpha\).
  • Slider-crank: \(\sin\phi = (r/\ell)\sin\theta\), and \(x = r\cos\theta + \ell\cos\phi\).
  • Angles on the sliders are in degrees. The derivatives use radians. 1 rpm is \(2\pi/60\) rad/s.
  • A belt may be stated in feet. The speed ratio is the same.
  • Rotating axes: \(\vec a_B = \vec a_A + \dot{\vec\Omega}\times\vec r + \vec\Omega\times(\vec\Omega\times\vec r) + 2\vec\Omega\times\vec v_{rel} + \vec a_{rel}\).

A slipping belt and a wheel that both rolls and slides stay out.
