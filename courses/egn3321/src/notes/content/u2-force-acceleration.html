Part I

Forces on straight paths

01Equations of motion

A free-body diagram comes first. Then \(\sum F = ma\) along each axis. The acceleration is the unknown, or the force is, but not both at once.

\[ \sum F_x = m a_x, \qquad \sum F_y = m a_y. \]

The law holds in an inertial frame, one that does not accelerate. For engineering problems on the ground, the ground is inertial enough. The forces are the ones drawn on the diagram: weight, contact forces, cable forces, springs. \(ma\) is not a force and is never drawn as one.

Worked example · an elevator scale

A 150 lb person stands on a scale in an elevator that accelerates upward at 4 ft/s². The forces on the person are the weight, 150 lb down, and the scale's push \(N\) up. The mass is \(150/32.2 = 4.66\) slug. So \(N - 150 = 4.66 \times 4\), and \(N = 169\) lb. The scale reads 169 lb.

Check with the ratio form: \(N = W(1 + a/g) = 150 \times 1.124 = 169\) lb.

Worked example · a push at an angle

A 25 kg crate is pushed with 200 N directed 30° below the horizontal. \(\mu_k = 0.3\). The push presses the crate into the floor, so \(N = 25(9.81) + 200\sin 30^\circ = 345\) N, not the weight. Friction is \(0.3 \times 345 = 104\) N. The horizontal equation gives \(a = (173.2 - 103.6)/25 = 2.79\) m/s².

Worked example · a hanging accelerometer

A 0.2 kg bob hangs on a string from a car's mirror. The car accelerates forward at 3 m/s². The string tilts back by \(\theta\). Horizontally, \(T\sin\theta = ma\). Vertically, \(T\cos\theta = mg\). Divide: \(\tan\theta = a/g\), so \(\theta = 17.0^\circ\). The tension is \(m\sqrt{g^2 + a^2} = 2.05\) N, more than the bob's weight of 1.96 N. Reading the angle measures the acceleration, whatever the bob's mass.

Two masses on one string over a light pulley make the classic connected system, Atwood's machine.

Why

Two masses on one string share one speed. Their accelerations are equal in size and opposite in direction. Let \(m_2\) descend with acceleration \(a\). Then \(m_2 g - T = m_2 a\) and \(T - m_1 g = m_1 a\). Adding the two equations cancels the tension: \((m_2 - m_1)g = (m_1 + m_2)a\). Put \(a\) back into either equation for \(T\).

\[ a = \frac{m_2 - m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\, g, \qquad T = \frac{2 m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\, g. \]
Worked example · a heavier side

Take \(m_1 = 2\) kg and \(m_2 = 4\) kg, with \(g = 9.81\) m/s². Then \(a = 3.27\) m/s² when the 4 kg mass descends. The tension is 26.2 N. The 4 kg weight is 39.2 N.

From rest, after 1 s, the masses move at 3.27 m/s and have each travelled 1.64 m.

Common mistake

The tension equals the hanging weight only when the acceleration is zero. While the system accelerates, the rope force and the weight differ. Hibbeler §13.3.

In the F = ma lab, the Two masses scenario draws both free bodies. The work–energy check in the readout must agree with the acceleration.

What the model leaves out

The pulley is light and the string does not stretch. Pulley inertia and the mass of the rope would change the acceleration: a heavy pulley needs Unit 6's rotational equation, and a stretching string makes the two accelerations differ while it stretches.

02Friction on an incline

Ask whether the block slides before writing a friction force. At rest, friction is whatever equilibrium needs, up to \(\mu_s N\). Once it slides, friction is \(\mu_k N\) and it opposes the velocity.

Why

Tilt the axes along the slope. Perpendicular to it nothing accelerates, so \(N = mg\cos\theta\). Along it, the downhill part of the weight is \(mg\sin\theta\). The block holds if friction can match that, so the test is \(mg\sin\theta \le \mu_s mg\cos\theta\), or \(\tan\theta \le \mu_s\). The mass cancels.

If the block slides down, kinetic friction acts uphill: \(ma = mg\sin\theta - \mu_k mg\cos\theta\). The mass cancels again.

So the block holds if \(\tan\theta \le \mu_s\). Sliding down, it accelerates at

\[ a = g(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta). \]

The steepest angle that holds is the angle of repose, \(\theta = \arctan\mu_s\). For \(\mu_s = 0.2\) it is 11.3°.

Worked example · a block that slides

A 3 kg block is released on a 30° incline. \(\mu_s = 0.2\) and \(\mu_k = 0.15\). The normal force is 25.5 N. Static friction cannot hold it. The downhill acceleration is 3.63 m/s².

Worked example · a block that holds

The same block on a 10° incline. \(\tan 10^\circ = 0.176\), which is below \(\mu_s = 0.2\), so it holds. The downhill pull is \(3(9.81)\sin 10^\circ = 5.11\) N. Static friction matches it: 5.11 N uphill. The limit \(\mu_s N = 5.80\) N is not reached.

Worked example · launched up the slope

Give the 3 kg block a push up the 30° slope at 4 m/s. Now it moves uphill, so kinetic friction acts downhill, alongside the weight. The deceleration is \(9.81(\sin 30^\circ + 0.15\cos 30^\circ) = 6.18\) m/s². It stops after \(0.647\) s and \(4^2/(2 \times 6.18) = 1.29\) m.

At the stop, friction must be re-tested. \(\tan 30^\circ = 0.577\) exceeds \(\mu_s = 0.2\), so the block does not stay. It slides back down at 3.63 m/s², the case above. The trip up and the trip down have different accelerations because friction flips direction.

Common mistake

Friction is not \(\mu N\) just because a coefficient is printed. At rest, and not on the verge, the friction force is smaller than \(\mu_s N\). On the 10° slope it is 5.11 N, not 5.80 N. Hibbeler §13.4.

Key

Test \(\mu_s\) first. Use \(\mu_k\) only after the block is sliding. While it holds, friction is whatever equilibrium needs.

In the F = ma lab, the Block on a rough incline scenario reports the regime, static or kinetic. Raise the angle past 11.3° and watch it change.

What the model leaves out

Friction here is Coulomb: one static limit, then a constant kinetic value. The model leaves out drag that grows with speed, a coefficient that changes along the surface, and the stick–slip chatter of a real block near the limit.

Another view. MIT OCW 8.01SC (Fall 2016), 6.1 Contact Forces, 6.2 Static Friction Lesson.

03Connected bodies

A block on an incline, tied to a hanging mass, is still one coordinate. Positive means the hanging mass descends. Friction on the block opposes that impending motion, or holds the system if it can.

Why

One light string means one tension. One coordinate means one acceleration. With block mass \(m\) and hanging mass \(M\), the hanging mass gives \(Mg - T = Ma\). If the block slides up, friction acts down the slope, so \(T - mg\sin\theta - \mu_k mg\cos\theta = ma\). Add the two:

\[ a = \frac{Mg - mg\sin\theta - \mu_k mg\cos\theta}{m + M}. \]

Before using it, check that the net drive \(Mg - mg\sin\theta\) beats \(\mu_s mg\cos\theta\). If not, the system holds. If the drive is negative, the block would slide down and pull the hanging mass up. Then friction acts up the slope, every sign in the block's equation flips, and the coordinate should be reversed before solving.

Worked example · a hanging tow

A 4 kg block on a 30° incline is tied to a hanging 6 kg mass. \(\mu_s = 0.3\) and \(\mu_k = 0.2\). The system slides. The acceleration is 3.24 m/s². The tension is 39.4 N. The hanging weight is 58.9 N.

From the formula: \((58.86 - 19.62 - 6.80)/10 = 3.24\) m/s². Then \(T = 6(9.81 - 3.24) = 39.4\) N.

Worked example · a tow that holds

Swap the 6 kg mass for 3 kg. The net drive is \(29.43 - 19.62 = 9.81\) N. The static limit is \(0.3 \times 4(9.81)\cos 30^\circ = 10.19\) N. The limit wins, so nothing moves. The tension equals the hanging weight, 29.4 N, and friction supplies the 9.81 N difference, down the slope.

Common mistake

Using \(\mu_k\) for a system that never started. With the 3 kg mass, the kinetic formula gives \(a = (29.43 - 19.62 - 6.80)/7 = 0.43\) m/s², a motion that does not happen. Test the static limit first. Hibbeler §13.4.

Key

At rest, the tension equals the hanging weight. In motion, \(T = M(g - a)\) when \(a\) is the downward acceleration of that mass.

In the F = ma lab, the Incline tied to a hanging mass scenario shows both free bodies. Lower the hanging mass until the regime turns static.

What the model leaves out

The string is light, so one tension serves both bodies. A heavy rope would make the tension vary along its length. The pulley is frictionless and massless, and the block does not tip.

Another view. MIT OCW 8.01SC (Fall 2016), 7.3 Solving Pulley Systems, 12.5 Worked Example - 2 Blocks and 2 Pulleys; 2.003SC (Fall 2011), Lecture 4, Free Body Diagrams and Fictitious Forces.

04Pulley constraints

When one rope runs through several pulleys, the bodies on it do not move at the same speed. The rope's length is fixed. That one fact ties their positions, then their speeds, then their accelerations.

Why

Measure the block A on the table by \(s_A\), toward the pulley, and the load B by its depth \(s_B\). If B hangs from \(n\) segments of the rope, lowering B by \(ds_B\) lengthens each segment by \(ds_B\). The rope's free end must give up \(n\,ds_B\), so A moves \(n\,ds_B\) toward the pulley.

The length is \(-s_A + n s_B + \text{constant} = L\). Differentiate it once and twice: \(v_A = n v_B\) and \(a_A = n a_B\).

One light rope over light, smooth pulleys carries one tension \(T\). On the block, \(T - f = m_A a_A\). The load hangs from \(n\) segments, so \(m_B g - nT = m_B a_B\). Put \(a_A = n a_B\) into the first and eliminate \(T\).

\[ a_B = \frac{m_B g - n f}{m_B + n^2 m_A}, \qquad T = m_A\,n\,a_B + f. \]

The block counts \(n^2\) times its mass. It moves \(n\) times as far as the load, and its resistance reaches the load multiplied by \(n\). A block and tackle trades speed for force in exactly that ratio.

Worked example · a table-top Atwood

A 4 kg block on a smooth table is tied to a 2 kg load hanging on one segment. Then \(a_B = 2(9.81)/6 = 3.27\) m/s², the same for both bodies, and \(T = 4(3.27) = 13.1\) N, less than the load's 19.6 N weight.

Worked example · a movable pulley

Hang the same 2 kg load from a movable pulley, so two segments hold it. Now the load sees \(2 + 4(4) = 18\) kg, and \(a_B = 19.62/18 = 1.09\) m/s². The block moves at twice that, 2.18 m/s².

The tension falls to \(4(2.18) = 8.72\) N. The load feels \(2T = 17.4\) N up against its 19.6 N weight. Two segments double what one tension does to the load. They do not double the tension.

Worked example · with friction

A 4 kg block on a rough table, \(\mu_k = 0.2\), drags a 6 kg load on two segments. Friction is \(0.2(4)(9.81) = 7.85\) N. Then \(a_B = (58.86 - 2(7.85))/(6 + 16) = 1.96\) m/s², and \(T = 4(3.92) + 7.85 = 23.5\) N.

Worked example · the same drop by energy

Let that load drop 0.5 m from rest. The weight does \(58.86(0.5) = 29.4\) J. Friction takes \(7.85\) N over the block's \(2(0.5) = 1\) m, 7.85 J. The block moves twice as fast as the load, so the kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12(6 + 4\cdot 2^2)v_B^2\).

Then \(11 v_B^2 = 21.6\), and \(v_B = 1.40\) m/s. The kinematics agree: \(\sqrt{2(1.96)(0.5)} = 1.40\) m/s, after 0.714 s.

Worked example · a load the block can hold

A 10 kg block on a table with \(\mu_s = 0.3\) holds a 6 kg load. With one segment the rope must carry the full 58.9 N, more than the 29.4 N static friction can give, and the load falls at 2.15 m/s². Reeve it on three segments. Holding needs only \(58.86/3 = 19.6\) N, and friction holds it.

Worked example · speeds only

The block is pulled toward the pulley at 2 m/s and speeds up at 1 m/s². The load hangs on two segments, so it sinks at \(2/2 = 1\) m/s and speeds up at 0.5 m/s². The constraint alone gives that. No force was needed.

Common mistake

Expecting a movable pulley to double the tension. In the second example the tension falls from 13.1 N to 8.72 N. What doubles is the pull of that one tension on the load, \(2T\). Hibbeler §12.9.

Common mistake

Writing the effective mass as \(m_B + n\,m_A\). The block enters twice: its acceleration is \(n\) times larger, and its force reaches the load \(n\) times larger. For the movable pulley that slip gives 1.96 m/s² instead of 1.09 m/s². Hibbeler §13.4.

In the Pulleys lab, change \(n\) and play the motion. The block's velocity arrow stays \(n\) times the load's, and the readout checks \(v_B\) against work and energy at every instant.

What the model leaves out

The rope is light and does not stretch, and the pulleys are light and frictionless. A pulley with mass needs its own \(\Sigma M = I\alpha\) (Unit 6), and the tension then differs on its two sides. Real tackle also loses a few percent at each sheave to bearing friction.

Another view. MIT OCW 8.01SC (Fall 2016), 12.2 Pulley Problem - Part II, Constraint Condition, 12.5 Worked Example - 2 Blocks and 2 Pulleys.

05Drag

A body moving through air feels a force against its velocity that grows with speed. For a person, a ball or a car it grows as the square of the speed: \(F = \tfrac12\rho C A v^2 = kv^2\), with \(\rho\) the air's density, \(A\) the frontal area and \(C\) a drag coefficient. Sea-level air is 1.225 kg/m³. The weight does not grow, so a falling body levels off at the speed where drag matches it.

Why

Down positive: \(m\,dv/dt = mg - kv^2\). The acceleration vanishes at the terminal speed, \(kv_t^2 = mg\), so \(v_t = \sqrt{mg/k}\).

Write the law as \(dv/dt = g(1 - v^2/v_t^2)\) and separate: \(dv/(1 - v^2/v_t^2) = g\,dt\). The left side integrates to \(v_t \tanh^{-1}(v/v_t)\). From rest, \(v = v_t\tanh(gt/v_t)\). Integrate once more for the distance: \(y = (v_t^2/g)\ln\cosh(gt/v_t)\).

Early on, \(\tanh x \approx x\), so \(v \approx gt\): every body starts at \(g\). Late, \(\tanh x \to 1\). The speed reaches 90% of \(v_t\) at \(gt/v_t = \tanh^{-1}0.9 = 1.47\).

\[ v_t = \sqrt{\frac{mg}{k}}, \qquad v = v_t\tanh\frac{gt}{v_t}, \qquad k = \tfrac12\rho C A. \]

The mass sits under the root. Four times the mass with the same shape doubles \(v_t\). That is why a heavy body of the same size falls faster, and why it falls with a light one for the first moments.

Worked example · one coffee filter

A 1.0 g coffee filter has a drag area \(C\!\cdot\!A = 0.0132\) m², so \(k = 0.5(1.225)(0.0132) = 0.00809\) kg/m. Then \(v_t = \sqrt{0.00981/0.00809} = 1.10\) m/s. It reaches 95% of that in \((1.10/9.81)\tanh^{-1}0.95 = 0.206\) s, after falling only 0.144 m.

Worked example · four filters nested

Nest four filters: four times the mass, the same shape. Then \(v_t = \sqrt{4} \times 1.10 = 2.20\) m/s. Dropped together from 2 m, the stack lands first, although for the first few centimetres the two fall side by side.

Worked example · a skydiver

An 80 kg skydiver falls belly down with \(C\!\cdot\!A = 0.5\) m², so \(k = 0.306\) kg/m and \(v_t = \sqrt{784.8/0.306} = 50.6\) m/s, about 182 km/h. Five seconds after the jump the speed is \(50.6\tanh(0.969) = 37.9\) m/s and the acceleration \(9.81(1 - 0.560) = 4.32\) m/s². The diver reaches 90% of \(v_t\) after 7.60 s and 217 m.

Worked example · a baseball

A 145 g baseball has \(C\!\cdot\!A \approx 0.0015\) m², so \(v_t = 39.3\) m/s. One second after release it moves at 9.61 m/s against 9.81 m/s with no air: drag has hardly started. A steel ball of the same size, eight times as heavy, has \(v_t = 111\) m/s and 9.78 m/s at one second.

Worked example · thrown up

Throw the baseball straight up at 30 m/s. On the way up drag and weight both point down: \(mv\,dv/dy = -(mg + kv^2)\). Separate and integrate from 30 m/s to rest: \(h = (m/2k)\ln(1 + kv_0^2/mg) = 78.9\ln 1.581 = 36.2\) m, against 45.9 m with no air.

Worked example · slow and small

A small bead settling in syrup feels drag that grows as \(v\), not \(v^2\): \(F = bv\). Then \(m\,dv/dt = mg - bv\), \(v_t = mg/b\), and from rest \(v = v_t(1 - e^{-t/\tau})\) with \(\tau = m/b\). A 2 g bead with \(b = 0.04\) kg/s has \(\tau = 0.05\) s and \(v_t = 0.49\) m/s. It reaches 63% of \(v_t\) in one \(\tau\).

Common mistake

Dropping the \(\tfrac12\) in \(\tfrac12\rho C A\). For the skydiver that gives \(v_t = 35.8\) m/s instead of 50.6 m/s. The \(\tfrac12\) is part of the definition of \(C\), not a choice. Hibbeler §13.4.

Common mistake

Taking the acceleration as \(g\) all the way down. With drag it falls as the speed grows: 4.32 m/s² for the skydiver after five seconds, and zero at \(v_t\). Hibbeler §13.4.

In the Drag lab, nest more filters and watch \(v_t\) grow as the root of the mass. The faint outline falls with no air. Play the skydiver and compare the curve with the dashed line \(v = gt\).

What the model leaves out

\(C\) is held constant. Real drag coefficients change with speed (the Reynolds number), and a spinning ball also feels lift. Air thins with height, so a skydiver's \(v_t\) is higher at altitude. Buoyancy, the weight of displaced air, is ignored. For a coffee filter it is under 0.2% of the weight.

Part II adds two things the straight-path problems lacked: a path that bends, and a force that depends on position.

Curves and springs

06Banked curve

The path is a horizontal circle, so \(a_n = v^2/\rho\) points toward the centre and \(a_t = 0\) at constant speed. Resolve the normal force and the friction along the bank, then check the same forces horizontally and vertically.

Why

With no friction the two equations are \(N\sin\theta = mv^2/\rho\) and \(N\cos\theta = mg\). Divide them. The mass cancels, and \(\tan\theta = v^2/(\rho g)\).

Worked example · no friction

A curve of radius 40 m is banked at 20°. With \(\mu = 0\), one speed stays on the road: 11.95 m/s. Any other speed needs friction.

Why

At the top speed, the car is about to slide up and out, so friction \(\mu N\) acts down the bank. Horizontally: \(N\sin\theta + \mu N\cos\theta = mv^2/\rho\). Vertically: \(N\cos\theta - \mu N\sin\theta = mg\). Divide:

\[ v_{\max}^2 = \rho g\,\frac{\sin\theta + \mu\cos\theta}{\cos\theta - \mu\sin\theta}. \]

At the lowest speed friction acts up the bank, and both signs on \(\mu\) flip.

Worked example · a band of speeds

The same 40 m curve at 20°, now with \(\mu = 0.2\). The formula gives \(v_{\max} = 15.4\) m/s and \(v_{\min} = 7.74\) m/s. The no-friction speed, 11.95 m/s, sits inside the band. A 1000 kg car at 12 m/s needs only 27.7 N of friction.

Worked example · a flat curve in feet

A flat curve, \(\theta = 0\), of radius 300 ft. \(\mu_s = 0.6\). Friction alone supplies \(mv^2/\rho\), so \(v_{\max} = \sqrt{\mu\rho g} = \sqrt{0.6 \times 300 \times 32.2} = 76.1\) ft/s. That is 51.9 mph. The car's weight cancels.

Common mistake

The no-friction speed is \(\sqrt{\rho g\tan\theta}\), not \(\sqrt{\rho g\sin\theta}\). The sine belongs in the horizontal equation, and the cosine in the vertical one. Hibbeler §13.5.

Key

Friction down the bank raises the maximum speed. Friction up the bank lowers the minimum. Together they make a band.

In the Curve lab, the Bank with friction scenario draws the band. Move the speed across it and watch friction change sign at the design speed.

What the model leaves out

The road is one horizontal circle, and the car stays on the pavement. The model leaves out a bank that changes along the road, a car that lifts an inside wheel, and the different grip of the front and rear tyres. A car is a rigid body, not a particle, and Unit 6 adds the moments.

Another view. MIT OCW 8.01SC (Fall 2016), 11.2 Worked Example - Car on a Banked Turn, 11.1 Newton's 2nd Law and Circular Motion.

07Vertical loop

Measure \(\phi\) from the bottom. The height above the bottom is \(\rho(1-\cos\phi)\). Energy gives the speed. The normal force, positive toward the centre, is \(N = m(v^2/\rho + g\cos\phi)\).

Why

The height above the bottom is \(\rho(1-\cos\phi)\). A smooth track does no work, so energy turns that height into lost speed: \(v^2 = v_b^2 - 2g\rho(1 - \cos\phi)\). Toward the centre, the track pushes with \(N\), and the weight contributes \(-mg\cos\phi\): at the bottom it points away from the centre, at the top toward it. So \(N - mg\cos\phi = mv^2/\rho\), which rearranges to the formula above.

Worked example · the top of the loop

A 0.5 kg particle enters a smooth loop of radius 0.4 m at 5 m/s. At the top the speed is 3.05 m/s and \(N = 6.73\) N. At the bottom, \(N = 36.2\) N.

Check both with the formula. At the top, \(v^2 = 25 - 4(9.81)(0.4) = 9.30\), so \(N = 0.5(9.304/0.4 - 9.81) = 6.725\) N, which rounds to 6.73 N. At the bottom, \(N = 0.5(25/0.4 + 9.81) = 36.2\) N, more than seven times the weight.

Why

The particle stays on the track while \(N \ge 0\). At the top, \(\cos\phi = -1\), so the condition is \(v_{\text{top}}^2 \ge g\rho\). Energy gives \(v_{\text{top}}^2 = v_b^2 - 4g\rho\). So the entry speed must satisfy \(v_b^2 \ge 5g\rho\).

Worked example · just enough speed

For \(\rho = 0.4\) m, the least entry speed is \(\sqrt{5 \times 9.81 \times 0.4} = 4.43\) m/s. Entering at that speed, the particle reaches the top at \(\sqrt{9.81 \times 0.4} = 1.98\) m/s, and the track pushes on it with almost nothing.

Worked example · falling short

Enter at 4 m/s instead. Set \(N = 0\) with \(v^2 = 16 - 7.848(1 - \cos\phi)\). That gives \(20.38 + 19.62\cos\phi = -9.81\cos\phi\), so \(\cos\phi = -0.692\) and \(\phi = 134^\circ\). The particle leaves the track there, past the side but short of the top, and falls inside the loop as a projectile.

Common mistake

The normal force is not the weight, and it is smallest at the top, not the bottom. At the top the weight points toward the centre, so it helps supply \(mv^2/\rho\). Hibbeler §13.5.

Key

A car on the inside of the track leaves when \(N\) would have to be negative. On a full loop that happens first at the top. The entry speed needs \(v_b^2 \ge 5g\rho\).

In the Curve lab, the Vertical loop scenario reports the regime: on track, leaves, or short. Sweep \(\phi\) and watch \(N\) fall toward the top.

What the model leaves out

The particle is a point on a smooth track. The model leaves out friction, the size of a vehicle, and any normal force that would have to pull on the car. A roller coaster's wheels lock it to the rail from both sides, so it can have negative \(N\). This model cannot.

08Polar equations of motion

Unit 1 found the polar components of the acceleration. Newton's law holds along each of them: the forces along the radial line give \(m a_r\), and the forces across it give \(m a_\theta\). A collar on a turning arm is the standard case: a cord through the pivot pulls along the arm, and the arm pushes across it.

Why

\(\Sigma\vec F = m\vec a\) is one vector equation. Resolve both sides along \(\hat u_r\) and \(\hat u_\theta\). Unit 1, section 6, gave \(a_r = \ddot r - r\dot\theta^2\) and \(a_\theta = r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta\).

\[ \Sigma F_r = m(\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2), \qquad \Sigma F_\theta = m(r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta). \]

On a smooth arm in a horizontal plane the cord is the only radial force, pulling in, and the arm's push \(N\) the only transverse one. So \(-T = m(\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2)\) and \(N = m(r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta)\).

Worked example · a cord drawing a collar in

An arm turns at a steady 4 rad/s. A cord draws a 1 kg collar in at a steady 1 m/s. At 0.5 m from the pivot, \(\ddot r = 0\), so \(T = mr\dot\theta^2 = 1(0.5)(16) = 8\) N.

Across the arm, \(N = m(2\dot r\dot\theta) = 1(2)(-1)(4) = -8\) N. The arm pushes against the turn: the collar arrives with more sideways speed than the arm has at its new radius, and the arm holds it back.

Worked example · letting out on a speeding arm

A 2 kg collar 0.8 m out is let slide at a steady 0.5 m/s while the arm turns at 3 rad/s and speeds up at 2 rad/s². Then \(T = 2(0.8)(9) = 14.4\) N, and \(N = 2[0.8(2) + 2(0.5)(3)] = 9.2\) N, ahead.

Worked example · a free bead

Take the cord away. Nothing pulls along the smooth arm, so \(\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2 = 0\). A bead 0.3 m out on an arm turning at 5 rad/s accelerates outward along it at \(\ddot r = 0.3(25) = 7.5\) m/s², although its radial acceleration in space is zero.

Common mistake

Setting the cord's pull to \(m\ddot r\). A cord moving at a steady speed has \(\ddot r = 0\), yet in the first example it carries 8 N: the radial acceleration is \(\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2\). Hibbeler §13.6.

In the Polar lab, set the arm to the first example: \(r_0 = 0.5\) m, \(\dot r = -1\) m/s, \(\dot\theta = 4\) rad/s. The purple parts of \(\vec a\) are \(-8\) m/s² along the arm and \(-8\) m/s² across it, which is \(T\) and \(N\) divided by the mass.

What the model leaves out

The arm is smooth and horizontal. Friction on the arm would add a radial force proportional to \(N\), and a vertical arm would add a component of the weight to each equation.

09Spring on a collar

A collar on a smooth rod has one axis, along the rod. The spring force is \(kx\), from the unstretched point. The rod's force is the part of the weight perpendicular to the rod. It does no work, and it does not enter \(\sum F = ma\) along the rod.

Why

Along the rod the forces are \(mg\sin\theta\) downhill and \(kx\) toward the unstretched point. Their difference is \(ma\). Perpendicular to the rod nothing accelerates, so the rod cancels \(mg\cos\theta\). That component is the rod force.

\[ a = g\sin\theta - \frac{k}{m}\,x, \qquad N_{\text{rod}} = mg\cos\theta. \]
Worked example · a stretched spring

A 2 kg collar is on a smooth rod at 20° above the horizontal. The spring has \(k = 30\) N/m and is stretched 0.1 m down the rod. The acceleration down the rod is 1.855 m/s². The rod pushes with 18.4 N.

Worked example · where it settles

The acceleration is zero where \(kx = mg\sin\theta\): \(x = 2(9.81)\sin 20^\circ/30 = 0.224\) m. Released at 0.1 m, the collar speeds up down the rod until it passes 0.224 m, then slows. With no friction it oscillates about that point forever.

Worked example · its speed at the rest point

Released from rest at 0.1 m, how fast is the collar at 0.224 m? Integrate \(a\,dx = v\,dv\) from Unit 1 along the rod: \(\tfrac12 v^2 = g\sin\theta\,\Delta x - \tfrac{k}{2m}(x_2^2 - x_1^2)\). That is \(3.355(0.1237) - 7.5(0.0400) = 0.115\) m²/s², so \(v = 0.479\) m/s. The same number comes from Unit 4's work–energy principle, which is this integral with \(m\) multiplied through. By symmetry the collar turns back at \(x = 0.347\) m.

Common mistake

Measuring the stretch from the equilibrium point instead of the unstretched point. The spring force is \(kx\) with \(x\) from where the spring has its natural length. Measured from 0.224 m, the 0.1 m position has \(x = -0.124\) m, and the force comes out with the wrong size. Hibbeler §13.4.

What the model leaves out

The spring lies along the rod, so the stretch equals the coordinate measured down the rod. A spring anchored off the rod stretches by a Pythagorean length, and its force has a component into the rod. This section also leaves out friction. The rod force does no work along the rod.

An application

10Application: the curve of a highway

A highway engineer turns section 6 around. The design speed is set, and so are the bank and the side friction drivers find comfortable. The curve's radius follows. AASHTO's design policy (A Policy on Geometric Design of Highways and Streets, 2011) allows superelevation up to 8% on most rural highways. Its side friction factor falls with speed: 0.14 at 50 mph, 0.12 at 60 mph, 0.10 at 70 mph.

Why

Section 6 found the top speed on a bank with friction \(f\) down the slope: \(v^2 = Rg(\sin\theta + f\cos\theta)/(\cos\theta - f\sin\theta)\). Divide top and bottom by \(\cos\theta\) and write the bank as its slope, \(e = \tan\theta\): \(v^2 = Rg(e + f)/(1 - ef)\).

The product \(ef\) is about 0.01, so AASHTO drops it: \(R = v^2/(g(e + f))\). In US units, with \(V\) in mph and \(R\) in feet, \(v^2/g = (1.4667V)^2/32.2 = V^2/15.0\).

\[ R_{min} = \frac{V^2}{15(e + f)}\quad(\text{mph, ft}), \qquad v^2 = Rg\,\frac{e + f}{1 - ef}\quad(\text{exact}). \]
Worked example · a 60 mph rural highway

With \(e = 0.08\) and AASHTO's \(f = 0.12\), \(R = 60^2/(15 \times 0.20) = 1200\) ft, 366 m. The exact balance on that curve allows 60.2 mph before the car needs all of \(f\): the dropped \(ef\) term is a small margin.

Worked example · an interstate at 70 mph

At 70 mph AASHTO allows only \(f = 0.10\). Then \(R = 4900/(15 \times 0.18) = 1815\) ft. The speed is a sixth higher, and the curve half again as wide.

Worked example · a gentler bank at 50 mph

A 50 mph road banked at only 4% has \(e + f = 0.04 + 0.14 = 0.18\), so \(R = 2500/2.7 = 926\) ft. At 8% it could have been 758 ft.

Worked example · the same curve, half the bank

Keep the 1,200 ft curve built for 60 mph, but bank it at 4%. At 60 mph, \(v^2/gR = 26.82^2/(9.81 \times 365.8) = 0.200\). The car needs \(f = (0.200 - 0.04)/(1 + 0.04 \times 0.200) = 0.159\), a third more than the design's 0.12.

Worked example · a driver over the limit

Take the 60 mph curve, banked at 8%, at 75 mph. Now \(v^2/gR = 0.313\), and the car needs \(f = (0.313 - 0.08)/(1 + 0.08 \times 0.313) = 0.228\). That is nearly twice the design value. Dry tyres can still supply it, which is why most such drivers get round; wet ones may not.

Common mistake

Leaving out the bank: \(R = V^2/(15f)\). At 60 mph that gives 2,000 ft instead of 1,200. The superelevation carries two fifths of the sideways push. Hibbeler §13.5.

Common mistake

Mixing units in \(v^2/(g(e + f))\): the speed in mph or the radius in feet with \(g\) in m/s². The 15 in AASHTO's formula is \(g\) and the mph-to-ft/s factor together. Use it only with mph and feet. Hibbeler §13.5.

In the Highway lab, raise the design speed and watch the radius curve climb. Then lower the bank and drive the old curve: the friction readout turns red.

What the model leaves out

The car is a point, so load shifting onto the outer tyres is left out, and so is the spiral that eases a real curve in from the straight. AASHTO's friction factor is a comfort limit, not what tyres can grip. Snow and ice also cap \(e\) in cold regions, so a car crawling round does not slide inward.

11Formula sheet

  • \(\sum \vec F = m\vec a\). Draw the free-body before writing a component.
  • Atwood: \(a = (m_2-m_1)g/(m_1+m_2)\), \(T = 2 m_1 m_2 g/(m_1+m_2)\).
  • Incline: holds if \(\tan\theta \le \mu_s\). Sliding down: \(a = g\sin\theta - \mu_k g\cos\theta\).
  • Tow: \(a = (Mg - mg\sin\theta - \mu_k mg\cos\theta)/(m + M)\), after the static test.
  • Kinematics in this unit uses \(g = 9.81\) m/s², or \(32.2\) ft/s². The slug is the US mass in \(F = ma\).
  • Bank, no friction: \(v = \sqrt{\rho g\tan\theta}\). With friction: \(v_{\max}^2 = \rho g(\sin\theta + \mu\cos\theta)/(\cos\theta - \mu\sin\theta)\).
  • Flat curve: \(v_{\max} = \sqrt{\mu\rho g}\).
  • Loop, \(\phi\) from the bottom: \(v^2 = v_b^2 - 2g\rho(1-\cos\phi)\), \(N = m(v^2/\rho + g\cos\phi)\). Full loop: \(v_b^2 \ge 5g\rho\).
  • Collar: \(a = g\sin\theta - (k/m)x\) along the rod. Rest point \(x = mg\sin\theta/k\).
  • Pulley constraint: \(-s_A + n s_B = L\), so \(v_A = n v_B\). Load on \(n\) segments: \(a_B = (m_B g - nf)/(m_B + n^2 m_A)\).
  • Drag \(kv^2\), \(k = \tfrac12\rho C A\): \(v_t = \sqrt{mg/k}\), \(v = v_t\tanh(gt/v_t)\). Drag \(bv\): \(v_t = mg/b\), \(\tau = m/b\).
  • Polar: \(\Sigma F_r = m(\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2)\), \(\Sigma F_\theta = m(r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta)\).
  • Highway curve: \(v^2 = Rg(e + f)/(1 - ef)\). AASHTO: \(R = V^2/(15(e + f))\), mph and ft.

Left out of this course: central-force motion (§13.7). Polar coordinates (§13.6), dependent motion (§12.9) and drag are taught above.
