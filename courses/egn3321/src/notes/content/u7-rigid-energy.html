Rigid-body energy

Work and energy for a rigid body in the plane. Translation of the mass centre and spin about that centre make up T. A one-degree linkage can write the same T about the instantaneous centre. Each formula below is derived by adding up the particles of Unit 3, and the numbers are recomputed.

01Kinetic energy

A rigid body in the plane carries kinetic energy in two parts. The mass centre translates. The body spins about that centre.

Why

Add up \(\tfrac12 v^2\,dm\) over the body. Each element's velocity is \(\vec v = \vec v_G + \omega\times\vec\rho\), with \(\vec\rho\) from the mass centre. Squaring gives three terms: \(v_G^2\), \(2\vec v_G\cdot(\omega\times\vec\rho)\), and \(\omega^2\rho^2\). The middle term integrates to zero, because \(\int\vec\rho\,dm = 0\) about the mass centre. The first gives \(\tfrac12 mv_G^2\). The last gives \(\tfrac12 I_G\omega^2\).

The mass centre moves as a particle and contributes \(\tfrac{1}{2}mv_G^2\). Spin about the centre contributes \(\tfrac{1}{2}I_G\omega^2\). Rolling without slip ties them by \(\omega = v_G/R\).

\[ T = \tfrac{1}{2}mv_G^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}I_G\omega^2 \]

\(v_G\) is the speed of the mass centre. \(\omega\) is the angular speed. \(I_G\) is the moment about the centre.

About a point P that is momentarily at rest, such as a fixed pin or the instantaneous centre, \(v_G = \omega d\), with \(d\) the distance from P to G. Then \(T = \tfrac12(md^2 + I_G)\omega^2 = \tfrac12 I_P\omega^2\), by the parallel-axis theorem. The two forms are one quantity.

Worked example · the same drop

A hoop, a disk, and a sphere roll 2 m down a 30° incline. They start from rest. The finish speeds are 3.13 m/s for the hoop, 3.62 m/s for the disk, and 3.74 m/s for the sphere. Each body of mass 2 kg gains the same kinetic energy, 19.62 J.

What differs is the split. The share in rotation is \(k/(1 + k)\): half for the hoop, a third for the disk, and 2/7 for the sphere. The more energy spinning, the less left for the centre's speed.

Worked example · a rolling disk

A disk of mass 2 kg and radius 0.25 m rolls at 3 m/s. Translation holds 9 J. Rotation holds 4.5 J. The total is 13.5 J.

Worked example · a car's wheels

A car moves at 20 m/s on wheels of radius 0.3 m. Treat each 15 kg wheel as a disk, so \(I_G = 0.675\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}\) and \(\omega = 66.7\) rad/s. Each wheel carries 3000 J in translation and 1500 J in spin. Braking must remove both, so the wheels act heavier than they weigh.

Worked example · a storage flywheel

A 50 kg steel disk of radius 0.3 m spins at 3000 rpm, which is 314 rad/s, about a fixed axis. Its centre does not move. \(I = 2.25\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}\), and \(T = \tfrac12(2.25)(314^2) = 111\) kJ, all of it rotational.

Common mistake

Reporting only \(\tfrac{1}{2}I_G\omega^2\) drops the translation of the mass centre. Both terms belong in \(T\). Hibbeler §18.1.

Key

For rolling without slip, \(\omega = v_G/R\). The same \(T\) is \(\tfrac{1}{2}I_C\omega^2\) about the contact.

In the Race lab, the readout splits each body's energy into translation and spin at the finish line.

What the model leaves out

Energy uses the speed of the mass centre and the spin about that centre. It does not give a friction force or a pin force. Those still need the force and moment equations of Unit 6. The bodies are rigid, so no energy goes into flexing or vibrating.

02Work of a couple

A couple does work through the angle it turns. The angle is in radians.

Why

A couple is two equal, opposite forces \(F\) a distance \(r\) apart. Turn the body by \(d\theta\) about a point midway. Each force's point of application moves \((r/2)\,d\theta\) along its own line, so each does \(F(r/2)\,d\theta\). The pair does \(Fr\,d\theta = M\,d\theta\). The same holds about any point.

For a torsional spring, \(M = -\kappa(\theta-\theta_0)\). Its work is the drop in \(\tfrac{1}{2}\kappa(\theta-\theta_0)^2\), just as a linear spring's work is the drop in \(\tfrac12 ks^2\).

\[ U = \int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2} M\,d\theta \]

A torsional spring has \(M = -\kappa(\theta - \theta_0)\). Between two angles its work equals the drop in \(\tfrac{1}{2}\kappa(\theta-\theta_0)^2\). The rate of work of a couple is its power: \(P = M\omega\).

Worked example · a bar released from horizontal

A 4 kg bar, 2 m long, is pinned at one end and released from rest while horizontal. At the bottom, \(\omega = 3.84\) rad/s and \(v_G = 3.84\) m/s. They match because the centre is 1 m from the pin. The kinetic energy is \(mgL/2\), which reads 39.2 J.

About the pin, \(T = \tfrac12(mL^2/3)\omega^2\). Setting it equal to \(mgL/2\) gives \(\omega^2 = 3g/L\). Unit 6 used this \(\omega\) to find the pin force at the bottom.

Worked example · a torsional spring

The same bar has a torsional spring of stiffness 15 N·m/rad, unstretched when the bar hangs down. From horizontal to the bottom the spring does 18.51 J of work. \(\omega = 4.65\ \mathrm{rad/s}\), and \(T = 57.7\ \mathrm{J}\).

The spring's work is \(\tfrac12(15)(\pi/2)^2 = 18.51\) J. Using 90 for the angle instead of \(\pi/2\) would give 60,750 J.

Worked example · a winch lifts a load

A drum of radius 0.2 m and moment 0.4 kg·m² winds a cable that lifts a 20 kg load from rest. A motor applies a steady 60 N·m. After the drum turns 10 rad, the load has risen 2 m. The motor's couple has done 600 J, and gravity −392 J.

The drum's \(T\) is \(0.2\omega^2\), and the load's is \(\tfrac12(20)(0.2\omega)^2 = 0.4\omega^2\). So \(0.6\omega^2 = 208\) J, \(\omega = 18.6\) rad/s, and the load rises at 3.72 m/s. The cable's tension is internal and does no net work. At that instant the motor delivers \(M\omega = 60 \times 18.6 = 1116\) W, while lifting the load takes only \(196 \times 3.72 = 730\) W. The other 386 W is still speeding up the drum and the load.

Worked example · a wrench over three turns

A steady 50 lb·ft torque turns a shaft through 3 revolutions. The angle is \(3 \times 2\pi = 18.85\) rad, so the work is \(50 \times 18.85 = 942\) ft·lb. Revolutions must become radians before multiplying.

Common mistake

The work of a couple is \(\int M\,d\theta\). A force times an arc length is a different job, and it misses this moment. Hibbeler §18.3.

Key

Unstretched at the bottom, the spring hands its stored energy to the bar. The bar arrives faster than it would under gravity alone.

In the Linkage lab, the bar scenario sets \(\kappa = 0\), and the couple scenario adds the spring. The readout lists each work term.

What the model leaves out

The angle in a couple is in radians. The model leaves out a spring that hits a stop, and a moment that depends on the speed, such as a motor whose torque falls as it speeds up. Bearing friction would add a small negative couple to every swing.

03Rolling without slip

A hoop, a disk, and a sphere roll from rest down the same incline. Write \(k = I_G/(mR^2)\). Energy gives the finish speed, and mass and radius cancel.

\[ v = \sqrt{\frac{2gs\sin\theta}{1+k}} \]
Why

Rolling without slip does no work at the contact: the contact point is momentarily at rest, so the friction there moves through no distance. Only gravity works. The centre drops \(s\sin\theta\), so \(mg s\sin\theta = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}I_G\omega^2\). Rolling sets \(\omega = v/R\) and \(I_G = kmR^2\). Cancel \(m\). The denominator is \(1+k\).

Worked example · a sphere finishes first

On a 2 m run at 30°, the times are 1.068 s for the sphere, 1.106 s for the disk, and 1.277 s for the hoop. The sphere has \(k = 2/5\), the smallest of the three.

Worked example · a sphere in feet

A sphere rolls 10 ft from rest down a 30° incline. With \(g = 32.2\ \mathrm{ft/s^2}\), the speed is 15.17 ft/s. The weight cancels.

Worked example · rolling up a ramp

A disk rolls at 3 m/s onto a 30° ramp. Its total energy is \(\tfrac12 mv^2(1 + k) = 6.75m\) J. It climbs until that is all potential: \(mg\,s\sin 30^\circ = 6.75m\), so \(s = 1.38\) m. A frictionless block at the same speed climbs only 0.917 m. The disk's spin carries it farther.

Worked example · a falling spool

A disk is wrapped with string, the free end tied above, and released. It unwinds as it falls, with the string's contact point at rest, so it obeys the same rule with \(g\) in place of \(g\sin\theta\). After a 1 m drop, \(v = \sqrt{2g/(1 + 1/2)} = 3.62\) m/s. Unit 6's force equation then gives the string tension: one third of the weight.

Worked example · a block beats them all

Race a frictionless block down the same 2 m at 30°. It has no spin to feed, so \(k = 0\), and it arrives at \(\sqrt{2(9.81)(1)} = 4.43\) m/s. The sphere arrives at 3.74 m/s. Every rolling body loses this race to a sliding one, because rolling stores energy in spin.

Common mistake

Putting \(k_G\) in the denominator counts the radius of gyration once. The factor is \(k_G^2/R^2\). Mass cancels, so only \(k\) orders the race. Hibbeler §18.5.

Key

Hoop \(k = 1\), disk \(k = 1/2\), solid sphere \(k = 2/5\). The sphere finishes first.

In the Race lab, change the mass or the radius. The finish order and the finish times do not move. Change the shape and they do.

What the model leaves out

The race assumes rolling without slip for the whole run. If friction cannot supply the torque, the body slips and this speed formula does not apply: kinetic friction then works, and energy is lost. Mass cancels only while \(k\) is fixed by the shape. Rolling resistance, which slowly drains a real wheel, is left out.

04The instantaneous centre

A rod with one end in a horizontal slot and one end in a vertical slot has a single degree of freedom. At each angle the instantaneous centre is the corner opposite the rod.

Why

The foot moves horizontally, so the centre of rotation lies on the vertical through the foot. The top moves vertically, so it also lies on the horizontal through the top. That corner is the instantaneous centre. \(I_{IC} = I_G + m(L/2)^2 = mL^2/3\), and \(T = \tfrac{1}{2}I_{IC}\omega^2\).

The corner is always \(L/2\) from the rod's mid-point, the half-diagonal of the rectangle the rod spans. So \(I_{IC}\) does not change with the angle, and energy gives \(\omega\) in one line: \(mg(L/2)(\sin\theta_1 - \sin\theta_2) = \tfrac12(mL^2/3)\omega^2\).

\[ \omega^2 = \frac{3g(\sin\theta_1 - \sin\theta_2)}{L} \]
Worked example · a rod in two slots

A 3 kg rod, 1.5 m long, is released from rest at 60° from the floor. At 30°, \(\omega = 2.68\ \mathrm{rad/s}\) and \(v_G = 2.01\ \mathrm{m/s}\). \(I_{IC} = mL^2/3 = 2.25\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}\).

The formula gives \(\omega^2 = 3(9.81)(0.866 - 0.5)/1.5 = 7.18\), so \(\omega = 2.68\) rad/s. The centre is \(L/2 = 0.75\) m from the corner, so \(v_G = 2.01\) m/s.

Worked example · both ends of the rod

At 30° the foot is \(L\sin 30^\circ = 0.75\) m below the corner and the top is \(L\cos 30^\circ = 1.30\) m beside it. So the foot moves at \(2.68 \times 0.75 = 2.01\) m/s and the top at \(2.68 \times 1.30 = 3.48\) m/s. Unit 5 found the same geometry for a ladder.

Worked example · a spring at the foot

The same rod has a spring at the foot. The natural length equals the rod, so the spring starts compressed. At 30°, \(\omega = 4.06\ \mathrm{rad/s}\) and \(T = 18.52\ \mathrm{J}\).

Here \(k = 40\) N/m, and the foot sits \(L\cos\theta\) from the corner. At 60° the spring is compressed 0.75 m. At 30° it is compressed 0.201 m. It gives back \(\tfrac12(40)(0.75^2 - 0.201^2) = 10.44\) J. Gravity adds \(3(9.81)(0.75)(0.866 - 0.5) = 8.08\) J. Then \(\tfrac12(2.25)\omega^2 = 18.52\) J gives the 4.06 rad/s.

Worked example · α without moments

Energy also gives the angular acceleration. Differentiate \(\omega^2 = 3g(\sin\theta_1 - \sin\theta)/L\) in time, with \(\dot\theta = -\omega\) as the rod falls. That gives \(2\omega\alpha = 3g\cos\theta\,\omega/L\), so \(\alpha = 3g\cos\theta/(2L)\). At 30°, \(\alpha = 8.50\) rad/s². No moment equation about the moving centre was needed, so the trap in the next box never opens.

Common mistake

\(T = \tfrac{1}{2}I_{IC}\omega^2\) is an identity at that instant. \(\Sigma M_{IC} = I_{IC}\alpha\) is not, because the instantaneous centre moves. Integrate the energy, or the real one-degree equation. Hibbeler §18.1.

Key

Use the instantaneous centre for \(T\). Keep the moment equation on a fixed point, or on the mass centre.

In the Linkage lab, the slots scenario follows the rod down. The readout's \(\omega\) matches the formula at every angle.

What the model leaves out

Kinetic energy about the instantaneous centre is valid at that instant. A moment sum about that moving point is not. The slots are frictionless, and the rod stays in both of them. A real rod sliding down a wall can leave the wall before the floor, when the wall's push would have to become a pull. That separation needs the force equations, and the course leaves it out.

05Conservation of energy

When the only forces that do work are conservative, the total \(T + V\) of a system stays fixed. For rigid bodies, the list of forces that do no work at all is long, and that is what makes energy so useful here.

Why

The principle of work and energy, \(T_1 + \sum U_{1-2} = T_2\), holds for each body. Split the work into a conservative part, \(V_1 - V_2\), and the rest. If the rest is zero, \(T_1 + V_1 = T_2 + V_2\).

These forces do no work, so they never enter \(\sum U\):

  • a pin force on a body turning about that fixed pin, since its point of application does not move;
  • friction and the normal force at a contact that rolls without slip, since the contact point is at rest;
  • a smooth surface's normal force, perpendicular to the motion;
  • the tension in an inextensible cable between two bodies of one system, which does equal and opposite work on its two ends.
\[ T_1 + V_1 = T_2 + V_2, \]

with the potential of the weight, a linear spring, and a torsional spring:

\[ V = mgy_G + \tfrac12 ks^2 + \tfrac12\kappa\theta^2. \]
Worked example · a pulley with mass

A cord wrapped on a 4 kg disk pulley of radius 0.2 m holds a 2 kg mass. The pulley turns on a fixed pin. Released from rest, how fast is the mass after falling 1.5 m? The pin and the cord do no net work. So \(2(9.81)(1.5) = \tfrac12(2)v^2 + \tfrac12(0.08)(v/0.2)^2\). Both terms on the right are \(v^2\), so \(2v^2 = 29.4\) and \(v = 3.84\) m/s.

A massless pulley would give \(\sqrt{2gh} = 5.42\) m/s. The pulley's spin takes half the energy. The mass falls at \(g/2\), so the cord tension is \(m(g - g/2) = 9.81\) N.

Worked example · a pendulum with a bob

Unit 6's pendulum, a 2 kg rod with a 1 kg sphere at its end, has \(I_O = 1.88\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}\). Its mass centre is \((2 \times 0.5 + 1 \times 1.1)/3 = 0.7\) m from the pin. Released from horizontal, it drops that 0.7 m by the bottom. So \(3(9.81)(0.7) = \tfrac12(1.88)\omega^2\), and \(\omega = 4.68\) rad/s.

Worked example · a wheel down a hill in feet

A 64.4 lb wheel, 2 slug, has a 1 ft radius and a radius of gyration of 0.75 ft. It rolls from rest down a 15 ft drop. Then \(k = 0.75^2/1^2 = 0.5625\), and \(v = \sqrt{2(32.2)(15)/1.5625} = 24.9\) ft/s. The mass is not needed: only the shape enters.

The rate form of the principle is power. A force does work at \(\vec F \cdot \vec v\), and a couple at \(M\omega\). Summed over a body, the power in equals the rate of change of \(T\).

Worked example · a motor at constant power

A motor delivers a steady 500 W to a flywheel with \(I = 2\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}\). Speeding it from 10 to 50 rad/s raises \(T\) by \(\tfrac12(2)(50^2 - 10^2) = 2400\) J. That takes \(2400/500 = 4.8\) s. The torque falls as the speed rises, from 50 N·m to 10 N·m, because \(M = P/\omega\).

Common mistake

Counting work for friction at a rolling contact, or for a pin on a fixed axis. Neither point moves, so neither force does work. Only slipping friction, or a pin that moves, enters \(\sum U\). Hibbeler §18.5.

Key

List the forces. Strike out those that do no work. If only gravity and springs remain, \(T_1 + V_1 = T_2 + V_2\), and the speed follows in one line.

What the model leaves out

Energy gives speeds at positions, not times, and not forces. The pulley's cord tension above came from a separate force equation. Every "no work" item depends on its constraint holding: a slipping wheel, a stretching cable, or a pin with friction turns a non-working force into a lossy one.

06Formula sheet

  • Hoop \(k = 1\). Disk \(k = 1/2\). Solid sphere \(k = 2/5\). Here \(k = I_G/(mR^2) = k_G^2/R^2\).
  • Rolling from rest: \(v = \sqrt{2gs\sin\theta/(1+k)}\). Rotational share: \(k/(1 + k)\).
  • \(T = \tfrac{1}{2}mv_G^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}I_G\omega^2\). About the contact, or about a fixed pin, \(T = \tfrac{1}{2}I\omega^2\).
  • Uniform bar, pinned, released from horizontal: \(\omega = \sqrt{3g/L}\) at the bottom, and \(v_G = \omega L/2\).
  • Couple: \(U = \int M\,d\theta\), \(P = M\omega\).
  • Torsional spring, unstretched at \(\theta_0\): \(U = \tfrac{1}{2}\kappa\big((\theta_1-\theta_0)^2 - (\theta_2-\theta_0)^2\big)\), with the angles in radians.
  • Rod in two slots: \(I_{IC} = mL^2/3\), \(T = \tfrac{1}{2}I_{IC}\omega^2\), and \(\omega^2 = 3g(\sin\theta_1 - \sin\theta_2)/L\).
  • \(g = 9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\). A US problem uses \(g = 32.2\ \mathrm{ft/s^2}\).

The formulas assume the constraint holds for the whole motion. Slip, bearing friction, and a spring that bottoms out are left out.
