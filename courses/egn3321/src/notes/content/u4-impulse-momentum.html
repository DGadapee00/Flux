Impulse and momentum

Impulse is the area under a force. A collision conserves momentum for every restitution. A rocket's gain follows the mass ratio. Each of these is \(\sum F = ma\) integrated over time, and each is derived below. The numbers are recomputed.

01Principle of linear impulse and momentum

The impulse of a force is the area under its graph of force against time. That area equals the change in momentum.

Why

Start from \(F = ma\), with \(a = dv/dt\). Multiply by \(dt\) and integrate both sides over time. The left side is the area. The right side is \(mv_2 - mv_1\). In vector form each component works the same way: \(\int \vec F\,dt = m\vec v_2 - m\vec v_1\).

\[ \int F\,dt = m\Delta v. \]

Impulse and momentum integrate the force over time. Work and energy, in Unit 4, integrate it over distance. Use impulse when the time is given or asked for, or when the force is a short, sharp blow whose distance is unknown.

Worked example · equal areas

A 2 kg particle starts at rest. A 100 N force acts for 0.4 s. Then 40 N acts for 1 s. Each area is 40 N·s. The speed becomes 40 m/s.

Worked example · a short pulse

The same mass starts at rest. A 400 N force acts for 0.1 s, which is still 40 N·s. Then 20 N acts for 1 s and adds 20 N·s. The speed becomes 30 m/s.

Worked example · a force that ramps up

A force \(F = 20t\) N acts on a 2 kg body at rest for 3 s. The impulse is the triangle under the graph: \(\int_0^3 20t\,dt = 10t^2\big|_0^3 = 90\) N·s. The speed becomes 45 m/s. A constant force with the same peak, 60 N, would have given twice that.

The average force over a contact is the impulse divided by the contact time. Directions matter: a rebound reverses the velocity, so the change is the sum of the two speeds.

Worked example · a ball off a wall

A 0.15 kg ball hits a wall at 20 m/s and rebounds at 15 m/s. Take the rebound as positive. The change in velocity is \(15 - (-20) = 35\) m/s, so the impulse is \(0.15 \times 35 = 5.25\) N·s. Over a 5 ms contact the average force is 1050 N, about 700 times the ball's weight.

Worked example · a bat in feet

A 0.32 lb baseball arrives at 90 mph and leaves the bat at 110 mph the other way. Its mass is \(0.32/32.2 = 0.00994\) slug. The velocity change is 200 mph, or 293 ft/s. The impulse is 2.92 lb·s. Over a 0.7 ms contact, the average force is 4160 lb.

Common mistake

The impulse is the area, not the height of the force. A short spike and a long push with the same area give the same change in speed. And in a rebound the speeds add: writing \(20 - 15\) instead of \(15 + 20\) undercounts the impulse sevenfold. Hibbeler §15.1.

Key

Add the areas. Divide the total by the mass. Add that change to the starting velocity, with its sign.

In the Impulse lab, reshape the force pulses while keeping their areas. The final speed does not move.

What the model leaves out

The body is a particle, and \(F\) is the net force along the line. The model leaves out a force missing from the area, and any rotation of a finite body. During a 5 ms blow, the weight's impulse is only 0.007 N·s, so non-impulsive forces like it are usually dropped. That is a choice, and it fails for long contacts.

Another view. MIT OCW 8.01SC (Fall 2016), 15.1 Momentum and Impulse, 15.3 Worked Example - Bouncing Ball.

02Conservation of momentum

Internal impulses between parts of a system cancel in pairs. If the external impulse is zero, the total momentum stays constant.

Why

Write the impulse equation for each body and add them. By Newton's third law, the force of A on B and of B on A are equal and opposite at every instant, so their impulses cancel. What is left is the external impulse on the system. If that is zero, the total momentum at the end equals the total at the start.

Worked example · a gun recoil

A 0.02 kg bullet leaves a 4 kg gun at 400 m/s. The gun was at rest. The recoil speed is 2 m/s. The two momenta sum to zero.

Kinetic energy is not conserved. The bullet carries 1600 J and the gun 8 J. The 1608 J came from the powder. Momentum shares the impulse equally. Energy goes mostly to the lighter body.

Worked example · a jump from a boat

A 70 kg person stands in a 30 kg boat at rest. The person jumps off the back at 2 m/s horizontally. The water's horizontal force is small over the jump, so momentum is kept: \(70(2) = 30v\), and the boat moves forward at 4.67 m/s. The lighter boat gets the larger speed.

Worked example · a block slides onto a cart

A 5 kg block slides at 4 m/s onto a 20 kg cart at rest on smooth wheels. Friction between them, with \(\mu_k = 0.3\), is internal to the pair, so their total momentum is kept. They end at a common speed: \(5(4) = 25v\), so \(v = 0.8\) m/s.

Impulse on each body separately gives the time. Friction is \(0.3(5)(9.81) = 14.7\) N. It slows the block at 2.94 m/s² and speeds the cart at 0.736 m/s². They match after \(4/(2.94 + 0.736) = 1.09\) s. Kinetic energy falls from 40 J to 8 J. The missing 32 J is friction times the 2.17 m the block slid relative to the cart.

Common mistake

Do not drop an external impulse and still claim conservation. Internal impulses cancel. An outside force acting over a time does not. A sprinter pushing off the ground is not a recoil problem: the ground's push is external. Hibbeler §15.3.

Key

For two bodies with no outside impulse, \(m_1 u_1 + m_2 u_2 = m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2\). Conservation can hold in one direction and fail in another.

What the model leaves out

Internal impulses cancel in pairs. An outside force that acts through the interval does not. Recoil here also leaves out momentum carried off by the gas, which is a few percent for a rifle. The boat ignores water drag during the jump.

Another view. MIT OCW 8.01SC (Fall 2016), 16.1 Conservation of Momentum, 18.2 Set up a Recoil Problem.

03Impact

The line of impact is the normal. Restitution sets the separation speed along that line. Momentum along the line is conserved for every value of \(e\).

Why

The collision is short, so the outside impulse is left out and momentum along the line is kept. Restitution sets the separation speed. Kinetic energy returns in full only when \(e = 1\).

Solving the two equations together gives each final velocity. With \(M = m_1 + m_2\) and \(w = u_1 - u_2\), the closing speed:

\[ \begin{aligned} v_1 &= \frac{m_1 u_1 + m_2 u_2 - m_2 e w}{M},\\ v_2 &= \frac{m_1 u_1 + m_2 u_2 + m_1 e w}{M}. \end{aligned} \]
\[ v_{n2} - v_{n1} = e(u_{n1} - u_{n2}). \]
Worked example · a direct collision

Two 1 kg bodies meet along a line. One moves at 4 m/s. The other is at rest. \(e = 0.5\). They leave at 1 m/s and 3 m/s. The kinetic energy lost is 3 J.

Worked example · they stick

A 1 kg body at 8 m/s hits a 3 kg body at rest. \(e = 0\). Both leave at 2 m/s. The collision loses 24 J.

Worked example · a head-on meeting

A 2 kg body at 3 m/s meets a 1 kg body coming the other way at 1 m/s. \(e = 0.8\). The closing speed is 4 m/s. The formulas give \(v_1 = (5 - 3.2)/3 = 0.6\) m/s and \(v_2 = (5 + 6.4)/3 = 3.8\) m/s. Momentum before and after is 5 kg·m/s. The separation speed is 3.2 m/s, which is 0.8 of 4.

Why

The energy lost has a closed form. Work in the frame moving with the centre of mass, where the total momentum is zero. There each body's speed is scaled by \(e\), so the kinetic energy is scaled by \(e^2\). The loss is

\[ \Delta T = \tfrac12\,\frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,(1 - e^2)\,w^2. \]

For the head-on case, \(\tfrac12(2/3)(0.36)(16) = 1.92\) J. For the two examples above it gives 3 J and 24 J again.

Two special cases follow from the formulas. Equal masses with \(e = 1\) exchange velocities: \(v_1 = u_2\) and \(v_2 = u_1\). That is why a row of steel balls passes one swing down the line. With \(e = 0\), both leave at the centre-of-mass speed, \((m_1 u_1 + m_2 u_2)/M\).

Restitution can be measured with a drop. A ball dropped from 2 m reaches the floor at 6.26 m/s. If it bounces to 1.28 m, it left at 5.01 m/s. The floor does not move, so \(e = 5.01/6.26 = 0.8\). In general, \(e = \sqrt{h_2/h_1}\).

Common mistake

Momentum is conserved for every coefficient of restitution. Kinetic energy is conserved only when \(e = 1\). Hibbeler §15.4.

Key

Solve the momentum equation and the restitution equation together. The energy loss follows from \(e\), the reduced mass, and the closing speed.

In the Impact lab, slide \(e\) from 0 to 1. The final velocities move, the total momentum does not, and the lost energy falls to zero.

What the model leaves out

The impact is treated as instantaneous, so outside impulses during the collision are neglected. Deformation of the bodies is summed up by \(e\), not drawn. Real \(e\) depends on the impact speed and the temperature. A single number is a fit, not a law.

Another view. MIT OCW 8.01SC (Fall 2016), 26.1 Momentum in Collisions, 27.2 Relative Velocity in 1D.

04Oblique impact

Split each velocity into a normal part and a tangent part. The normal parts follow the direct-impact equations. The tangent parts stay as they were.

Why

A smooth face has no tangent impulse, so each tangent velocity is unchanged. The normal velocities are a direct impact, with the same \(e\) and the same momentum balance.

Worked example · the tangent is unchanged

Two 2 kg bodies collide. One approaches at 4 m/s, 30° from the normal. The other is at rest on the normal. \(e = 1\). The tangent part stays 2 m/s. The normal parts exchange, so the second body leaves at 3.46 m/s.

Worked example · a bounce off a smooth floor

A ball hits a smooth floor at 10 m/s, 30° from the vertical. \(e = 0.6\). The normal part, 8.66 m/s, comes back at \(0.6 \times 8.66 = 5.20\) m/s. The tangent part, 5 m/s, is unchanged. The ball leaves at \(\sqrt{5.20^2 + 5^2} = 7.21\) m/s, at 43.9° from the vertical. It comes off flatter than it went in.

Worked example · two pucks, partly elastic

Puck A, 1 kg, slides at 3 m/s and strikes puck B, 1 kg and at rest, with A's velocity 45° from the line of centres. \(e = 0.5\). Along the line, A brings 2.12 m/s. The direct-impact formulas give A 0.530 m/s and B 1.59 m/s along the line. A keeps its 2.12 m/s tangent part, so it leaves at 2.19 m/s, 76° from the line. B leaves along the line. The two paths are no longer at right angles, as they would be with \(e = 1\).

Common mistake

Do not multiply the tangent velocity by \(e\). Restitution acts only along the line of impact. Scaling the whole velocity by 0.6 would send the floor ball off at 6 m/s and 30°. Hibbeler §15.4.

Key

Keep the tangent component. Solve the normal components as a direct impact.

What the model leaves out

The faces are smooth, so there is no tangent impulse. A rough collision would change the tangent speeds, and set a ball spinning. That model is left out, as is any rotation the impact gives the bodies.

Another view. MIT OCW 8.01SC (Fall 2016), 27.6 2D Collisions.

05Angular momentum

The angular momentum of a particle about a point is \(\mathbf{r} \times m\mathbf{v}\). The moment arm is measured from that point to the particle.

Why

Angular momentum about a point is \(\mathbf{r}\times m\mathbf{v}\). In the plane the cross product has one component, \(m(xv_y - yv_x)\).

Differentiate it: \(\dot{\mathbf{H}} = \dot{\mathbf{r}}\times m\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{r}\times m\dot{\mathbf{v}}\). The first term is \(\mathbf{v}\times m\mathbf{v} = 0\). The second is \(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}\), the moment. So the moment about a fixed point is the rate of change of angular momentum about it. If the moment is zero, \(\mathbf{H}\) is constant.

\[ L_z = m(xv_y - yv_x), \qquad \sum M_O = \dot H_O. \]
Worked example · a particle passing a point

A 2 kg particle is at (0.4, −0.3) m. Its velocity is (1, 3) m/s. The angular momentum about the origin is 3 kg·m²/s.

Worked example · pulling a string through a hole

A 0.2 kg puck circles on a smooth table at 2 m/s, on a string 0.5 m long that passes down through a hole at the centre. The string is pulled until the radius is 0.25 m. The string's force points at the hole, so its moment about the hole is zero. Then \(rv\) is constant, and the speed becomes \(0.5(2)/0.25 = 4\) m/s.

The kinetic energy rises from 0.4 J to 1.6 J. The 1.2 J difference is the work done pulling the string: momentum about the hole is conserved, energy is not.

Worked example · an angular impulse

A 0.5 kg particle sits at the end of a light 1.2 m arm, pivoted at the other end, on a horizontal table. A motor at the pivot applies a 2 N·m moment for 3 s. The angular impulse is 6 N·m·s, which equals the change in \(H = mrv\). From rest, \(v = 6/(0.5 \times 1.2) = 10\) m/s.

Common mistake

The moment arm runs from the chosen point to the particle. It is not the distance measured along the velocity. Hibbeler §15.5.

Key

Use one point. Keep the signs of \(x\), \(y\), \(v_x\), and \(v_y\). A force through that point leaves \(H\) unchanged.

What the model leaves out

This is the angular momentum of a particle. It is not yet the spin of a rigid body, which Unit 6 builds by adding up particles. Central-force orbits, where this conservation does most of its work, are left out of the course.

Another view. MIT OCW 8.01SC (Fall 2016), 32.1 Angular Momentum for a Point Particle, 34.1 Torque Causes Angular Momentum to Change - Point Particle; 2.003SC (Fall 2011), Lecture 3, Angular Momentum and Torque.

06A rocket

A rocket loses mass. Thrust is the burn rate times the exhaust speed relative to the rocket. Gravity subtracts \(g\) times the burn time.

Why

Over a short time \(dt\), a rocket of mass \(m\) at speed \(v\) throws back a mass \(-dm\) at speed \(v_e\) relative to itself. Apply impulse and momentum to the whole system, rocket plus ejected gas. The only outside force is the weight. The momentum balance reduces to

\[ m\,dv = -v_e\,dm - mg\,dt. \]

Divide by \(m\) and integrate from \(m_0\) to \(m_f\). The first term gives \(v_e\ln(m_0/m_f)\). The second gives \(-gt_b\).

\[ \Delta v = v_e \ln(m_0/m_f) - g t_b. \]
Worked example · a coasting burn

A rocket burns from 200 kg to 80 kg. The exhaust speed is 1500 m/s and the burn rate is 40 kg/s. With no gravity, \(\Delta v\) is 1374 m/s. The mass ratio is 2.5. The burn lasts 3 s.

Worked example · the same mass burned

The same 120 kg leaves a heavier rocket, from 400 kg down to 280 kg, at the same rate and exhaust speed. The ratio is 1.429. \(\Delta v\) falls to 535 m/s.

Worked example · gravity during the burn

The first rocket burns the same way with \(g = 9.81\) m/s². The speed gain is 1345 m/s. Gravity took \(9.81 \times 3 = 29.4\) m/s during the burn.

The logarithm sets a hard limit. To reach \(\Delta v = 2v_e\), the mass ratio must be \(e^2 = 7.39\). Nearly 87% of the rocket must be propellant. That is why launch vehicles drop empty stages.

Worked example · a stage in feet

A stage with exhaust speed 8000 ft/s burns to a third of its starting mass, in space. \(\Delta v = 8000\ln 3 = 8790\) ft/s. The units of \(\Delta v\) are the units of \(v_e\). The logarithm has none, so no conversion is needed.

Common mistake

\(\Delta v\) is \(v_e\) times the log of the mass ratio, minus \(g t_b\). It is not \(v_e\) times the burned mass divided by \(m_0\). For the first rocket, that wrong form gives 900 m/s instead of 1374 m/s. Hibbeler §15.9.

Key

A larger mass ratio gives a larger \(\Delta v\), even when the burned mass is the same. Steady flow of a fluid stream, §15.8, is left out of this course.

In the Rocket lab, the coast, heavy, and gravity scenarios are the three examples above. The lab integrates the momentum balance step by step and matches the logarithm.

What the model leaves out

The exhaust speed and the burn rate are constant, the burn is vertical, and drag is left out. Gravity is taken as constant over the short burn. A real ascent also turns over as it climbs, which changes the gravity loss from \(gt_b\) to \(g\sin\gamma\) integrated over the burn.

Another view. MIT OCW 8.01SC (Fall 2016), 19.1 Rocket Problem 1 - Set up the Problem, 19.6 Rocket Problem 6 - Solution for No External Forces.

07Application: an upper stage

An engine data sheet gives thrust, burn rate and specific impulse. The rocket equation needs the exhaust speed. All three are the same fact, written three ways.

Why

The thrust is the momentum the exhaust carries away each second: \(F = \dot m\,v_e\). So \(v_e = F/\dot m\). Specific impulse is the impulse per unit weight of propellant: \(I_{sp} = F/(\dot m g_0) = v_e/g_0\). It is quoted in seconds. Multiply by \(g_0 = 9.81\) m/s² to get back to a speed.

\[ v_e = \frac{F}{\dot m} = I_{sp}\,g_0, \qquad \Delta v = v_e\ln\frac{m_0}{m_f} - g\,t_b. \]

A stage must also lift itself. At ignition the thrust must beat the weight: \(F > m_0 g\). The ratio \(F/(m_0 g)\) is the thrust-to-weight ratio.

Worked example · a methane–oxygen stage

A 500 kg stage burns 5 kg/s and makes 15,000 N. Then \(v_e = 15000/5 = 3000\) m/s, and \(I_{sp} = 3000/9.81 = 306\) s. At ignition the thrust-to-weight ratio is \(15000/4905 = 3.06\).

It burns down to 200 kg, a mass ratio of 2.5. The burn takes \(300/5 = 60\) s. The ideal gain is \(3000\ln 2.5 = 2749\) m/s. Gravity takes \(9.81 \times 60 = 589\) m/s. The speed at burnout is 2160 m/s.

Worked example · the same stage, throttled up

Double the burn rate to 10 kg/s at the same exhaust speed. The thrust doubles to 30,000 N. The mass ratio is still 2.5, so the ideal gain is still 2749 m/s. The burn now takes 30 s, and gravity takes only 294 m/s. The speed at burnout rises to 2455 m/s.

A faster burn spends less time holding itself up. That is why launch stages start with a thrust-to-weight ratio well above 1.

Worked example · a stage that barely lifts

Fit the same 15,000 N engine to a 1500 kg stage that burns down to 600 kg. The mass ratio is again 2.5, and the ideal gain again 2749 m/s. But the thrust-to-weight ratio is \(15000/14715 = 1.02\). The burn lasts \(900/5 = 180\) s. Gravity takes 1766 m/s, and only 983 m/s is left.

At ignition the net acceleration is \(15000/1500 - 9.81 = 0.19\) m/s². The stage creeps off the pad and spends its first minute fighting its own weight.

Common mistake

The burn time is the propellant over the burn rate, \((m_0 - m_f)/\dot m\). It is not the whole mass over the burn rate. For the first stage, \(500/5 = 100\) s would put the gravity loss at 981 m/s instead of 589 m/s. The dry mass never leaves the nozzle. Hibbeler §15.9.

Key

The mass ratio sets the ideal gain. The thrust-to-weight ratio sets how much of it gravity takes. Read \(v_e\) from \(F/\dot m\) or \(I_{sp}g_0\).

In the Rocket lab, the burn-in-gravity scenario takes all three stages above. Move the burn rate and watch \(t_b\) and the gravity loss fall together.

What the model leaves out

A real engine's thrust grows as the outside pressure falls, so sea-level and vacuum specific impulse differ. The model holds \(v_e\) constant. It also leaves out drag, which is largest low and slow, and the turn toward horizontal flight.

Another view. MIT OCW 8.01SC (Fall 2016), 19.5 Rocket Problem 5 - Thrust and External Forces, 19.7 Rocket Problem 7 - Solution with External Forces.

08Formula sheet

  • \(\int F\,dt = m\Delta v\). The impulse is the area. Average force is impulse over contact time.
  • If the external impulse is zero, the total momentum is constant.
  • Direct impact: momentum along the line, and \(v_2 - v_1 = e(u_1 - u_2)\).
  • Energy lost: \(\tfrac12\,m_1 m_2/(m_1 + m_2)\,(1 - e^2)(u_1 - u_2)^2\). Drop test: \(e = \sqrt{h_2/h_1}\).
  • Oblique impact: the tangent components stay as they were.
  • \(L_z = m(xv_y - yv_x)\). \(\sum M_O = \dot H_O\).
  • Rocket: \(\Delta v = v_e\ln(m_0/m_f) - gt_b\), with \(t_b = (m_0 - m_f)/\dot m\).
  • This unit uses \(g = 9.81\) m/s², or \(32.2\) ft/s². The slug is the US mass.
  • Steady flow, §15.8, stays out.

Steady flow of a fluid stream stays out. Drag on the rocket, and a burn rate that changes, stay out of the formulas above.
