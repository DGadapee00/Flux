Part I

Launch loads

1Shaken at the base

During launch the rocket shakes everything bolted to it. A component on a bracket is a mass on a spring with some damping, driven at its base.

Derivation

Let the base move as \(y(t)\) and the mass as \(x(t)\). The bracket pushes on the mass with \(k(y - x) + c(\dot y - \dot x)\), so with \(z = x - y\),

\[ m\ddot z + c\dot z + kz = -m\ddot y. \]

For a sine at frequency \(f\), the mass's acceleration in steady state is the base's times the transmissibility

\[ T = \sqrt{\frac{1 + (2\zeta r)^2}{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}}, \qquad r = \frac{f}{f_n}, \qquad f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac km}, \qquad \zeta = \frac{c}{2\sqrt{km}}. \]

Well below resonance \(T \approx 1\): the mass moves with the base. Near \(r = 1\), \(T \approx 1/(2\zeta)\). Above \(r = \sqrt2\), \(T < 1\): the mount isolates, and more damping makes that worse.

Worked example · a bracket at resonance

A 2 kg component on a bracket with \(f_n = 40\) Hz and \(\zeta = 0.05\), shaken at 40 Hz and 1 g. Then \(r = 1\) and

\[ T = \frac{\sqrt{1 + 0.1^2}}{0.1} = 10.05, \]

so the component sees 10 g and the bracket carries \(2 \times 10.05 \times 9.81 = 197\) N, ten times its static load.

Common mistake

Sizing a bracket for strength alone. A strong but flexible bracket whose natural frequency lands in the launcher's shaking band multiplies the load by about \(1/(2\zeta)\). Launchers set a minimum first natural frequency for this reason.
