Part I

Launch loads

1Shaken at the base

During launch the rocket shakes everything bolted to it. A component on a bracket is a mass on a spring with some damping, driven at its base.

Derivation

Let the base move as \(y(t)\) and the mass as \(x(t)\). The bracket pushes on the mass with \(k(y - x) + c(\dot y - \dot x)\), so with \(z = x - y\),

\[ m\ddot z + c\dot z + kz = -m\ddot y. \]

For a sine at frequency \(f\), the mass's acceleration in steady state is the base's times the transmissibility

\[ T = \sqrt{\frac{1 + (2\zeta r)^2}{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}}, \qquad r = \frac{f}{f_n}, \qquad f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac km}, \qquad \zeta = \frac{c}{2\sqrt{km}}. \]

Well below resonance \(T \approx 1\): the mass moves with the base. Near \(r = 1\), \(T \approx 1/(2\zeta)\). Above \(r = \sqrt2\), \(T < 1\): the mount isolates, and more damping makes that worse.

Worked example · a bracket at resonance

A 2 kg component on a bracket with \(f_n = 40\) Hz and \(\zeta = 0.05\), shaken at 40 Hz and 1 g. Then \(r = 1\) and

\[ T = \frac{\sqrt{1 + 0.1^2}}{0.1} = 10.05, \]

so the component sees 10 g and the bracket carries \(2 \times 10.05 \times 9.81 = 197\) N, ten times its static load.

Common mistake

Sizing a bracket for strength alone. A strong but flexible bracket whose natural frequency lands in the launcher's shaking band multiplies the load by about \(1/(2\zeta)\). Launchers set a minimum first natural frequency for this reason.

2Random vibration and fatigue

Besides a few sharp events, a launcher shakes its payload with broadband noise. It is described by a power spectral density \(W\), in g²/Hz, and its damage builds up like fatigue.

Derivation

A component on a bracket with natural frequency \(f_n\) and amplification \(Q\) passes the input mostly near \(f_n\). Integrating the transmissibility against a flat input gives

\[ g_\text{rms} = \sqrt{\tfrac{\pi}{2} f_n Q W\,(1 + 1/Q^2)} \approx \sqrt{\tfrac{\pi}{2} f_n Q W}\ \text{(Miles)}. \]

The stress follows, \(\sigma_\text{rms} = k\,g_\text{rms}\). Narrow-band peaks follow a Rayleigh distribution, and with an S–N curve \(N \propto S^{-b}\) the damage per second is \(f_n(\sqrt2\sigma_\text{rms})^b\Gamma(1 + b/2)/(N_\text{ref}S_\text{ref}^b)\).

Worked example · a 100 Hz bracket

With \(Q = 10\) under 0.04 g²/Hz: \(g_\text{rms} = \sqrt{1.571 \times 100 \times 10 \times 0.04} = 7.93\) g by Miles, 7.97 g with the \(1/Q^2\) term. At 4 MPa per g the stress is 32 MPa rms, and the bracket lasts about 590 hours of this shaking.

Common mistake

Checking only the 3σ peak against strength. A bracket can pass that check and still fail in fatigue during qualification: at 300 Hz, \(Q = 30\) and 0.1 g²/Hz it lasts about half a minute.

4Build it

Support the components without exceeding a stated stress or deflection limit.

Method

  1. Draw the load path: from each component through its bracket to the launcher interface.
  2. Take a launch acceleration for the case you are checking and state where it comes from.
  3. Check the most loaded attachment and one frame member against an allowable, with a factor of safety.
  4. Estimate the first natural frequency of the stack and compare it with the launcher’s requirement.
  5. Record what a static check cannot show: random vibration, fatigue and joint preload.

What to produce

A mass budget, load case, attachment check and deflection limit.

Handoff to other subsystems

Give Attitude control the mass layout and moments of inertia. Reserve space for the other subsystems.

Review checklist

  • The load path is drawn end to end.
  • Load case and its source are stated.
  • Stress is compared with an allowable and a factor of safety.
  • A first natural frequency is estimated.
  • Limits of the analysis are named.
  • Mass layout and inertia go to Attitude control.

5Choose the material

Stock arrives as plate, bar or sheet with a mill certificate. The alloy and its temper set what the shop can do to it and what the frame can carry.

What drives the choice

  • Stiffness for the mass: the first natural frequency must clear the launcher’s limit.
  • Strength where a fastener or a rail takes the launch load.
  • Expansion: the frame must grow and shrink with what holds it and what it holds.

Stiffness for the mass is about 25 for every metal here, so a stiffer metal frame comes from its shape, not its alloy. In bending the measure is ∛E/ρ, where low density wins: aluminium beats titanium by about 40%.

Aluminium 6061-T6

Fits: Cuts fast, welds, and comes as plate, bar, sheet and extrusion. The default for a first frame.

Tradeoff: The lowest strength of the heat-treated picks here. A weld takes about 40% of it away nearby.

What you need: Plate or bar in the T6 or T651 temper, with its certificate. Standard carbide cutters.

Aluminium 7075-T73

Fits: About 60% more yield strength than 6061-T6, at nearly the same weight and stiffness.

Tradeoff: It forms poorly at room temperature. The stronger T6 temper can crack under stress where it corrodes, which is why T73 exists.

What you need: Plate in the T73 temper. Order the temper by name: T6 and T73 are different materials.

Aluminium 5052-H32

Fits: The usual alloy for sheet metal work, and it resists corrosion well.

Tradeoff: It cannot be heat treated. Its strength comes from cold work and is the lowest here.

What you need: Sheet in the H32 temper.

Titanium Ti-6Al-4V

Fits: Twice the strength of 6061-T6 for the mass and about a third of its expansion. It conducts heat about twenty times worse than aluminium, which suits a mount that must not pass heat.

Tradeoff: No stiffer for its mass than aluminium, and worse in bending. Slow to cut, because its low conductivity keeps the heat at the tool.

What you need: Annealed bar or plate. Plan for far more machining time than aluminium takes.

Carbon fibre laminate

Fits: Along the fibres it is over four times stiffer for its mass than any metal here, and it barely changes length with temperature.

Tradeoff: Only along the fibres. A panel loaded in several directions needs plies at several angles and gives much of that back. Carbon against aluminium corrodes the aluminium.

What you need: Fibre and resin or prepreg, a mould, vacuum bagging and a cure. An insert wherever a fastener goes.

When the choice changes

  • A payload that must hold its alignment through each eclipse: choose for expansion, titanium or carbon fibre.
  • A mount that must not pass heat: titanium.
  • A large flat panel where mass is the limit: a carbon fibre laminate, with the layup and inserts that come with it.

Checks before choosing

  • A CubeSat deployer accepts four aluminium alloys without a waiver: 7075, 6061, 5005 and 5052. The frame must expand as the deployer does, or it jams.
  • A temper is part of the material. Heat from a weld or a hot cure can undo it.
  • Carbon fibre bolted to aluminium makes a galvanic pair. Isolate the two.

6Choose the process

The shop turns stock into a frame. Each route needs its own machines, tooling and inspection, and each limits the shapes and alloys you can use.

What drives the choice

  • Quantity: one frame, or a few.
  • Interfaces: rails, mating faces and bolt patterns must land on size.
  • Shape: pockets and ribs, or channels and forms no cutter can reach.
OptionStarts fromDoes wellLeaves you to do

Compare what each route leaves you to do. Machine time is the visible cost. The steps after it, and the kit to check the result, are the rest.

Machine from plate

Fits: One machine and its tooling make the whole frame, in the alloy and temper you bought.

Tradeoff: The cutter must reach every surface, and thin walls move under it.

What you need: A mill, workholding, cutters and the software to program it. Calipers, micrometers and a height gauge to check the result.

Cut and bend sheet

Fits: The fastest route from stock to a light panel or bracket.

Tradeoff: A bent sheet is thin. Its stiffness comes from folds and from the joints you add.

What you need: A shear, laser or waterjet to cut. A press brake and its dies to bend.

Print in metal

Fits: Several parts become one, and the shape is nearly free.

Tradeoff: Unmelted gaps, pores and cracks can hide inside. Grains grow along the build direction, so strength depends on direction.

What you need: The printer and its inert gas, with oxygen held below 1000 ppm. Safe powder handling, a furnace, a way to cut parts off the plate, and a mill for the interfaces.

Lay up a laminate

Fits: The lightest stiff panel.

Tradeoff: The mould comes first. Every joint is a bonded insert, and a cured laminate cannot be reworked as metal can.

What you need: A mould, vacuum bagging, an oven or autoclave, and dust control for trimming.

When the choice changes

  • Internal channels, or several parts that could be one: print it, and accept the steps after the build.
  • Flat covers and plain brackets: cut and bend sheet.
  • Many identical frames: tooling for forming or casting starts to pay, because its cost is shared across the lot.

Checks before choosing

  • A printed part still needs a mill. Faces that mate or seal are machined after the build.
  • A printed part can look sound and hide pores. Plan how the inside is checked before you choose the route.
  • Bending works the metal. A temper that bends well and a temper that is strong are often different.
  • Fixturing is part of the design. A part that cannot be held cannot be cut.

7Choose the joints

A frame in several pieces needs joints, and every joint adds mass, flexibility and something to inspect. Decide first which joints must open again.

What drives the choice

  • Access: what must come apart for assembly, test and rework.
  • Load path: the joint carries the launch load and sets the frame’s stiffness.
  • Proof: how you show the joint is good without breaking it.

Ask of each joint: will it open again, what load crosses it, and how is it proved. A joint that inspection cannot prove has to be proved by controlling the process that made it.

Bolted, with thread inserts

Fits: Comes apart as often as integration needs, and each fastener can be checked with a wrench.

Tradeoff: Preload from torque scatters widely. A joint that slips under vibration loosens.

What you need: Taps, insert tooling, calibrated torque wrenches and controlled fastener lots.

One piece, no joint

Fits: The stiffest and lightest answer: the joint is designed away.

Tradeoff: Bigger stock, longer cuts, and no way in once it is closed.

What you need: A machine with the travel for the whole piece.

Riveted

Fits: Fast, light and proven for thin sheet: aircraft frames are held by solid rivets.

Tradeoff: It is close to permanent. Opening the joint means drilling out every rivet.

What you need: Drill jigs, a rivet gun or squeezer, and a bucking bar.

Adhesive bonded

Fits: Spreads load over an area with no holes, and joins thin parts and unlike materials.

Tradeoff: Strength depends on surface preparation and cure, and neither leaves a visible trace.

What you need: A surface preparation line, a way to mix and dispense, fixtures, a cure oven, and coupons made with every batch.

Welded

Fits: Permanent and sealed, which a tank needs. Friction stir welding stays below melting, so it avoids pores and solidification cracks.

Tradeoff: Heat undoes the temper beside a fusion weld. Friction stir welding needs large forces, heavy clamping and a seam with a plain shape.

What you need: A welder and a qualified procedure, or a rigid stir welding machine with its tools. Fixtures either way.

When the choice changes

  • A sealed volume, such as a tank: weld it.
  • Thin sheet panels that stay closed: rivet them.
  • A laminate, or a part too thin for threads: bond it, and make coupons with every batch.

Checks before choosing

  • Aluminium threads strip and gall. Put a steel insert wherever a screw goes in more than once.
  • Titanium threads gall too, and so does stainless steel. Lubricate or coat them.
  • Torque is a poor measure of preload. Where preload matters, measure bolt stretch, which is good to about ±5%.
  • An anodised face does not conduct. A joint that must ground the frame needs bare or conversion-coated contact.

8Choose the finish

Bare aluminium marks, corrodes on the ground and can seize against its neighbour in vacuum. The finish also sets whether a joint conducts and how a face trades heat with space.

What drives the choice

  • Grounding: which faces must conduct across a joint.
  • Wear: which faces slide, such as the rails in a deployer.
  • Temperature: how much sunlight a face absorbs and how well it radiates.

A finish is chosen face by face. Mark each face on the drawing: it conducts, it slides, it radiates, or none of these.

Bare machined aluminium

Fits: No process and no change in size, and the face conducts.

Tradeoff: It marks and corrodes in handling, and it radiates heat poorly.

What you need: Clean gloves and bagging.

Chemical conversion coating

Fits: Protects the surface and keeps it conductive, so it suits the faces that ground the frame.

Tradeoff: It adds little wear resistance. The usual baths contain hexavalent chromium, which needs strict handling.

What you need: Tanks to clean, coat and rinse, with chemical handling and waste disposal.

Sulfuric anodise, Type II

Fits: Resists corrosion and wear, takes dye, and holds paint and adhesive better than bare metal.

Tradeoff: It insulates, so grounding faces must be masked. It changes every size it covers: half the layer grows outward, so a 20 µm coat adds 10 µm to each face.

What you need: An anodising line: tanks, a power supply, racking, masking and sealing.

Hard anodise, Type III

Fits: A hard face for anything that slides.

Tradeoff: The thickest layer moves sizes the most, and it needs a chilled tank and closer control.

What you need: The anodising line, with a refrigerated tank and a larger power supply.

Thermal control paint

Fits: Sets how much sunlight a face absorbs and how well it radiates. Chosen with the Thermal subsystem.

Tradeoff: It degrades in the space environment, and it is one more process to control.

What you need: A spray booth, surface preparation, a cure, and a way to measure film thickness.

When the choice changes

  • A face that radiates heat or sits in sunlight: the Thermal subsystem chooses its coating.
  • A part that stays inside and grounds through every joint: conversion coat alone.
  • A close fit: allow for the layer in the tolerance, or mask the fit.

Checks before choosing

  • Anodise insulates. An unmasked joint leaves part of the frame ungrounded.
  • The layer changes sizes. Put the finish and its thickness on the drawing before the fits are toleranced.
  • An anodised coat can crack above about 80 °C, because the oxide expands less than the metal. It cracks but does not peel.
  • Clean bare metal can cold weld to like metal in vacuum. Do not leave sliding faces bare.
