Part I

Heat in a vacuum

1The radiation balance

In orbit there is no air to carry heat away. A satellite gains heat from sunlight, from sunlight reflected off Earth, from Earth's own infrared glow and from its electronics, and loses it only by radiating.

Derivation

Take a sphere of radius \(R\), surface \(A = 4\pi R^2\), shadow \(A_p = \pi R^2\), with solar absorptivity \(\alpha\) and infrared emissivity \(\varepsilon\). If Earth fills a share \(F\) of its view, it settles where

\[ \varepsilon\sigma A T^4 = \alpha S A_p + \alpha a S A F + \varepsilon q_E A F + Q, \]

with \(S = 1361\) W/m², albedo \(a \approx 0.3\) and Earth's infrared \(q_E \approx 237\) W/m². Dividing by \(\varepsilon\) shows the finish enters through \(\alpha/\varepsilon\). In eclipse the sunlight terms vanish. A real satellite has heat capacity \(mc\), so it swings between the two and reaches neither:

\[ mc\,\frac{dT}{dt} = \text{absorbed}(t) + Q - \varepsilon\sigma A T^4. \]
Worked example · a black sphere at 500 km

Black paint, \(\alpha = 0.95\), \(\varepsilon = 0.9\), on a 0.5 m sphere with 10 W inside. In sun for good it would settle at 45.2 °C; in shadow for good, at −74.5 °C. With 10 kg of aluminium it never gets there: through each orbit it swings between −10.8 °C and 35.8 °C.

Common mistake

Expecting black to run hottest. Polished aluminium absorbs only 15% of sunlight but radiates only 5% as well as a black body: \(\alpha/\varepsilon = 3\), and it runs above 100 °C at 500 km. White paint, \(\alpha/\varepsilon = 0.22\), runs coldest.

2Two faces and a link

A single temperature for the whole craft hides the real problem: the face toward the Sun runs hot while the electronics on the shaded radiator run cold.

Derivation

Split the craft into nodes, each with its own balance. For a sunlit face (1) and a radiator (2) joined by a conductive link \(G\),

\[ \alpha SA_1 = \varepsilon\sigma A_1T_1^4 + G(T_1 - T_2), \qquad Q + H + G(T_1 - T_2) = \varepsilon\sigma A_2T_2^4, \]

with \(Q\) the electronics' heat and \(H\) a heater's. Two equations in \(T_1\), \(T_2\), solved together.

Worked example · full sun, a 1 W/K link

Faces of 0.25 m², \(\alpha = 0.6\), \(\varepsilon = 0.85\), 15 W of electronics: with no link the sunlit face reaches 88 °C and the radiator −85 °C. A 1 W/K link carries 56 W across and the faces settle at 60 °C and 4 °C.

Common mistake

Designing to the craft's average temperature. The average can be comfortable while one face cooks and another freezes. Check each node against its own limits.

4Build it

Keep the electronics within a chosen temperature range.

Method

  1. Collect the heat inputs: absorbed sunlight, Earth’s reflected and emitted heat, and the electronics’ dissipation from Power.
  2. Choose surface finishes and look up their absorptivity and emissivity.
  3. Write the balance for a hot case in full sun and a cold case in eclipse.
  4. Solve each for the temperature and compare with the electronics’ limits.
  5. If a case fails, change finishes, add insulation or a radiator, and solve again.
  6. State where a single-temperature model stops being true.

What to produce

Hot and cold temperature estimates with surface assumptions.

Handoff to other subsystems

Return temperature limits to Power and Payload. A bigger array also changes the exposed area.

Review checklist

  • Hot and cold cases are both solved.
  • Absorptivity and emissivity are stated with their source.
  • Electronics dissipation comes from the power budget.
  • Temperatures are compared with stated limits.
  • The limits of the lumped model are stated.
