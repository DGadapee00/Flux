Part I

Two-body orbits

1Ellipses and their speeds

A craft moving only under Earth's gravity follows an ellipse with Earth's centre at one focus. Its closest point is perigee, its farthest apogee.

Derivation

With perigee and apogee radii \(r_p\) and \(r_a\), the semi-major axis and eccentricity are

\[ a = \frac{r_p + r_a}{2}, \qquad e = \frac{r_a - r_p}{r_a + r_p}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{\mu}}. \]

Energy per unit mass is fixed at \(-\mu/2a\), so the speed at any radius is the vis-viva equation, \(v^2 = \mu(2/r - 1/a)\). Angular momentum is fixed too, so the line from Earth to the craft sweeps equal areas in equal times: fast near perigee, slow near apogee.

Worked example · a geostationary transfer orbit

Perigee 250 km and apogee 35,786 km above a 6378 km Earth: \(r_p = 6628\) km, \(r_a = 42{,}164\) km, so \(a = 24{,}396\) km and \(e = 0.728\). With \(\mu = 398{,}600\) km³/s², \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{a^3/\mu} = 37{,}920\) s, 10.53 h. At perigee \(v = \sqrt{\mu(2/6628 - 1/24{,}396)} = 10.2\) km/s; at apogee 1.60 km/s.

Common mistake

Using altitudes for \(r\). Every radius in these formulas is measured from Earth's centre: add Earth's radius.

2Kepler's equation

The angle round the orbit does not grow steadily with time. What does is the mean anomaly \(M = nt\), with \(n = \sqrt{\mu/a^3}\) and \(t\) from perigee.

Derivation

Equal areas in equal times, worked through for an ellipse, ties \(M\) to the eccentric anomaly \(E\), an angle measured from the ellipse's centre:

\[ M = E - e\sin E, \qquad r = a(1 - e\cos E), \qquad \tan\frac\nu2 = \sqrt{\frac{1 + e}{1 - e}}\,\tan\frac E2. \]

Given \(t\), the first equation has no closed-form solution for \(E\). Newton's method solves it: \(E \leftarrow E - (E - e\sin E - M)/(1 - e\cos E)\), a handful of steps to machine precision.

Worked example · a quarter period on

On the transfer orbit, a quarter period after perigee, \(M = 90°\). Newton's method gives \(E = 125.4°\), and then \(\nu = 156.4°\). In a quarter of the period the craft has gone 156° round, not 90°: the rest of the orbit, past apogee, takes the remaining three quarters.

Key idea

A ground station needs the position at a given time, so every orbit predictor solves Kepler's equation, or integrates the motion numerically. The lab does both and compares them.

3Orbit transfers

To change orbit, change speed. Where the burn happens, and which way, decide what the new orbit looks like.

Derivation

The orbit's energy per kilogram is \(v^2/2 - \mu/r = -\mu/2a\), which rearranges to the vis-viva equation

\[ v^2 = \mu\left(\frac2r - \frac1a\right). \]

A forward burn on a circle of radius \(r_1\) makes that point the perigee of a bigger ellipse. A Hohmann transfer picks the burn whose apogee is the target radius \(r_2\), so \(a = (r_1 + r_2)/2\), and burns again at apogee to round the orbit out:

\[ \Delta v_1 = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r_1}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2r_2}{r_1 + r_2}} - 1\right), \qquad \Delta v_2 = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r_2}}\left(1 - \sqrt{\frac{2r_1}{r_1 + r_2}}\right), \]

with half the ellipse, \(t = \pi\sqrt{a^3/\mu}\), between them.

Worked example · low orbit to geostationary

From 300 km, \(r_1 = 6678\) km, to 35,786 km, \(r_2 = 42{,}164\) km: the circular speeds are 7.73 and 3.07 km/s, and

\[ \Delta v_1 = 2426\ \text{m/s}, \qquad \Delta v_2 = 1467\ \text{m/s}, \qquad t = 5.27\ \text{h}. \]

3.89 km/s in all, spent speeding up twice, and yet the craft ends 4.66 km/s slower than it began: the climb trades speed for height.

Common mistake

Burning forward to catch a craft ahead on the same orbit. The burn raises the orbit and lengthens its period, so you fall further behind. Burn backward: a lower orbit is faster round.

Part II

The mission's clock

4Eclipses and ground-station passes

Two times run a satellite's day: how long it spends in Earth's shadow, and how long a ground station can see it.

Derivation

At radius \(r = R + h\) the period is \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{r^3/\mu}\). With the Sun in the orbit plane and the shadow a cylinder of radius \(R\), the satellite is in shadow over an arc of \(2\arcsin(R/r)\):

\[ t_\text{eclipse} = \frac{T}{\pi}\arcsin\frac{R}{r}. \]

A station sees the satellite above elevation \(\varepsilon\) while it is within an Earth-central angle \(\lambda = \arccos((R/r)\cos\varepsilon) - \varepsilon\), so an overhead pass lasts \(T\lambda/\pi\).

Worked example · 400 km

At 400 km, \(r = 6771\) km and \(T = 92.4\) min. \(\arcsin(6371/6771) = 70.2^\circ\), so the eclipse lasts 36.0 min. With a 10° mask, \(\lambda = 13.3^\circ\) of arc either side, and an overhead pass lasts 6.2 min.

Common mistake

Using the altitude where the radius belongs. Both the period and the shadow need \(r\) measured from Earth's centre.

6Build it

Predict where the satellite goes and how long each orbit takes.

Method

  1. Pick an altitude and say why: lifetime against drag, coverage, launch options.
  2. Measure r from Earth’s centre: r = R + h.
  3. Compute speed from v = √(GM/r) and period from T = 2πr/v, with units at every step.
  4. Check the period a second way: integrate the orbit, or use T = 2π√(r³/GM).
  5. Estimate the longest eclipse: the share of the orbit behind Earth for a circular orbit at that altitude.
  6. List what you assumed: circular orbit, no drag, spherical Earth.

What to produce

An orbit model with altitude, speed, period and assumptions.

Handoff to other subsystems

Pass the period and an estimated eclipse duration to Power. Pass the ground range to Communications.

Review checklist

  • r is measured from Earth’s centre, not the surface.
  • Speed and period agree with a second method within 1%.
  • Units are stated and carried through.
  • The eclipse duration is estimated and explained.
  • Assumptions are listed, with what each one leaves out.
  • The numbers Power and Communications need are stated.
Worked example · build review

Choose an altitude for a circular Earth orbit. Compute speed and period, state your units, and check the period by a second method.
