Part I

Describing an attitude

1Rotation matrices and three angles

An attitude is the rotation that takes the reference axes onto the body axes. A rotation matrix \(R\) does it: its columns are the body's x, y and z axes written in the reference frame.

Derivation

The columns stay unit length and perpendicular, so \(R^{\mathsf T}R = I\) and \(R^{-1} = R^{\mathsf T}\). Nine numbers with six conditions leave three freedoms. Three angles in sequence name them: yaw \(\psi\) about z, then pitch \(\theta\) about the new y, then roll \(\phi\) about the body x,

\[ R = R_z(\psi)\,R_y(\theta)\,R_x(\phi), \qquad R_z(\psi) = \begin{bmatrix}\cos\psi & -\sin\psi & 0\\ \sin\psi & \cos\psi & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}. \]

Order matters: the same three angles in another order give another attitude.

Worked example · a 3-2-1 turn

Yaw 30°, pitch 20°, roll 10°. Roll turns the craft about its own nose, so the nose, the first column, depends on yaw and pitch only:

\[ \hat{\mathbf x}_\text{body} = (\cos\psi\cos\theta,\ \sin\psi\cos\theta,\ -\sin\theta) = (0.814,\ 0.470,\ -0.342). \]

The nose points 30° round from x and 20° below the x-y plane.

Common mistake

Adding angles from different sequences, or applying the turns in the other order. Rotations do not commute: \(R_zR_y \ne R_yR_z\).

2Gimbal lock

A controller measures the body's angular velocity \(\boldsymbol\omega\) and must turn it into angle rates. Three angles cannot always do that.

Derivation

Each angle rate turns the body about its own axis: roll about the body x, pitch about the once-turned y, yaw about the reference z. Written in body axes,

\[ \boldsymbol\omega = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & -\sin\theta\\ 0 & \cos\phi & \cos\theta\sin\phi\\ 0 & -\sin\phi & \cos\theta\cos\phi\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\dot\phi\\ \dot\theta\\ \dot\psi\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det = \cos\theta. \]

Solving for the angle rates divides by \(\cos\theta\). At \(\theta = \pm90^\circ\) the yaw and roll columns are the same line, so the three angles span only two directions. The third is lost: that is gimbal lock.

Worked example · pitched up 80°

With zero roll, a turn of 1°/s about body z needs \(\dot\psi\cos\theta = 1\) and \(\dot\phi - \dot\psi\sin\theta = 0\):

\[ \dot\psi = \frac{1}{\cos 80^\circ} = 5.76\ ^\circ/\text{s}, \qquad \dot\phi = \tan 80^\circ = 5.67\ ^\circ/\text{s}. \]

At 85° both double. At 90° no rates exist: the turn about body z has no angle left to make it.

Common mistake

Reading the angle rates as the body's turn rates. They match only near zero pitch; near 90° a gentle turn needs violent angle rates.

3Quaternions

Any rotation is a single turn by an angle \(\alpha\) about one axis \(\hat{\mathbf n}\). A unit quaternion stores that axis and half the angle in four numbers.

Derivation \[ \mathbf q = \left(\cos\tfrac\alpha2,\ \hat{\mathbf n}\sin\tfrac\alpha2\right), \qquad \lvert\mathbf q\rvert = 1. \]

One turn followed by another is a quaternion product, so the 3-2-1 sequence is \(\mathbf q = q_z(\psi)\,q_y(\theta)\,q_x(\phi)\). A small body turn \(\delta\mathbf q\) updates it as \(\mathbf q \otimes \delta\mathbf q\), which works at every attitude: there are no angles to line up. \(\mathbf q\) and \(-\mathbf q\) are the same attitude.

Worked example · the tilted craft as a quaternion

Yaw 30°, pitch 20°, roll 10° multiply out to

\[ \mathbf q = (0.9515,\ 0.0381,\ 0.1893,\ 0.2393), \]

a single turn of \(2\arccos 0.9515 = 34.2^\circ\) about the axis \((0.0381, 0.1893, 0.2393)\) made unit length.

Key idea

Flight software carries a quaternion, or a matrix, and shows angles only to people. Four numbers with one condition, no lock, and cheap products.
