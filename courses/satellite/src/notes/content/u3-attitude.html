Part I

Describing an attitude

1Rotation matrices and three angles

An attitude is the rotation that takes the reference axes onto the body axes. A rotation matrix \(R\) does it: its columns are the body's x, y and z axes written in the reference frame.

Derivation

The columns stay unit length and perpendicular, so \(R^{\mathsf T}R = I\) and \(R^{-1} = R^{\mathsf T}\). Nine numbers with six conditions leave three freedoms. Three angles in sequence name them: yaw \(\psi\) about z, then pitch \(\theta\) about the new y, then roll \(\phi\) about the body x,

\[ R = R_z(\psi)\,R_y(\theta)\,R_x(\phi), \qquad R_z(\psi) = \begin{bmatrix}\cos\psi & -\sin\psi & 0\\ \sin\psi & \cos\psi & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}. \]

Order matters: the same three angles in another order give another attitude.

Worked example · a 3-2-1 turn

Yaw 30°, pitch 20°, roll 10°. Roll turns the craft about its own nose, so the nose, the first column, depends on yaw and pitch only:

\[ \hat{\mathbf x}_\text{body} = (\cos\psi\cos\theta,\ \sin\psi\cos\theta,\ -\sin\theta) = (0.814,\ 0.470,\ -0.342). \]

The nose points 30° round from x and 20° below the x-y plane.

Common mistake

Adding angles from different sequences, or applying the turns in the other order. Rotations do not commute: \(R_zR_y \ne R_yR_z\).

2Gimbal lock

A controller measures the body's angular velocity \(\boldsymbol\omega\) and must turn it into angle rates. Three angles cannot always do that.

Derivation

Each angle rate turns the body about its own axis: roll about the body x, pitch about the once-turned y, yaw about the reference z. Written in body axes,

\[ \boldsymbol\omega = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & -\sin\theta\\ 0 & \cos\phi & \cos\theta\sin\phi\\ 0 & -\sin\phi & \cos\theta\cos\phi\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\dot\phi\\ \dot\theta\\ \dot\psi\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det = \cos\theta. \]

Solving for the angle rates divides by \(\cos\theta\). At \(\theta = \pm90^\circ\) the yaw and roll columns are the same line, so the three angles span only two directions. The third is lost: that is gimbal lock.

Worked example · pitched up 80°

With zero roll, a turn of 1°/s about body z needs \(\dot\psi\cos\theta = 1\) and \(\dot\phi - \dot\psi\sin\theta = 0\):

\[ \dot\psi = \frac{1}{\cos 80^\circ} = 5.76\ ^\circ/\text{s}, \qquad \dot\phi = \tan 80^\circ = 5.67\ ^\circ/\text{s}. \]

At 85° both double. At 90° no rates exist: the turn about body z has no angle left to make it.

Common mistake

Reading the angle rates as the body's turn rates. They match only near zero pitch; near 90° a gentle turn needs violent angle rates.

3Quaternions

Any rotation is a single turn by an angle \(\alpha\) about one axis \(\hat{\mathbf n}\). A unit quaternion stores that axis and half the angle in four numbers.

Derivation \[ \mathbf q = \left(\cos\tfrac\alpha2,\ \hat{\mathbf n}\sin\tfrac\alpha2\right), \qquad \lvert\mathbf q\rvert = 1. \]

One turn followed by another is a quaternion product, so the 3-2-1 sequence is \(\mathbf q = q_z(\psi)\,q_y(\theta)\,q_x(\phi)\). A small body turn \(\delta\mathbf q\) updates it as \(\mathbf q \otimes \delta\mathbf q\), which works at every attitude: there are no angles to line up. \(\mathbf q\) and \(-\mathbf q\) are the same attitude.

Worked example · the tilted craft as a quaternion

Yaw 30°, pitch 20°, roll 10° multiply out to

\[ \mathbf q = (0.9515,\ 0.0381,\ 0.1893,\ 0.2393), \]

a single turn of \(2\arccos 0.9515 = 34.2^\circ\) about the axis \((0.0381, 0.1893, 0.2393)\) made unit length.

Key idea

Flight software carries a quaternion, or a matrix, and shows angles only to people. Four numbers with one condition, no lock, and cheap products.

4Euler’s equations and spin stability

Spinning a satellite is the cheapest way to hold it steady: a spinning body keeps its angular momentum. But not every spin axis works.

Derivation

About its principal axes a body's angular momentum is \(\mathbf H = (I_1\omega_1, I_2\omega_2, I_3\omega_3)\). In the turning body frame, \(\dot{\mathbf H} + \boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf H = \mathbf M\), which with no torque gives Euler's equations,

\[ I_1\dot\omega_1 = (I_2 - I_3)\,\omega_2\omega_3, \qquad I_2\dot\omega_2 = (I_3 - I_1)\,\omega_3\omega_1, \qquad I_3\dot\omega_3 = (I_1 - I_2)\,\omega_1\omega_2. \]

Spin at \(\omega_0\) about axis \(s\) with small rates \(w_a, w_b\) on the other two, and keep first-order terms:

\[ \ddot w_a = -\omega_0^2\,\frac{(I_s - I_a)(I_s - I_b)}{I_aI_b}\,w_a. \]

About the least or the greatest axis both factors have the same sign: the wobble oscillates. About the middle axis they differ: the wobble grows as \(e^{\lambda t}\) and the body flips.

Worked example · a brick about its middle axis

A 12 kg brick, 0.6 × 0.4 × 0.2 m, has \(I = m(b^2 + c^2)/12 = 0.20,\ 0.40,\ 0.52\) kg·m². Spun at 1 rad/s about the middle axis,

\[ \lambda = \sqrt{\frac{(0.40 - 0.20)(0.52 - 0.40)}{(0.20)(0.52)}} = 0.480\ \text{s}^{-1}. \]

The wobble grows e-fold every 2.1 s, so a 1% wobble becomes a flip in about 15 s.

Common mistake

Trusting the least axis for good. A rigid body is steady there, but a real one flexes and loses energy while keeping its angular momentum, and drifts to spin about its greatest axis. Spin-stabilized satellites spin about the greatest axis.

5Feedback and PID

A reaction wheel holds the satellite on target: a sensor measures the pointing error, a controller turns it into a torque command, and the wheel spins up to push back. Gains chosen by feel can make that loop ring, or worse.

Derivation

One axis with inertia \(J\) under a disturbance \(d\), and a wheel that answers its command \(u\) with a lag \(\tau\):

\[ J\ddot\theta = T + d, \qquad \tau\dot T = u - T, \qquad u = -\left(K_p\theta + K_d\dot\theta + K_i\!\int\!\theta\,dt\right). \]

With \(G(s) = 1/\big(Js^2(\tau s + 1)\big)\) and \(C(s) = K_p + K_d s + K_i/s\), the closed-loop poles are the roots of \(1 + C(s)G(s) = 0\):

\[ J\tau s^4 + Js^3 + K_ds^2 + K_ps + K_i = 0. \]

Every pole must sit left of the imaginary axis. With \(K_d = K_i = 0\) the \(s\) term is missing and some pole always lies on the right: P alone never settles. For PD, Routh's test gives stability only while \(K_p < K_d/\tau\). PD settles at \(\theta = d/K_p\); the integral term keeps pushing until the error is zero.

Worked example · holding against 10 mN·m

\(J = 10\) kg·m², \(\tau = 0.5\) s, \(K_p = 10\), \(K_d = 20\), \(K_i = 0\). The poles are \(0\), \(-0.639\) and \(-0.681 \pm 1.633i\): stable, as \(10 < K_d/\tau = 40\). The slowest decays as \(e^{-0.639t}\), so the loop settles in about \(4/0.639 = 6.26\) s, at

\[ \theta = \frac{d}{K_p} = \frac{0.01}{10} = 10^{-3}\ \text{rad} = 0.0573^\circ. \]
Common mistake

Turning up the gain to hold tighter. A larger \(K_p\) shrinks the offset only until it meets \(K_d/\tau\); past that the wheel's lag turns the push into a growing swing. Read the poles before raising a gain.

6Poles and stability

The Laplace transform turns a linear differential equation into algebra. The ratio of output to input, \(G(s)\), is the transfer function, and the roots of its denominator, its poles, are the modes of every response.

Derivation

A pair of poles \(p = \sigma \pm j\omega\) gives \(G(s) = K/(s^2 - 2\sigma s + \sigma^2 + \omega^2)\). For a unit step in, partial fractions split \(G(s)/s\) into one term per pole, so

\[ y(t) = 1 + 2\,\mathrm{Re}\!\left(\frac{K\,e^{pt}}{p\,(p - \bar p)}\right), \qquad |e^{pt}| = e^{\sigma t}, \]

with \(K = \sigma^2 + \omega^2\). The imaginary part \(\omega\) sets how fast it rings. The real part \(\sigma\) sets the envelope: it shrinks if \(\sigma < 0\) and grows if \(\sigma > 0\), however small. For a quadratic, Routh's test says the same from the coefficients: stable when both \(-2\sigma\) and \(\sigma^2 + \omega^2\) are positive.

Worked example · a damped pair

Poles at \(-1 \pm 2j\). The ringing has period \(2\pi/2 = 3.14\) s and the envelope \(e^{-t}\). The first peak overshoots by \(e^{\pi\sigma/\omega} = e^{-\pi/2} = 20.8\%\), and the response stays within 2% of 1 after about

\[ t_s = \frac{4}{|\sigma|} = 4\ \text{s}. \]
Worked example · σ = +0.1

Move the poles to \(0.1 \pm 2j\). For the first few seconds the response looks like a lightly damped one. But its envelope \(e^{0.1t}\) doubles every \(\ln 2/0.1 = 6.93\) s, and by 30 s it has swung out to 19.8, with no end.

Common mistake

Calling a slowly growing mode nearly stable. A pole just right of the axis is as unstable as one far right: it only takes longer to show. Mirror it to \(\sigma = -0.1\) and the response settles, though only after \(4/0.1 = 40\) s.

7Reaction wheels and momentum dumping

A reaction wheel points a craft by trading angular momentum with it. Every outside torque the craft resists ends up stored in the wheel.

Derivation

With no outside torque the total angular momentum, craft plus wheel, is fixed. To hold the craft still against a disturbance \(\tau(t)\), the wheel takes \(dH/dt = \tau(t)\). A part of \(\tau\) that reverses each orbit cancels over the orbit; the steady part \(\tau_0\) builds up. A magnetorquer with dipole \(m\) gives \(\mathbf m \times \mathbf B\), which can act only across the field; as the field turns once an orbit, it removes on average \((2/\pi)mB\). So

\[ t_\text{full} = \frac{H_\text{max}}{\tau_0 - \tfrac{2}{\pi}mB}. \]
Worked example · 10 µN·m

A steady 10 µN·m fills a 0.5 N·m·s wheel in \(0.5/10^{-5} = 50\,000\) s, 13.9 hours. A 0.3 A·m² torquer in a 30 µT field removes \((2/\pi)(0.3)(3 \times 10^{-5}) = 5.7\) µN·m on average, and the wheel lasts 32.5 hours. At 0.53 A·m² it never fills.

Common mistake

Treating a reaction wheel as a source of torque that can hold a craft forever. It only stores momentum; something that pushes on the outside world, a magnetorquer or thrusters, must empty it.
