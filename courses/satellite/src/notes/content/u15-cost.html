Part I

Estimating

1How cost is estimated

An estimate starts from a work breakdown structure: every task and item the project pays for, in levels, so nothing is counted twice or missed.

Key idea
  • Parametric: cost estimating relationships fitted to past missions, cost \(= a + b x^c\), where \(x\) is a mass, a power or an aperture. Fast, and the tool for trade studies early on. Use one only within the range of the data it was fitted to.
  • Analogy: scale the cost of a similar past system. Works at any level, but is hard to vary in a trade.
  • Bottom-up: price every task and part. The most accurate and the most work, once the design is mature.
  • Wraps cover what no part carries: management, systems engineering, product assurance, integration and test. They are often around 30% of development.

Independent estimates of the same thing can be combined, each weighted by the inverse of its variance.

Worked example · two estimates of a bus

One relationship gives a bus at USD 2.0M with a standard error of USD 0.6M. Another, fitted on closer analogues, gives USD 2.6M ± 0.4M. Weighted by \(1/\sigma^2\), the combined estimate is USD 2.42M ± 0.33M: closer to the tighter one, and tighter than either.

Common mistake

Using a relationship fitted to 100 kg satellites for a 10 kg CubeSat. Outside its data a fitted curve says nothing reliable.

2The learning curve

Each unit of a production run costs less than the one before: tooling is set up, people learn, suppliers sell in bulk.

Key idea

Each doubling of the number built multiplies the unit cost by the slope \(S\). Unit \(n\) costs \(T_1 n^b\), with \(b = \log_2 S\). Lecture 15's guide for aerospace: 95% for fewer than 10 units, 90% for 10 to 50, 85% beyond. Development is paid once, so it is shared among every unit built.

Some models use the cumulative-average form instead: the average of the first \(N\) units is \(T_1 N^b\), so the run costs \(T_1 N^{b+1}\). With the same slope it gives a smaller total. Say which form an estimate uses.

Derivation

A run of \(N\) costs \(\sum T_1 n^b\). The sum is close to the integral over \([\tfrac12, N + \tfrac12]\):

\[ \sum_{n=1}^{N} T_1 n^b \approx T_1\,\frac{(N + \tfrac12)^{b+1} - (\tfrac12)^{b+1}}{b + 1}. \]

The lab subtracts the midpoint rule's next term too, and checks the result against the exact sum.

Worked example · one craft

A 6U craft takes USD 3M of development and USD 1M to build. Built once, it costs USD 4M.

Worked example · ten at 95%

Ten of them cost USD 8.95M to build: the tenth costs USD 843k. With the development, USD 12.0M in all, or USD 1.20M a craft.

Worked example · a hundred at 85%

A hundred cost USD 43.8M to build, and the hundredth costs USD 340k. With the development they average USD 468k each. With no learning they would average USD 1.03M: the learning, not the shared development, did most of the saving.

Common mistake

Applying an 85% slope to three units. The steep slopes come from long production runs. A handful of craft learn little.

Part II

Uncertainty and time

3Confidence and reserves

An estimate is a distribution, not a number. A budget is chosen to cover it at some confidence.

Key idea
  • The spread of an element's cost follows its technology readiness: Lecture 15 gives over 25% at TRL 1 and 2, 20 to 25% at TRL 3, 15 to 20% at TRL 4, 10 to 15% at TRL 5 and under 10% from TRL 6.
  • Costs run high more often than low, so each is skewed, like a lognormal. Its median lies below its mean.
  • For independent elements the variances add, not the spreads. The reserve the whole project needs is smaller than the elements' reserves added.
  • A management reserve is held on top, for what the estimate missed. Lecture 15 suggests 10% for off-the-shelf hardware up to 50% for new designs beyond the state of the art. It is not for scope changes.
Derivation

The total's mean and variance are

\[ \mu = \sum c_i, \qquad \sigma^2 = \sum (c_i s_i)^2. \]

A lognormal with the same two moments has \(\sigma_L^2 = \ln(1 + \sigma^2/\mu^2)\) and \(m = \ln\mu - \sigma_L^2/2\). The budget at confidence \(p\) is \(e^{m + z_p \sigma_L}\), with \(z_p\) the normal quantile. The lab checks the moments by convolving the elements' distributions on a grid.

Worked example · a mature design

Unit 8's magnetometer craft, in six elements adding to USD 3.25M, all at TRL 6 or better. To finish within budget 70 times in 100 it needs USD 59k of reserve, 1.8%. Holding each element at 70% on its own and adding would ask for USD 121k.

Worked example · a new magnetometer

Make the USD 600k magnetometer a new design at TRL 3, with a TRL 4 boom and TRL 5 software. It now sets 57% of the spread, and the 70% reserve grows to USD 90k. At 90% confidence it is USD 231k.

Common mistake

Funding a project at the sum of its point estimates. Skewed costs put that near the median: it overruns about half the time.

4Life-cycle cost and the value of money

A satellite's cost does not end at launch, and money spent later is worth less today.

Key idea
  • The life cycle adds launch, operations and, for a service, replacing failures. Operations are easy to underestimate: a ground station and a team for years.
  • Design commits most of the cost early. The SE Handbook shows design spending around 15% of life-cycle cost while committing about 75%.
  • A sum \(F\) paid \(N\) years from now is worth \(P = F(1 + r)^{-N}\) today, at discount rate \(r\).
Worked example · five years of operations

Operating the craft costs USD 400k a year for five years. At a 5% discount rate that is worth USD 1.73M today, not USD 2.0M.

Common mistake

Choosing the design with the cheapest build and the dearest operations. Autonomy built in during design can cost less than the team it replaces over five years.

6Build it

Estimate what the mission costs and how sure that estimate is.

Method

  1. Write the work breakdown structure to the third level.
  2. Estimate each element by the best method its maturity allows, and record the basis.
  3. Give each element a spread from its readiness level.
  4. For more than one craft, apply a learning curve and say which form.
  5. Find the budget at the confidence the sponsor asks for, and add a management reserve.
  6. Add operations and launch, and discount future years.

What to produce

A cost estimate by work breakdown element, its basis, its confidence curve, and a phased budget.

Handoff to other subsystems

Give Mission and requirements the budget at its confidence. Tell each subsystem lead which element drives the spread. Give Operations the yearly cost it must work within.

Review checklist

  • Every element has an estimate and a basis.
  • No relationship is used outside its data.
  • Spreads follow readiness levels.
  • The budget states its confidence.
  • Operations and launch are included and discounted.
Worked example · build review

For your mission, give the work breakdown with its estimates, the budget at 70% confidence and its reserve, and the element that drives the spread.

Use the checklist above to review the result. State each assumption and the evidence supporting each interface value.
