Part I

Imaging

1Light, apertures and telescopes

An optical payload collects light with an aperture and focuses it onto a detector. Its size, its focal length and the wavelength it works in decide what it can see.

Key idea
  • Bands. Visible light runs from 400 to 700 nm, the near infrared from 0.7 to 2.5 µm, and ultraviolet lies below 400 nm.
  • Mirrors over lenses. A lens bends each colour differently; a mirror reflects them all the same way. Space telescopes are mostly mirrors, folded so a long focal length fits in a short tube.
  • Focal length sets the scale on the detector: an angle \(\alpha\) on the sky lands \(f\alpha\) across the focal plane.
  • The f-number, \(f/D\), sets how bright the image is: a lower f-number gathers light faster.
  • The detector is a grid of pixels, each turning photons into charge.
Worked example · a 6U telescope

A 9 cm mirror with a 580 mm focal length works at f/6.4. Each 5.5 µm pixel sees

\[ \alpha = \frac{p}{f} = \frac{5.5\times10^{-6}}{0.58} = 9.5\ \mu\text{rad} \]

of sky. Diffraction at 550 nm blurs a point over \(1.22\lambda/D = 7.5\) µrad, a little less than a pixel.

Common mistake

Taking the f-number as the resolution. Two telescopes at f/6 with different apertures resolve differently: the aperture sets the blur, the f-number only the brightness and the scale for a given aperture.

2Resolution and ground sampling

Two things limit the detail in an image from orbit: the ground one pixel covers, and the blur the aperture makes. The coarser of the two wins.

Derivation

Looking straight down from height \(h\), a pixel of pitch \(p\) covers the ground sample distance

\[ \text{GSD} = \frac{h\,p}{f}. \]

A circular aperture of diameter \(D\) blurs every point into an Airy pattern. Its first dark ring lies where \(\pi D \sin\theta/\lambda\) equals 3.8317, the first zero of the Bessel function \(J_1\), so

\[ \theta_R \approx 1.22\,\frac{\lambda}{D}, \]

and two points closer than that are not resolved (the Rayleigh criterion). On the ground the blur is \(h\theta_R\). The ratio \(Q = \lambda f/(Dp)\) compares the two: the pixels and the blur match at \(Q = 1/1.22 = 0.82\). Below it the pixels set the detail; above it the optics do.

The lab also finds the zero of \(J_1\) from its integral and checks it gives the same blur.

Worked example · a 9 cm telescope at 580 mm

From 500 km, each pixel covers \(500\,000 \times 5.5\times10^{-6}/0.58 = 4.74\) m. The blur at 550 nm is \(500\,000 \times 1.22 \times 550\times10^{-9}/0.09 = 3.73\) m. The pixels set the detail at 4.74 m, and \(Q = 0.64\). 4096 pixels across cover a 19.4 km swath.

Worked example · the same aperture at 1500 mm

Stretch the focal length to 1500 mm. Each pixel now covers 1.83 m, finer than the 3.73 m blur, so the optics set the detail and \(Q = 1.67\). The swath shrinks to 7.5 km. Smaller pixels would add nothing; a larger aperture would.

Common mistake

Quoting the ground sample distance as the resolution. A camera can sample the ground every metre and still blur it over four.

Part II

Laser links

3The laser link budget

A laser beam spreads far less than a radio beam, so a small terminal can put much more of its power on a ground telescope. The price is pointing.

Derivation

A Gaussian beam leaving with waist radius \(w_0\) spreads at the half-angle

\[ \theta = \frac{\lambda}{\pi w_0}. \]

At range \(d\) its \(1/e^2\) radius is \(w = \theta d\). A telescope of radius \(r\) at the beam's centre catches

\[ P_r = P_t\left(1 - e^{-2r^2/w^2}\right) \approx P_t\,\frac{2r^2}{w^2}. \]

Twice the range or half the transmit aperture makes \(w\) twice as large and delivers a quarter of the power. Pointed off by \(\varepsilon\), the beam's centre lands \(\varepsilon d\) away, and the power falls as \(e^{-2(\varepsilon/\theta)^2}\): half is lost at \(\varepsilon = \theta\sqrt{\ln 2/2} = 0.59\,\theta\).

The lab computes the power caught off-centre as a series and checks it against integrating the beam over the telescope.

Worked example · a 10 mm terminal at 1000 km

At 1550 nm a 10 mm aperture, waist radius 5 mm, spreads \(\theta = 1.55\times10^{-6}/(\pi \times 0.005) = 98.7\) µrad. At 1000 km the beam is 98.7 m in radius. A 40 cm telescope catches \(2(0.2)^2/98.7^2 = 8.2\times10^{-6}\) of 1 W: 8.2 µW, −20.9 dBm. At 200 Mbit/s that is \(3.2\times10^5\) photons for each bit.

Worked example · pointed by the body alone

A craft that points with its star tracker and wheels holds about 0.02°, 349 µrad (Unit 3). Turned toward the station by the body alone, the 10 mm beam lands 349 m off, three and a half beam radii. The power falls 109 dB to −130 dBm: nothing usable arrives. Half the power is lost at 58 µrad.

Common mistake

Widening the beam to ease pointing and expecting the same link. A beam ten times wider forgives ten times the error but delivers a hundredth of the power on target.

4Pointing, acquisition and weather

A laser link works only when the two ends can find each other, keep the beam on target and see through the air between them.

Key idea
  • Two-stage pointing. The craft's attitude control points coarsely; a fine-steering mirror inside the terminal holds the beam to a few microradians. Some small terminals manage with body pointing alone, with precise wheels and a star tracker.
  • Acquisition. The ground and the craft first shine wider beacon beams, or scan, to find each other before the narrow data beam locks on.
  • Weather. Clouds stop the link. Optical ground stations sit at dry, clear sites, often beside astronomical observatories, and data waits for a clear pass or goes to another station.
  • Spectrum. Optical frequencies are not licensed at present, unlike radio, and the narrow beam is hard to intercept or jam.
Worked example · what flies

NASA's small spacecraft report lists small laser terminals from 1 to 200,000 Mbit/s, mostly at 1550 nm, weighing a few kilograms or less and drawing 3 to 100 W. Aerospace Corporation's OCSD CubeSats sent 200 Mbit/s from a 1.5U craft to a 40 cm ground telescope, pointing the whole craft.

Common mistake

Planning a laser downlink as if every pass were usable. Cloud cover at one station can block days of passes; plan more stations or store the data until a clear one.

6Build it

Show the payload can see what the mission needs, and the link can bring it down.

Method

  1. State the smallest thing the mission must see, and the band.
  2. Choose an aperture whose diffraction blur from your altitude is finer than that, then a focal length and pixel that sample it.
  3. Find the swath and the data per image, and give the data to the data budget.
  4. For a laser downlink, find the beam divergence, the power on the ground telescope and the pointing error that costs 3 dB.
  5. Compare that pointing error with what attitude control holds; add a fine-steering mirror if it is not enough.
  6. Plan for cloud: stations, storage and the passes that will be lost.

What to produce

An imaging budget with GSD, blur and swath; a laser link budget with pointing loss; and a pointing requirement for Attitude control.

Handoff to other subsystems

Give Attitude control the pointing and stability the payload and the laser need. Give the data budget the volume per image. Give Thermal the terminal's heat.

Review checklist

  • The blur from the aperture is finer than the smallest required detail.
  • Pixels sample the blur, neither far coarser nor far finer.
  • The laser's received power is stated with its pointing loss.
  • The pointing requirement is compared with what attitude control holds.
  • Clouds and acquisition are in the operations plan.
Worked example · build review

For your payload, give the GSD, the blur and which limits; for your downlink, the divergence, the received power and the pointing error that costs 3 dB.

Use the checklist above to review the result. State each assumption and the evidence supporting each interface value.
