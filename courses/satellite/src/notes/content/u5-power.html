Part I

The energy budget

1Daylight and eclipse

A satellite in low orbit spends a third of each orbit in Earth's shadow. Its array works only in daylight; the battery carries it through the dark.

Derivation

Treat Earth's shadow as a cylinder of radius \(R\). With the Sun in the orbit's plane, an orbit of radius \(r\) is in shadow over an arc of half-angle \(\arcsin(R/r)\) either side of midnight, so

\[ T_e = T\,\frac{\arcsin(R/r)}{\pi}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{\mu}}, \qquad T_s = T - T_e. \]

The load \(P_L\) runs all the time. In daylight the array carries it and refills the battery for the eclipse; every watt-hour going through the battery loses a share in charging (\(\eta_c\)) and again in discharging (\(\eta_d\)). A sun-tracking array therefore needs

\[ P_\text{sun} = P_L\,\frac{T_s + T_e/(\eta_c\eta_d)}{T_s}. \]

The battery gives \(P_L T_e/\eta_d\) each eclipse; that over its capacity is the depth of discharge.

Worked example · a 20 W load at 500 km

At 500 km, \(T = 94.6\) min and \(\arcsin(6378/6878) = 1.19\) rad, so the eclipse lasts 35.8 min and the daylight 58.8 min. With 90% each way,

\[ P_\text{sun} = 20\,\frac{58.8 + 35.8/0.81}{58.8} = 35.0\ \text{W}. \]

The eclipse draws \(20 \times 35.8/60/0.9 = 13.2\) Wh: a 40 Wh battery cycles to 33% depth every orbit.

Common mistake

Sizing the array to the orbit-average load, \(P_L T/T_s = 32.1\) W here. That forgets the battery's losses, and the charge sinks a little every orbit until it runs flat.

2Battery fade and array ageing

A battery in low orbit cycles once an orbit, about 5700 times a year, and every cycle costs a little capacity. The array loses power to radiation year by year.

Derivation

Suppose each cycle costs capacity in proportion to the square of its depth, \(d = E/C\): \(dC/dn = -k(E/C)^2\). As \(C\) falls the same eclipse energy \(E\) is a deeper discharge, so the fade speeds up. Separating and integrating,

\[ C(n) = \left(C_0^3 - 3kE^2n\right)^{1/3}. \]

The array's power decays by a fixed share each year: \(P(y) = P_0(1 - 0.0275)^y\).

Worked example · 175 Wh for five years

With \(E = 35\) Wh, the battery starts at 20% depth. With \(k = C_0/6000\), after \(5 \times 5700\) cycles \(C = (175^3 - 3 \times 0.0292 \times 35^2 \times 28\,500)^{1/3} = 132\) Wh, and the eclipse takes 26.5% of it.

Common mistake

Sizing the battery for the depth of discharge at launch. A 100 Wh battery starts at 35% depth and, because each cycle deepens the next, is exhausted in 2.7 years.

4Build it

Keep the electronics powered through sunlight and eclipse.

Method

  1. List every load with its power and the share of each orbit it runs.
  2. Split the orbit into sunlight and eclipse using the Orbit numbers.
  3. Size the battery for the eclipse: energy needed divided by the depth of discharge you allow and the path efficiency.
  4. Size the array to run the loads in sunlight and refill the battery, at end of life, not at launch.
  5. Add a margin and say how big it is and why.
  6. Check one orbit by stepping the battery’s charge through sun and shadow.

What to produce

A power budget, battery capacity and solar-array requirement.

Handoff to other subsystems

Give Thermal the dissipated power. Give every powered subsystem its allowed draw.

Review checklist

  • Every load is listed with power and duty cycle.
  • Battery size follows from eclipse energy, depth of discharge and efficiency.
  • Array size covers loads plus recharge, with degradation.
  • A margin is stated.
  • A step-through of one orbit agrees with the budget.
  • Dissipated power is passed to Thermal.
