Part I

The energy budget

1Daylight and eclipse

A satellite in low orbit spends a third of each orbit in Earth's shadow. Its array works only in daylight; the battery carries it through the dark.

Derivation

Treat Earth's shadow as a cylinder of radius \(R\). With the Sun in the orbit's plane, an orbit of radius \(r\) is in shadow over an arc of half-angle \(\arcsin(R/r)\) either side of midnight, so

\[ T_e = T\,\frac{\arcsin(R/r)}{\pi}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{\mu}}, \qquad T_s = T - T_e. \]

The load \(P_L\) runs all the time. In daylight the array carries it and refills the battery for the eclipse; every watt-hour going through the battery loses a share in charging (\(\eta_c\)) and again in discharging (\(\eta_d\)). A sun-tracking array therefore needs

\[ P_\text{sun} = P_L\,\frac{T_s + T_e/(\eta_c\eta_d)}{T_s}. \]

The battery gives \(P_L T_e/\eta_d\) each eclipse; that over its capacity is the depth of discharge.

Worked example · a 20 W load at 500 km

At 500 km, \(T = 94.6\) min and \(\arcsin(6378/6878) = 1.19\) rad, so the eclipse lasts 35.8 min and the daylight 58.8 min. With 90% each way,

\[ P_\text{sun} = 20\,\frac{58.8 + 35.8/0.81}{58.8} = 35.0\ \text{W}. \]

The eclipse draws \(20 \times 35.8/60/0.9 = 13.2\) Wh: a 40 Wh battery cycles to 33% depth every orbit.

Common mistake

Sizing the array to the orbit-average load, \(P_L T/T_s = 32.1\) W here. That forgets the battery's losses, and the charge sinks a little every orbit until it runs flat.
