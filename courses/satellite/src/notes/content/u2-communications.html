Part I

Noise

1Noise from heat

The electrons in any resistor jiggle with its temperature, so it makes a small random voltage with no signal at all. A receiver adds noise of its own the same way. Both are rated as a temperature.

Derivation

Nyquist's argument: a resistor at temperature \(T\) feeding a matched load delivers \(k_BT\) of power in every hertz of band, whatever its resistance. A larger resistor makes a larger noise voltage, \(\overline{v^2} = 4k_BTRB\), but delivers the same power to a matched load. In a band \(B\),

\[ N = k_B\,T_\text{sys}\,B, \qquad k_B = 1.381\times10^{-23}\ \text{J/K}. \]

\(T_\text{sys}\) gathers everything: the sky the antenna sees, the cable and the amplifier. At 290 K, \(k_BT\) is \(4.0\times10^{-21}\) W/Hz, or −174 dBm in each hertz.

Worked example · a small satellite’s downlink

A ground station with \(T_\text{sys} = 500\) K listens in a 10 kHz band. The noise is

\[ N = (1.381\times10^{-23})(500)(10^4) = 6.9\times10^{-17}\ \text{W} = -131.6\ \text{dBm}. \]

A signal arriving at −120 dBm, \(10^{-15}\) W, is 11.6 dB above it: \(S/N = 14.5\).

Common mistake

Calling a signal too weak because it is small. −120 dBm is a millionth of a millionth of a milliwatt, and it reads cleanly. A signal is lost when it sinks below the noise in its band.

2Bits in noise

A digital link cares about each bit's energy against the noise. Sending fewer bits a second lets each one last longer, and a matching narrower band lets in less noise.

Derivation

A bit lasts \(1/R_b\), so it carries \(E_b = S/R_b\). The noise per hertz is \(N_0 = k_BT_\text{sys}\), so

\[ \frac{E_b}{N_0} = \frac{S}{k_B T_\text{sys} R_b}. \]

For binary phase-shift keying, each bit is a sign, \(\pm\sqrt{E_b}\), and noise flips it with probability \(\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}\sqrt{E_b/N_0}\). One error in a million needs about 10.5 dB.

Worked example · a weak signal, a narrower band

The signal drops to −135 dBm. At 10 kbit/s, \(E_b/N_0 = -3.4\) dB and about one bit in six is wrong. At 1 kbit/s in a 1 kHz band,

\[ \frac{E_b}{N_0} = \frac{3.16\times10^{-17}}{(1.381\times10^{-23})(500)(10^3)} = 4.6 = 6.6\ \text{dB}, \]

and the error rate is \(\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}\sqrt{4.6} = 1.24\times10^{-3}\). A tenth of the data rate bought 10 dB.

Key idea

Every dB counts the same wherever it comes from: transmit power, antenna gain, a colder receiver or a lower bit rate. A link budget adds them up.

3The link budget

Whether a link closes is a chain of multiplications: power out, antenna gain, the loss over the range, the dish's gain, against the noise. In decibels the chain becomes a sum, written as a budget.

Derivation

Power \(P_t\) radiated with gain \(G_t\) spreads over a sphere, so at range \(d\) it arrives as \(P_tG_t/4\pi d^2\) per square metre. A dish of effective area \(A_e = G_r\lambda^2/4\pi\) catches

\[ P_r = P_tG_tG_r\left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi d}\right)^2, \qquad G = \eta\left(\frac{\pi D}{\lambda}\right)^2. \]

In decibels, \(P_r = P_t + G_t + G_r - L\), with \(L = 20\log_{10}(4\pi d/\lambda)\). The power falls as \(1/d^2\): twice the range costs 6 dB. Then \(E_b/N_0 = P_r - 10\log_{10}(k_BT) - 10\log_{10}R_b\), all in decibels.

Worked example · a low-orbit downlink

2 W (3.0 dBW) in S band, \(\lambda = 13.6\) cm, through a 6 dBi antenna, 1000 km to a 3 m dish at 300 K, at 1 Mbit/s. The path loss is \(20\log_{10}(4\pi\times10^6/0.136) = 159.3\) dB and the dish gains \(10\log_{10}\big(0.55(\pi\cdot3/0.136)^2\big) = 34.2\) dBi, so \(P_r = 3.0 + 6 - 159.3 + 34.2 = -116.1\) dBW. With \(k_BT = -203.8\) dBW/Hz,

\[ \frac{E_b}{N_0} = -116.1 + 203.8 - 60 = 27.7\ \text{dB}, \]

17 dB more than the 10.5 dB needed. At geostationary range the path costs 31 dB more and the link fails.

Common mistake

Scaling received power with distance. It goes as \(1/d^2\): twice as far is a quarter of the power, and 36 times as far is 31 dB less.
