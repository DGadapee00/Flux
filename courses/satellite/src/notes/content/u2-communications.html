Part I

Noise

1Noise from heat

The electrons in any resistor jiggle with its temperature, so it makes a small random voltage with no signal at all. A receiver adds noise of its own the same way. Both are rated as a temperature.

Derivation

Nyquist's argument: a resistor at temperature \(T\) feeding a matched load delivers \(k_BT\) of power in every hertz of band, whatever its resistance. A larger resistor makes a larger noise voltage, \(\overline{v^2} = 4k_BTRB\), but delivers the same power to a matched load. In a band \(B\),

\[ N = k_B\,T_\text{sys}\,B, \qquad k_B = 1.381\times10^{-23}\ \text{J/K}. \]

\(T_\text{sys}\) gathers everything: the sky the antenna sees, the cable and the amplifier. At 290 K, \(k_BT\) is \(4.0\times10^{-21}\) W/Hz, or −174 dBm in each hertz.

Worked example · a small satellite’s downlink

A ground station with \(T_\text{sys} = 500\) K listens in a 10 kHz band. The noise is

\[ N = (1.381\times10^{-23})(500)(10^4) = 6.9\times10^{-17}\ \text{W} = -131.6\ \text{dBm}. \]

A signal arriving at −120 dBm, \(10^{-15}\) W, is 11.6 dB above it: \(S/N = 14.5\).

Common mistake

Calling a signal too weak because it is small. −120 dBm is a millionth of a millionth of a milliwatt, and it reads cleanly. A signal is lost when it sinks below the noise in its band.

2Bits in noise

A digital link cares about each bit's energy against the noise. Sending fewer bits a second lets each one last longer, and a matching narrower band lets in less noise.

Derivation

A bit lasts \(1/R_b\), so it carries \(E_b = S/R_b\). The noise per hertz is \(N_0 = k_BT_\text{sys}\), so

\[ \frac{E_b}{N_0} = \frac{S}{k_B T_\text{sys} R_b}. \]

For binary phase-shift keying, each bit is a sign, \(\pm\sqrt{E_b}\), and noise flips it with probability \(\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}\sqrt{E_b/N_0}\). One error in a million needs about 10.5 dB.

Worked example · a weak signal, a narrower band

The signal drops to −135 dBm. At 10 kbit/s, \(E_b/N_0 = -3.4\) dB and about one bit in six is wrong. At 1 kbit/s in a 1 kHz band,

\[ \frac{E_b}{N_0} = \frac{3.16\times10^{-17}}{(1.381\times10^{-23})(500)(10^3)} = 4.6 = 6.6\ \text{dB}, \]

and the error rate is \(\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}\sqrt{4.6} = 1.24\times10^{-3}\). A tenth of the data rate bought 10 dB.

Key idea

Every dB counts the same wherever it comes from: transmit power, antenna gain, a colder receiver or a lower bit rate. A link budget adds them up.

3The link budget

Whether a link closes is a chain of multiplications: power out, antenna gain, the loss over the range, the dish's gain, against the noise. In decibels the chain becomes a sum, written as a budget.

Derivation

Power \(P_t\) radiated with gain \(G_t\) spreads over a sphere, so at range \(d\) it arrives as \(P_tG_t/4\pi d^2\) per square metre. A dish of effective area \(A_e = G_r\lambda^2/4\pi\) catches

\[ P_r = P_tG_tG_r\left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi d}\right)^2, \qquad G = \eta\left(\frac{\pi D}{\lambda}\right)^2. \]

In decibels, \(P_r = P_t + G_t + G_r - L\), with \(L = 20\log_{10}(4\pi d/\lambda)\). The power falls as \(1/d^2\): twice the range costs 6 dB. Then \(E_b/N_0 = P_r - 10\log_{10}(k_BT) - 10\log_{10}R_b\), all in decibels.

Worked example · a low-orbit downlink

2 W (3.0 dBW) in S band, \(\lambda = 13.6\) cm, through a 6 dBi antenna, 1000 km to a 3 m dish at 300 K, at 1 Mbit/s. The path loss is \(20\log_{10}(4\pi\times10^6/0.136) = 159.3\) dB and the dish gains \(10\log_{10}\big(0.55(\pi\cdot3/0.136)^2\big) = 34.2\) dBi, so \(P_r = 3.0 + 6 - 159.3 + 34.2 = -116.1\) dBW. With \(k_BT = -203.8\) dBW/Hz,

\[ \frac{E_b}{N_0} = -116.1 + 203.8 - 60 = 27.7\ \text{dB}, \]

17 dB more than the 10.5 dB needed. At geostationary range the path costs 31 dB more and the link fails.

Common mistake

Scaling received power with distance. It goes as \(1/d^2\): twice as far is a quarter of the power, and 36 times as far is 31 dB less.

Part II

Telemetry, tracking and command

4What TT&C does

Telemetry, tracking and command is the craft's only line to the ground. Through it the team learns the craft's health, finds where it is, and tells it what to do.

Key idea
  • Commands come up, are decoded, and are checked before anything acts: the right address, an error check that passes, a valid command, valid timing, and an authenticated sender. Any doubt and the command is rejected.
  • Telemetry goes down: every sensor sampled, digitized and packed into frames with a sync word, a header and an error check.
  • Tracking measures where the craft is: the range from the time a signal takes to come back, the range rate from its Doppler shift, and the direction from where the antenna points.

Commands need fewer errors than telemetry, because a wrong command acts on the craft: MIT 16.851 gives one bit in a million for commands and one in a hundred thousand for telemetry.

Sample each signal at least twice as fast as it changes, the Nyquist criterion, and with enough bits that one step of the converter is smaller than the change you care about. Temperatures change in minutes; a gyro's rate in fractions of a second.

Worked example · a housekeeping telemetry rate
  • 40 temperatures, 12 bits, every 10 s: 48 bit/s.
  • 16 voltages and currents, 12 bits, every second: 192 bit/s.
  • 6 gyro and magnetometer axes, 16 bits, 10 times a second: 960 bit/s.
  • 32 status flags, once a second: 32 bit/s.

That is 1232 bit/s of data. Sync words, headers and error checks add about 10%, so the frames run at 1.36 kbit/s: the 2 kbit/s of housekeeping in Unit 10's data budget covers it.

Common mistake

Sampling everything at the fastest rate any signal needs. Forty temperatures at 10 samples a second would send 4.8 kbit/s of numbers that barely move.

5Tracking: range and Doppler

The station measures how far away the craft is and how fast that distance changes. A run of these through a pass fixes the orbit.

Derivation

A craft at radius \(a\) passes straight over a station at radius \(R\). At angle \(\theta = nt\) from overhead, with \(n = \sqrt{\mu/a^3}\), the law of cosines gives the range and its rate:

\[ \rho = \sqrt{R^2 + a^2 - 2Ra\cos\theta}, \qquad \dot\rho = \frac{R a n \sin\theta}{\rho}. \]

The carrier \(f\) arrives shifted by \(\Delta f = -f\dot\rho/c\): high as the craft closes, zero overhead, low as it recedes. Overhead the shift changes fastest, at \(f R a n^2/(c(a - R))\). In a two-way coherent link the craft turns the station's uplink around, so the shift is counted twice and the craft's own oscillator drops out. A code sent up and returned measures the light time \(2\rho/c\), and so the range.

The lab checks the range rate against propagating the orbit and projecting the velocity on the line of sight.

Worked example · UHF at 500 km

A craft at 500 km passes overhead. Above 10° the pass lasts 7.38 min and the range runs from 1695 km at the edge to 500 km overhead. At the edge it closes at 6.96 km/s, so a 437 MHz carrier arrives

\[ \Delta f = \frac{(437\times10^6)(6.96)}{299\,792} = 10.1\ \text{kHz} \]

high. Overhead the shift changes 157 Hz each second. The two-way light time runs from 3.34 ms overhead to 11.3 ms at the edge.

Worked example · S-band, two-way coherent

At 2200 MHz the one-way shift is 5.03 times larger, 51.0 kHz. Made two-way coherent it doubles to 102 kHz, and the station's receiver must sweep that whole range in the 7.4 minutes of the pass.

Common mistake

Using the orbital speed, 7.6 km/s, as the range rate. Only the part of the velocity along the line of sight shifts the carrier, and overhead that part is zero.

6Command security and licensing

Anyone with a transmitter in the right band can send a craft a command. The command link must accept only its own operators, and every transmitter needs a licence.

Key idea

NASA requires its missions to protect command authority with authenticated encryption, from where the command is made to the craft, using validated cryptographic modules. Backup command links must be authenticated too.

Every non-federal U.S. craft with a transmitter needs an FCC licence, and so does its ground station. University CubeSats often use an experimental licence, FCC Part 5. An amateur licence, Part 97, is for hobby and non-profit use: the downlink may not be encrypted, the data may not be used for profit, and a licensed amateur control operator is required.

Worked example · checking a command

A command arrives with the craft's address, a valid opcode and a passing error check, but its authentication tag does not match the key on board. Command logic rejects it and counts the rejection in telemetry. A burst of rejections warns the operators that someone else is transmitting.

Common mistake

Leaving the command uplink open because the craft is small. An open uplink lets anyone switch the transmitter on, fire a thruster or erase the software, and the craft cannot tell the difference.

8Build it

Return sensor readings within the available energy and contact time.

Method

  1. Take the range from Orbit and the data volume from Payload.
  2. Choose a frequency, transmitter power and antennas, and state their gains.
  3. Write the link budget in decibels: power plus gains minus path and other losses.
  4. Compute the noise from the receiver’s noise temperature and bandwidth.
  5. Find the margin: what you have above what the data rate needs.
  6. Check that contact time and transmitter energy can return the planned data.

What to produce

A link budget and a contact-by-contact data and energy estimate.

Handoff to other subsystems

Take range from Orbit, pointing from Attitude control, power from Power and data volume from Payload. Tell Payload how much data can be returned.

Review checklist

  • Every term of the budget is in decibels with its source.
  • Path loss follows from range and wavelength.
  • Noise follows from temperature and bandwidth.
  • A positive margin is shown and its size justified.
  • Data returned per contact covers the payload’s volume.
  • Transmitter energy goes back to Power.
Worked example · build review

State range, frequency, antenna assumptions, data rate and receiver sensitivity. Build a link budget with a margin, then check whether contact time and transmitter energy can return the planned data.
