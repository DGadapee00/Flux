Part I

Air and the Sun

1The environment of a low orbit

Low orbit is not empty. It holds a thin, hot gas, charged particles, a magnetic field and radiation, and the Sun changes all of them.

Key idea

Six things act on a craft in low orbit:

  • The Sun runs an eleven-year cycle. Its radio flux at 10.7 cm, F10.7, tracks the cycle: about 70 when quiet and 250 when active.
  • The neutral atmosphere above 200 km is mostly atomic oxygen. It drags the orbit down and erodes surfaces.
  • The plasma of the ionosphere charges surfaces, and an uneven charge can arc.
  • The magnetic field steers charged particles and gives the attitude system something to push against.
  • Radiation builds up dose in parts and makes single-event upsets in logic.
  • The vacuum lets materials outgas and coat optics.
Worked example · an oxygen atom at orbital speed

At 400 km a craft moves at \(v = \sqrt{\mu/r} = 7.67\) km/s. An oxygen atom it meets has, in the craft's frame,

\[ E = \tfrac12 m v^2 = \tfrac12 (2.66\times10^{-26}\ \text{kg})(7670\ \text{m/s})^2 = 7.8\times10^{-19}\ \text{J} = 4.9\ \text{eV}. \]

That is several times the energy of a chemical bond, a few electron-volts, so the atoms break polymers apart. Kapton and silver erode on the forward faces, and those faces need oxide coatings.

Common mistake

Designing for a fixed environment. The air at 500 km is several times denser at solar maximum than at minimum, and a solar storm can multiply the radiation for a day. Say which conditions each number assumes.

2Drag and the upper atmosphere

Drag in low orbit is tiny, but it never stops, and it decides how long the satellite lives.

Derivation

A gas at rest balances its weight with its pressure: \(dp/dz = -\rho g\). For an ideal gas at one temperature, \(p = \rho kT/m\), so the density falls by e every scale height:

\[ \rho = \rho_r\, e^{-(z - z_r)/H}, \qquad H = \frac{kT}{mg}. \]

Above 300 km the gas is mostly atomic oxygen at the thermosphere's temperature \(T_\infty \approx 500 + 3.4\,F_{10.7}\) kelvin. An active Sun heats it, \(H\) grows, and more air reaches the satellite.

Drag \(D = \tfrac12 \rho v^2 C_D A\) takes energy from a circular orbit of radius \(a\), where \(v = \sqrt{\mu/a}\) and the energy is \(-\mu m/2a\). Equating the loss of energy to \(Dv\),

\[ \frac{da}{dt} = -\frac{C_D A}{m}\,\rho(a)\sqrt{\mu a}. \]

Separating and integrating from the start to 200 km gives the life. With an exponential \(\rho\), the integral is Dawson's integral; the lab also steps the orbit down in time and checks the two agree.

Worked example · a 6U craft at 500 km

Take a 12 kg craft with 0.06 m² facing the flow and \(C_D = 2.2\): \(C_D A/m = 0.011\) m²/kg. At \(F_{10.7} = 147\), \(T_\infty = 1000\) K and

\[ H = \frac{(1.38\times10^{-23})(1000)}{(2.66\times10^{-26})(8.94)} = 58.1\ \text{km}. \]

Anchored at the standard atmosphere's \(1.92\times10^{-11}\) kg/m³ at 300 km, the air at 500 km is \(1.92\times10^{-11}\,e^{-200/58.1} = 6.14\times10^{-13}\) kg/m³. The orbit starts falling 11 km a year and comes down in 5.2 years.

This model is 23% denser than the 1976 standard atmosphere at 400 km and 18% at 500 km, because it treats all the air above 300 km as oxygen at full temperature.

Worked example · a quiet Sun

At \(F_{10.7} = 70\) the thermosphere cools to 738 K and \(H\) shrinks to 42.9 km. The air at 500 km thins to \(1.81\times10^{-13}\) kg/m³, and the same craft lasts 13 years. At \(F_{10.7} = 250\) it lasts 2.8.

Common mistake

Taking the decay rate at the start and dividing the height by it. The air thickens as the orbit falls, so the decay speeds up: 500 km at 11 km a year suggests 27 years to reach 200 km, against 5.2.

Part II

Radiation

3Where the radiation comes from

Three sources reach a craft in low orbit. Each harms electronics in its own way.

Key idea

Trapped particles: protons and electrons held in the Van Allen belts by Earth's field. In low orbit they come mostly from the South Atlantic Anomaly, where the inner belt dips low. Solar particle events: bursts of protons from the Sun, lasting hours to days. Galactic cosmic rays: heavy nuclei from outside the solar system, too energetic to shield.

They do harm in two ways. Total ionizing dose builds up over the mission and slowly degrades a part; it is measured in rad. Single-event effects come from one particle: an upset flips a memory bit and does no lasting harm, while a latch-up opens a path that draws a destructive current.

Worked example · sorting the effects
  • A regulator's output drifts a little more each month: total dose.
  • A counter jumps by 2¹⁷ once, and a reload fixes it: a single-event upset.
  • A processor's current jumps and stays high until the power is cycled: a single-event latch-up.

Shielding slows dose. Codes and scrubbing handle upsets. Latch-up needs current limits and a power switch.

Common mistake

Expecting shielding to stop cosmic rays. They carry far too much energy for the millimetres a small craft can afford; their upsets are handled by design, not by mass.

4Total dose and shielding

Dose builds year by year. A part fails when its dose reaches its tolerance, and the model behind the dose is uncertain.

Key idea

Compare the dose the part will see, times a radiation design margin, with the dose it tolerates. NASA's reliability practice asks for a margin of 2. Commercial parts often tolerate 2 to 10 krad.

Worked example · a commercial part in a CubeSat

NASA's small spacecraft report gives, for a 500 km orbit at 85° behind the 0.2 cm aluminium of a typical CubeSat frame, 1383 rad a year from the trapped belts and about 750 rad from solar particle events: 2133 rad in all. With a margin of 2, a part must tolerate 4.27 krad for each year. A 5 krad part covers

\[ t = \frac{5000}{2 \times 2133} = 1.17\ \text{years}. \]

Behind 1.11 cm of aluminium the report gives 73.6 rad a year trapped, measured on orbit, and 183 rad from solar events: 257 rad. The same part then covers 9.7 years.

Common mistake

Leaving out the margin. The belt models below 840 km are extrapolated and uncertain by at least a factor of two, so a part rated just above the predicted dose can fail early.

5Single-event upsets and scrubbing

Upsets cannot be stopped, so a design lets them happen and corrects them.

Derivation

Each bit flips at random at \(\lambda\) per day. A code in each \(k\)-bit word corrects one flipped bit and detects two. A scrubber reads every word, corrects it and writes it back each \(T\) days. A word gathers upsets at \(k\lambda\), so the number in one interval is Poisson with mean \(x = k\lambda T\). Data is lost when two or more land first:

\[ P_2 = 1 - e^{-x}(1 + x) \approx \frac{x^2}{2}. \]

A memory of \(N\) bits holds \(N/k\) words, so it loses

\[ \frac{N}{k}\,\frac{P_2}{T} \approx \frac{N k \lambda^2 T}{2} \]

words a day. The losses go as the square of the rate and in proportion to the scrub interval.

Worked example · Cassini's rate in 1 Gbit

Cassini's recorders saw about 280 single-bit errors a day in 2.5 Gbit: \(\lambda = 1.12\times10^{-7}\) per bit per day. A 1 Gbit memory takes 112 upsets a day. With a code on 72-bit words and a daily scrub, \(x = 72 \times 1.12\times10^{-7} = 8.06\times10^{-6}\) and

\[ \frac{10^9}{72}\cdot\frac{(8.06\times10^{-6})^2}{2} = 4.52\times10^{-4}\ \text{a day}, \]

one lost word every 6.1 years.

Common mistake

Fitting the code and never scrubbing. Single upsets then pile up in the words that are seldom read, and two in one word cannot be corrected. The code needs the scrubber.

7Build it

Say what the environment will do to the craft, and show the design survives it.

Method

  1. State the orbit and the launch date, so you know where in the solar cycle the mission falls.
  2. Find the life from drag at a quiet and an active Sun, and check it against the disposal rule that applies.
  3. Find the yearly dose behind the craft's shielding, and the margin each part has.
  4. List the parts that can upset or latch up, and how each is handled: a code and scrubbing, current limits, a watchdog.
  5. Choose surface materials that resist atomic oxygen on the forward faces.

What to produce

An environment summary, an orbital life range, a dose table with margins for the key parts, and a single-event plan.

Handoff to other subsystems

Give Avionics the dose and upset rates. Give Structure the shielding thickness. Give Mission and requirements the orbital life against the disposal rule.

Review checklist

  • The solar conditions behind each number are stated.
  • The life is given at a quiet and an active Sun.
  • Every key part has a dose margin of at least 2.
  • Every memory has a code and a scrub interval.
  • Latch-up has a current limit and a way to cycle power.
Worked example · build review

For your orbit, give the life at both ends of the solar cycle, the yearly dose behind your frame, the margin on your processor, and the upset rate your memory will lose data at.

Use the checklist above to review the result. State each assumption and the evidence supporting each interface value.
