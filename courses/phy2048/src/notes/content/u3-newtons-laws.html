Part I

The three laws

1Force and the first law

A force is a push or a pull, with a size and a direction. Forces are vectors, so they add by components, as in Unit 1. The net force on a body is the vector sum of every force on it from outside.

The first law says that a body keeps its velocity, in size and direction, unless a net force acts on it. A body at rest stays at rest. A moving body keeps moving in a straight line at a steady speed. This tendency is inertia. A force is needed to change a motion, not to keep one going.

The law holds in an inertial frame: a frame that is not itself accelerating. The ground is nearly one. An accelerating car is not, which is why a coffee cup slides on its dashboard with no force pushing it.

Worked example · riding at a steady speed

An elevator carries a 75.0 kg man up at a steady speed. His velocity does not change, so the net force on him is zero. Two forces act: his weight, \(mg = 735.8\ \text{N}\) down, and the push of the floor up. The push must also be 735.8 N. Moving up does not need extra force; only speeding up does.

Common mistake

A steadily moving body does not need a net force in its direction of motion. A car cruising at 100 km/h has its forward push from the road exactly balanced by drag and rolling resistance. If the push were bigger, the car would speed up.

2The second law

A net force changes the velocity, and the change is larger for a smaller mass. The second law makes this exact:

\[ \sum\vec F = m\vec a. \]

It is a vector equation, so it is three equations, one per axis: \(\sum F_x = ma_x\), and the same for \(y\) and \(z\). The acceleration points the same way as the net force. The SI unit of force follows from it: one newton gives one kilogram an acceleration of \(1\ \text{m/s}^2\), so \(1\ \text{N} = 1\ \text{kg·m/s}^2\).

Worked example · a lawn mower

The push on a 24 kg mower, less the friction on it, is a net 51 N along the ground. The acceleration is \(a = F_{\text{net}}/m = 51/24 = 2.1\ \text{m/s}^2\), in the direction of the net force. Nothing here says how big the push or the friction is on its own; only their difference matters.

Worked example · several forces on a particle

Four forces act on a 4.0 kg particle: 10.0 N at \(30^\circ\) above \(+x\), 40.0 N down, 5.0 N along \(-x\) and 2.0 N up. Add each column:

\[ a_x = \frac{10.0\cos30^\circ - 5.0}{4.0} = 0.915\ \text{m/s}^2, \qquad a_y = \frac{10.0\sin30^\circ + 2.0 - 40.0}{4.0} = -8.25\ \text{m/s}^2. \]

The size is \(\sqrt{0.915^2 + 8.25^2} = 8.30\ \text{m/s}^2\), at \(276^\circ\) from \(+x\): almost straight down, a little to the right.

Common mistake

The book prints this acceleration's size as \(8.4\ \text{m/s}^2\). Its own components, 0.92 and \(-8.25\), give 8.30. Rounding \(a_y\) to \(-8.3\) and then squaring gives 8.35, which rounds to 8.4. Keep the extra digits until the end.

Forces do not add as numbers. Adding the four sizes, 57 N, and dividing by 4.0 kg gives \(14\ \text{m/s}^2\), far too big, because most of the forces point in different directions.

3Mass and weight

Mass measures inertia: how hard a body is to accelerate. It is the same everywhere. Weight is the gravitational pull on the body. In free fall it is the only force, and the body accelerates at \(g\), so the second law gives

\[ \vec w = m\vec g, \qquad w = mg. \]

A 5.0 kg body weighs 49 N on Earth and 8.4 N on the Moon, where \(g = 1.67\ \text{m/s}^2\). Its mass is 5.0 kg in both places. A pound is a unit of force: one pound-force is 4.448 N.

Worked example · lifting a stone

A farmer lifts a stone that weighs 180 N, so it speeds up at \(1.5\ \text{m/s}^2\). The mass is not 180: it is \(m = w/g = 180/9.81 = 18.35\ \text{kg}\). Two forces act, his pull \(F\) up and the weight down:

\[ F - w = ma \quad\Rightarrow\quad F = 180 + 18.35(1.5) = 207.5\ \text{N}. \]

If he pulls with 230.0 N instead, \(a = (230.0 - 180)/18.35 = 2.72\ \text{m/s}^2\).

Common mistake

The book's answer to that last question is \(2.78\ \text{m/s}^2\). It rounds the mass to 18 kg first: \(50/18 = 2.78\). With \(m = 18.35\ \text{kg}\) the acceleration is \(2.72\ \text{m/s}^2\), 2% less.

A weight in newtons is not a mass. Putting 180 in \(F = ma\) as the mass gives a force about ten times too big.

4The third law and choosing a system

Forces come in pairs. If body A pushes on body B, then B pushes on A with a force of the same size in the opposite direction:

\[ \vec F_{AB} = -\vec F_{BA}. \]

The two forces of a pair act on different bodies, so they never cancel each other in one body's equation. A swimmer pushes the wall backward; the wall pushes her forward, and that push is what moves her. A car's tyres push the road backward; the road pushes the car forward.

Which forces enter \(\sum\vec F = m\vec a\) depends on the system you choose. A force between two parts of the system is internal: its pair is inside the system too, and the two cancel. Only external forces count.

Worked example · a professor and a cart

A 65.0 kg professor pushes a 12.0 kg cart carrying 7.0 kg of equipment. She pushes back on the floor with 150 N, so the floor pushes her forward with 150 N. Friction on the cart totals 24.0 N. Take all three as the system: the forces between her and the cart are internal.

\[ a = \frac{150 - 24.0}{65.0 + 12.0 + 7.0} = \frac{126}{84.0} = 1.50\ \text{m/s}^2. \]

For her push on the cart, choose the cart and equipment alone. Now her push \(P\) is external: \(P - 24.0 = 19.0(1.50)\), so \(P = 52.5\ \text{N}\).

Common mistake

A book's weight and the table's push on it are not a third-law pair. Both act on the book, and they are equal only because the book does not accelerate: that is the second law. The weight's pair is the book's pull on Earth.

Part II

The forces you meet

5Normal force and tension

A surface pushes back on anything pressed against it. That push is the normal force \(\vec N\), perpendicular to the surface. It is as large as it needs to be to stop the body sinking in, no larger.

Derivation

On a slope at angle \(\theta\), tilt the axes to lie along and across the surface. The weight has a part \(mg\sin\theta\) down the slope and \(mg\cos\theta\) into it. Nothing moves into the surface, so across it the forces balance:

\[ N - mg\cos\theta = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad N = mg\cos\theta. \]

On level ground \(\theta = 0\) and \(N = mg\). On a slope \(N\) is less than the weight, and on a vertical wall it is zero.

Worked example · the normal force on a slope

A 60 kg snowboarder rides a \(13^\circ\) slope. Her weight is 588.6 N. Into the slope it has \(588.6\cos13^\circ = 573.5\ \text{N}\), and the slope pushes back with exactly that. Along it the weight has \(588.6\sin13^\circ = 132.4\ \text{N}\), which is what friction has to fight.

A string, rope or cable pulls along its own length. The size of that pull is the tension \(T\). A light string over a frictionless pulley has the same tension all along it.

Worked example · a tightrope and a traffic light

A 70.0 kg walker stands at the middle of a wire that sags \(5.0^\circ\) on each side. The two tensions are equal, and their vertical parts hold up the weight: \(2T\sin5.0^\circ = 686.7\ \text{N}\), so \(T = 3940\ \text{N}\). That is almost six times the weight. A small sag needs a huge tension.

A 15.0 kg traffic light hangs from wires at \(30^\circ\) and \(45^\circ\). Along \(x\), \(T_1\cos30^\circ = T_2\cos45^\circ\). Along \(y\), \(T_1\sin30^\circ + T_2\sin45^\circ = 147.2\ \text{N}\). Solving, \(T_1 = 107.7\ \text{N}\) and \(T_2 = 131.9\ \text{N}\). The steeper wire carries more.

Common mistake

On a slope the normal force is not \(mg\). Using \(N = mg\) overstates the friction by the factor \(1/\cos\theta\): 2.6% at \(13^\circ\), 41% at \(45^\circ\).

What the model leaves out

Strings and cables here have no mass and do not stretch, and pulleys have no mass or friction. A real cable sags under its own weight and stretches a little, and a real pulley takes some force to turn.

6Free-body diagrams

A free-body diagram shows one body alone, as a dot or a box, with every external force on it as an arrow from that point. Build it the same way each time. Draw the weight. Then add one force for each thing that touches the body: a normal force and, if rough, a friction force for each surface; a tension for each string. Add nothing else.

Worked example · two blocks on a ramp

Block B slides down a ramp. Block A sits on B's top face and is pulled up the ramp by a rope. Block A touches only B: its diagram has its weight, the tension, a normal force from B, and friction from B down the ramp, against its slide relative to B.

Block B touches two surfaces, the ramp and block A. Its diagram has its weight, a normal force and friction from the ramp, and a normal force and friction from A. The friction from A on B points up the ramp: it is the third-law pair of B's friction on A. The rope is tied to A, so it does not appear on B.

Key idea

Every force on a free-body diagram has a source you can name: Earth, or something touching the body. A force without a source does not belong.

Common mistake

The force of a throw does not travel with the ball. Once it leaves the hand, a ball in flight has only its weight on it, if air drag is ignored.

Do not draw a centrifugal force on a body moving in a circle. In the ground's frame nothing pushes outward. The net force points inward, toward the centre (Section 10).

Part III

Solving problems

7Connected bodies

When bodies are tied together, draw each alone and write the second law for each. A light string makes their accelerations equal in size and carries one tension. The equations share \(a\) and \(T\), and adding them removes \(T\).

Worked example · a block on a table and a hanging block

A 5.00 kg block on a smooth table is tied over a pulley to a 3.00 kg block hanging below. For the table block, \(T = m_1a\). For the hanging block, down positive, \(m_2g - T = m_2a\). Add them:

\[ a = \frac{m_2g}{m_1 + m_2} = \frac{3.00(9.81)}{8.00} = 3.68\ \text{m/s}^2, \qquad T = m_1a = 18.4\ \text{N}. \]

The hanging block weighs 29.4 N, but the string pulls it up with only 18.4 N. The difference, 11.0 N, is what accelerates it.

Worked example · an Atwood machine

Blocks of 2.00 kg and 4.00 kg hang on either side of a pulley. Up positive for the light one: \(T - m_1g = m_1a\) and \(T - m_2g = -m_2a\). Subtract:

\[ a = \frac{(m_2 - m_1)g}{m_1 + m_2} = \frac{2.00(9.81)}{6.00} = 3.27\ \text{m/s}^2, \qquad T = m_1(g + a) = 26.2\ \text{N}. \]

The tension lies between the two weights, 19.6 N and 39.2 N. Double both masses and \(a\) does not change, but \(T\) doubles.

Worked example · a tractor pulling carts

A 650.0 kg baggage tractor pulls carts of 250.0 kg and 150.0 kg from rest. The ground pushes it forward with \(F = 820.0t\) N. For the whole train, \(a = 820.0t/1050 = 0.7810t\ \text{m/s}^2\). The acceleration grows, so integrate: \(v = 0.3905t^2\), and after 3.00 s it is moving at 3.51 m/s.

The cable to the first cart pulls both carts, so at 3.00 s its tension is \(T = (250.0 + 150.0)(0.7810)(3.00) = 937\ \text{N}\). The book prints 938 N, because it uses \(a = 0.7805t\) in that step: \(820/1050\) is 0.7810.

Common mistake

The tension is not the hanging weight while the system accelerates. Setting \(T = m_2g\) leaves the hanging block no net force, so it could not speed up. The tension equals the weight only when nothing accelerates.

What the model leaves out

A real pulley has mass and must be spun up, so the tensions on its two sides differ. Unit 6 adds that.

8Apparent weight

A bathroom scale reads the force it pushes up with, the normal force on you. In an accelerating elevator that is not your weight. With up positive, the second law for you is

\[ N - mg = ma \quad\Rightarrow\quad N = m(g + a). \]

The scale reads more than your weight while your acceleration points up, less while it points down, and zero in free fall. What matters is the direction of the acceleration, not of the motion.

Worked example · a scale in a rising elevator

A 75.0 kg man stands on a scale in an elevator that speeds up going upward at \(1.20\ \text{m/s}^2\). The scale reads \(N = 75.0(9.81 + 1.20) = 825.8\ \text{N}\). A scale marked in kilograms divides by \(g\) and shows 84.2 kg. Once the elevator climbs at a steady speed, it reads 735.8 N, his weight.

Worked example · slowing on the way down

The same elevator goes down and slows to stop at \(1.20\ \text{m/s}^2\). Slowing a downward motion is an upward acceleration, \(a = +1.20\ \text{m/s}^2\). The scale reads 825.8 N, the same as when it sped up on the way up.

Common mistake

Going down does not make the scale read less. Speeding up on the way down does; slowing down on the way down makes it read more. At any steady speed it reads the weight.

What the course model leaves out

A real elevator ramps its acceleration in and out smoothly, so the reading slides from one value to the next. A spring scale also bounces for a moment after each change.

9Friction

Friction acts along a surface, against the sliding of one body over another, or against the slide that would happen without it. It comes in two kinds.

Static friction holds a body at rest. It takes whatever value is needed, up to a limit: \(f_s \le \mu_sN\). Kinetic friction acts once the body slides. It has one value, \(f_k = \mu_kN\), against the sliding. Usually \(\mu_k\) is less than \(\mu_s\): it is harder to start a crate moving than to keep it moving.

Derivation

A body rests on a slope. It stays put while static friction can match the pull down the slope: \(mg\sin\theta \le \mu_smg\cos\theta\). The mass cancels, leaving

\[ \tan\theta \le \mu_s. \]

Once it slides, along the slope \(mg\sin\theta - \mu_kmg\cos\theta = ma\), so \(a = g(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta)\). The mass cancels again: a heavy box and a light one slide alike.

Worked example · a snowboarder and a skier

A snowboarder rides a \(13^\circ\) slope with \(\mu_k = 0.20\): \(a = 9.81(\sin13^\circ - 0.20\cos13^\circ) = 0.295\ \text{m/s}^2\). On a \(10^\circ\) slope the same snow gives \(-0.229\ \text{m/s}^2\): she slows down.

A 62 kg skier on a \(25^\circ\) slope feels 45.0 N of friction. The normal force is \(62(9.81)\cos25^\circ = 551.2\ \text{N}\), so \(\mu_k = 45.0/551.2 = 0.082\).

Worked example · a box that will not stay put

A box is set down on a \(25^\circ\) ramp with \(\mu_s = 0.30\). Since \(\tan25^\circ = 0.466\) is more than 0.30, static friction cannot hold it, and it slides. A box five times as heavy slides too: the mass does not enter the test.

Worked example · a crate on an accelerating truck

A 50.0 kg crate rides on a truck bed with \(\mu_s = 0.400\) and \(\mu_k = 0.300\). Friction is the only horizontal force on it. To ride along at \(2.00\ \text{m/s}^2\) it needs \(50.0(2.00) = 100\ \text{N}\), less than the \(\mu_smg = 196\ \text{N}\) available, so it holds.

At \(5.00\ \text{m/s}^2\) it would need 250 N, more than 196 N, so it slips. Friction becomes kinetic, \(0.300(490.5) = 147\ \text{N}\), and the crate accelerates at \(2.94\ \text{m/s}^2\). It slides back across the bed at \(2.06\ \text{m/s}^2\).

Common mistake

Whether a body starts to slide is decided by \(\mu_s\), not \(\mu_k\). Once it slides, its acceleration uses \(\mu_k\).

Static friction is not always \(\mu_sN\). That is its largest value. A crate at rest on a gentle ramp has exactly \(mg\sin\theta\) of friction, often much less.

What the model leaves out

A coefficient of friction is not a fixed number. It changes with the surfaces, their cleanliness, the temperature and, a little, the speed. Values are good to a digit or two.

Part IV

Curves and drag

10Centripetal force

Unit 2 showed that motion on a circle at speed \(v\) has an acceleration \(v^2/r\) toward the centre. The second law then says a net force must point there:

\[ F_c = \frac{mv^2}{r} = m\omega^2r. \]

This centripetal force is not a new kind of force. It is the net of the real forces: friction, a normal force, tension or gravity.

Worked example · a car on a flat curve

A 900.0 kg car takes a flat 500.0 m curve at 25.00 m/s. It needs \(F_c = 900.0(25.00)^2/500.0 = 1125\ \text{N}\) toward the centre. On a flat road only friction points that way. Static friction can give at most \(\mu_smg\), so

\[ \mu_s \ge \frac{v^2}{rg} = \frac{625}{500.0(9.81)} = 0.127. \]

The mass cancels. At twice the speed the car needs four times the friction: \(\mu_s \ge 0.51\).

Derivation

On a road banked at \(\theta\), the normal force leans toward the centre. With no friction, its horizontal part must give \(mv^2/r\) and its vertical part must hold up \(mg\):

\[ N\sin\theta = \frac{mv^2}{r}, \qquad N\cos\theta = mg \quad\Rightarrow\quad \tan\theta = \frac{v^2}{rg}. \]

At that one speed the curve needs no friction. Faster, friction must point down the bank; slower, up it. Friction turns the single design speed into a band.

Worked example · a steeply banked curve

A 100.0 m curve is banked at \(31.0^\circ\). Its design speed is \(v = \sqrt{rg\tan\theta} = \sqrt{100.0(9.81)(0.6009)} = 24.3\ \text{m/s}\), about 87 km/h. At that speed a car holds the curve even on ice.

Worked example · the top of a loop

A 40.0 kg rider passes upside down over the top of a 7.00 m loop at 10.0 m/s. At the top the centre is below her, so the seat's push \(N\) and her weight both point to it: \(N + mg = mv^2/r\). So \(N = 40.0(100/7.00 - 9.81) = 179\ \text{N}\). She leaves the seat when \(N\) would drop below zero, at \(v = \sqrt{gr} = 8.29\ \text{m/s}\).

Common mistake

The book writes \(\tan31.0^\circ = 0.6009\) and then puts 0.609 into the next line. Its answer, 24.3 m/s, comes from 0.6009. With 0.609 the speed would be 24.4 m/s.

There is no outward centrifugal force in the ground's frame. A passenger pressed against the outer door is being pushed inward by the door. His inertia carries him straight while the car turns.

What the model leaves out

The car is a point. A real car shifts its load to the outer wheels and can tip before it slides, and its tyres grip with a slip angle that this model ignores.

11Drag and terminal speed

A body moving through air or water feels drag, a force against its motion that grows with its speed. For people, cars and balls in air it grows as the square of the speed:

\[ F_D = \tfrac12\rho CAv^2, \]

with \(\rho\) the fluid's density, \(A\) the area facing the flow, and \(C\) the drag coefficient, a number near 1 measured in a wind tunnel. For small, slow bodies in a liquid, drag is closer to \(F = bv\).

Derivation

A body falls from rest. Down positive, \(m\,dv/dt = mg - \tfrac12\rho CAv^2\). At first drag is small and it speeds up at nearly \(g\). As \(v\) grows the drag grows, until it equals the weight. Then the net force is zero and the speed stops changing: the terminal speed,

\[ \tfrac12\rho CAv_t^2 = mg \quad\Rightarrow\quad v_t = \sqrt{\frac{2mg}{\rho CA}}. \]

Separating and integrating the equation gives \(v = v_t\tanh(gt/v_t)\). A body that starts faster than \(v_t\) has more drag than weight and slows toward it.

Worked example · a skydiver, spread-eagle

An 85 kg skydiver falls spread-eagle with \(A = 0.70\ \text{m}^2\) and \(C = 1.0\) through air of density \(1.21\ \text{kg/m}^3\):

\[ v_t = \sqrt{\frac{2(85)(9.81)}{1.21(1.0)(0.70)}} = 44.4\ \text{m/s} = 160\ \text{km/h}. \]

A 50 kg skydiver in the same position reaches \(\sqrt{50/85} \times 44.4 = 34.0\ \text{m/s}\).

Worked example · head first

A 75 kg skydiver diving head first presents only \(A = 0.18\ \text{m}^2\), with \(C = 0.70\). Now \(CA = 0.126\ \text{m}^2\), and \(v_t = 98.2\ \text{m/s}\), about 354 km/h. Shrinking the drag area is how a diver catches a partner below.

Worked example · a bead sinking in oil

A 0.050 kg bead sinks in thick oil with linear drag, \(b = 0.50\ \text{kg/s}\). Down positive, \(m\,dv/dt = mg - bv\). Integrate from rest: \(v = v_t(1 - e^{-t/\tau})\), with \(v_t = mg/b = 0.981\ \text{m/s}\) and \(\tau = m/b = 0.10\ \text{s}\). After one time constant it has \(1 - 1/e = 63\%\) of \(v_t\): 0.620 m/s.

The same law slows a coasting boat. With the motor off, \(m\,dv/dt = -bv\) gives \(v = v_0e^{-bt/m}\). A boat that drops from 4.0 to 1.0 m/s in 10 s has \(b/m = \ln4/10 = 0.139\ \text{s}^{-1}\), and it coasts at most \(v_0m/b = 28.9\ \text{m}\).

Common mistake

At terminal speed the acceleration is zero, not \(g\). The body still falls, but at a constant velocity: drag has grown to match the weight.

Twice the mass does not double a quadratic terminal speed. Since \(v_t \propto \sqrt m\), it grows by \(\sqrt2\). Four times the area halves it.

What the model leaves out

The drag coefficient changes with speed and shape, and a falling body may turn. The air is thinner high up, so a jumper's terminal speed falls as she descends. Buoyancy is left out, which matters for a bead in oil.
