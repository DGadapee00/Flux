1Model and interpretation

Key relation
\[E_n=-\frac{13.605693\ \mathrm{eV}}{n^2},\quad E_\gamma=E_i-E_f\]

Discrete atomic energies produce discrete transition energies. An emitted photon carries the difference between two levels. The reference zero is the separated electron and proton, so bound-state energies are negative.

Work the model

  1. Label the initial and final levels. Emission has initial energy greater than final energy.
  2. Subtract signed energies before converting the positive difference into wavelength.
  3. Distinguish the size of a level energy from the difference of two level energies.
  4. Treat the Bohr picture as a historical model. Modern atomic states are wavefunctions, not literal planet-like electron trajectories.
Worked example · reason through the setup

In the infinite-nuclear-mass hydrogen model, a transition from n = 4 to n = 2 releases about 2.551 eV, corresponding to a vacuum wavelength near 486.0 nm. The upper and lower energies are both negative; their difference is positive.

Common mistake

A negative level energy does not mean a photon has negative energy.

Common mistake

One successful hydrogen spectrum model does not make the same formula valid for every many-electron atom.

3Limits and further study

What the model leaves out

This model neglects finite proton mass, fine and hyperfine structure, external fields and selection rules. Wavelengths are idealized vacuum values.

Worked reflection · transfer the idea

Hold the lower level fixed and raise the upper level. Explain why the emitted photon energies approach a series limit.

Write a prediction, identify the quantity held fixed and test the prediction in the lab. Name one additional physical effect that would require a different model.
