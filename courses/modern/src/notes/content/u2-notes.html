1Model and interpretation

Key relation
\[E_\gamma=hf=hc/\lambda,\quad K_{\max}=hf-\phi\]

A photon transfers energy in one absorption event. A surface needs a minimum energy to release an electron. Above threshold, the remaining energy can appear as electron kinetic energy.

Constants are rounded from NIST CODATA 2022. Keep extra digits during calculation and round the final answer.

Work the model

  1. Convert wavelength to metres if using h in joule-seconds. An electron-volt is an energy; multiply by the elementary charge to express it in joules.
  2. Compare photon energy with the work function before subtracting. A below-threshold result means no photoemission in this ideal single-photon model.
  3. For emitted electrons, divide maximum kinetic energy in joules by the elementary charge to obtain the stopping-potential magnitude in volts.
  4. Separate photon energy from photon arrival rate. Increasing ideal intensity at fixed wavelength changes the number of incident photons, not the energy per photon.
Worked example · reason through the setup

For an illustrative surface with a 3.0 eV work function and 250 nm illumination, photon energy is about 4.959 eV. The ideal maximum electron energy is 1.959 eV and the stopping-potential magnitude is 1.959 V. These are chosen classroom values, not a measured material certificate.

Common mistake

A negative calculated kinetic energy is not an emitted electron moving backward. It indicates that the threshold was not met.

Common mistake

The readout shows a stopping-potential magnitude. The actual electrode polarity must oppose the emitted electrons.

3Limits and further study

What the model leaves out

The model omits contact potentials, surface contamination, energy distributions, multiphoton processes and the current-voltage curve.

Worked reflection · transfer the idea

Keep the work function fixed and compare shorter wavelength with greater intensity. Explain which changes maximum electron energy and which can change ideal photocurrent.
