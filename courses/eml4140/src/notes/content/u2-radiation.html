Part I

Emission

1Black bodies

Every surface above absolute zero emits electromagnetic waves, and needs no medium to do it. A black body emits the most any surface can at each wavelength.

Derivation

Planck's law gives the black body's emission per unit wavelength:

\[ E_{b\lambda} = \frac{C_1}{\lambda^5\left(e^{C_2/\lambda T} - 1\right)}, \qquad C_1 = 3.742\times10^8\ \text{W·µm}^4/\text{m}^2, \quad C_2 = 1.4388\times10^4\ \text{µm·K}. \]

Integrating over all wavelengths gives the Stefan–Boltzmann law, \(E_b = \sigma T^4\) with \(\sigma = 5.670\times10^{-8}\) W/(m²·K⁴). Setting \(dE_{b\lambda}/d\lambda = 0\) gives Wien's law, \(\lambda_\text{max}T = 2898\) µm·K: hotter bodies peak at shorter wavelengths.

Worked example · the Sun

The Sun's surface is near 5778 K. Its peak is at \(2898/5778 = 0.502\) µm, in the visible, and it emits \(\sigma(5778)^4 = 6.32\times10^7\) W/m². A room-temperature wall at 300 K peaks at 9.7 µm, deep in the infrared, and emits 459 W/m².

Common mistake

Putting temperatures in degrees Celsius into \(\sigma T^4\) or Wien's law. Both need kelvin.

2Real surfaces in sunlight

A real surface emits a fraction \(\varepsilon_\lambda\) of a black body's emission at each wavelength, and absorbs the same fraction of what falls on it there. Because sunlight is short-wave and a room-temperature surface glows long-wave, one surface can absorb sunlight poorly and emit its own heat well.

Derivation

The solar absorptivity \(\alpha_s\) weighs \(\varepsilon_\lambda\) by the Sun's spectrum; the emissivity \(\varepsilon\) weighs it by the surface's own. A plate facing the Sun with its back insulated settles where it absorbs as much as it emits:

\[ \alpha_s S = \varepsilon\,\sigma T^4 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T = \left(\frac{\alpha_s}{\varepsilon}\,\frac{S}{\sigma}\right)^{1/4}, \qquad S = 1361\ \text{W/m}^2. \]
Worked example · a plate in sunlight

A black-painted plate (\(\alpha_s = \varepsilon = 0.95\)) settles at \((1361/5.67\times10^{-8})^{1/4} = 394\) K. Model white paint as emissivity 0.2 below 3 µm and 0.9 above: then \(\alpha_s = 0.215\) and \(\varepsilon = 0.90\), so \(T = 394\,(0.215/0.90)^{1/4} = 275\) K. Polished aluminum, 0.15 and 0.05, reaches 512 K: it absorbs little but can shed even less.

Key idea

The ratio \(\alpha_s/\varepsilon\) sets a sunlit surface's temperature. Spacecraft radiators use coatings with low \(\alpha_s\) and high \(\varepsilon\); solar collectors use the reverse.
