Part I

Conduction in time

1The heat equation

Heat flows down a temperature gradient: Fourier's law says the flux is \(q = -k\,\partial T/\partial x\). Where more heat flows into a slice than out, the slice warms.

Derivation

Take a slice of a rod, of area \(A\) and thickness \(dx\). The heat stored per second is \(\rho c A\,dx\,\partial T/\partial t\). The net heat in is the flux in at \(x\) minus the flux out at \(x + dx\), which is \(-A\,(\partial q/\partial x)\,dx\). Set them equal and use Fourier's law:

\[ \rho c\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = k\,\frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial x^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \alpha\,\frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial x^2}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{k}{\rho c}. \]

In three dimensions the same balance on a small volume, with the divergence theorem, gives \(\partial T/\partial t = \alpha\nabla^2 T\). The diffusivity \(\alpha\) says how fast temperature spreads: copper's is 117 mm²/s, carbon steel's 17.7 mm²/s.

Worked example · a copper rod cools to half

A copper rod 10 cm long has its ends held at 20 °C. Its middle starts 80 °C hotter, in a half-sine profile. Section 2 shows this profile keeps its shape and sinks as \(e^{-\pi^2 \alpha t/L^2}\). It is half gone when

\[ \pi^2\frac{\alpha t}{L^2} = \ln 2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t = \frac{\ln 2}{\pi^2}\,\frac{L^2}{\alpha} = 0.0702\times\frac{(0.1)^2}{1.17\times10^{-4}} = 6.0\ \text{s}. \]

The middle is then at \(20 + 40 = 60\) °C. In carbon steel the same takes 39.7 s.

Common mistake

Scaling the time with \(L\). It scales with \(L^2\): a rod twice as long takes four times as long.

2Sines that fade

With both ends held at the same temperature, measure \(\theta = (T - T_\text{end})/\Delta T_0\). Each sine that is zero at both ends is a solution on its own.

Derivation

Try \(\theta = \sin(n\pi x/L)\,g(t)\). The equation gives \(g' = -\alpha(n\pi/L)^2 g\), so \(g = e^{-(n\pi)^2\mathrm{Fo}}\) with the Fourier number \(\mathrm{Fo} = \alpha t/L^2\). Any starting profile is a sum of these sines, with coefficients \(b_n = 2\int_0^1 f(s)\sin(n\pi s)\,ds\):

\[ \theta(x, t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} b_n \sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\;e^{-(n\pi)^2\mathrm{Fo}}. \]

Mode \(n\) fades \(n^2\) times faster than the first. Sharp features need short sines, so they go first.

Worked example · the ripple and the hump

Start the copper rod from a half sine with a ripple on it: \(\theta_0 = \sin(\pi s) + \tfrac13\sin(5\pi s)\). At \(\mathrm{Fo} = 0.02\), which is 1.71 s,

\[ e^{-\pi^2(0.02)} = 0.821, \qquad e^{-25\pi^2(0.02)} = 0.0072. \]

The hump keeps 82% and the ripple keeps 0.7%. At the middle, where \(\sin(5\pi/2) = 1\), \(\theta = 0.821 + 0.0024 = 0.823\), so \(T = 20 + 80(0.823) = 85.9\) °C.

3Stepping on a grid

A computer does not need the series. Cut the rod into cells and replace each derivative by a difference.

Derivation \[ \frac{\theta_i^{\,new} - \theta_i}{\Delta t} = \alpha\,\frac{\theta_{i+1} - 2\theta_i + \theta_{i-1}}{\Delta x^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta_i^{\,new} = \theta_i + r\,(\theta_{i+1} - 2\theta_i + \theta_{i-1}), \qquad r = \frac{\alpha\,\Delta t}{\Delta x^2}. \]

Each cell moves toward the average of its neighbours. The step is stable only for \(r \le \tfrac12\). Past that, the shortest wiggle on the grid grows instead of fading.

Worked example · a tent on 80 cells

Start from a tent, 1 at the middle and 0 at the ends. Its corner needs many sines; their coefficients are \(b_n = 8\sin(n\pi/2)/(n\pi)^2\). At \(\mathrm{Fo} = 0.01\) the series gives \(\theta = 0.774\) at the middle. Stepping 80 cells with \(r = 0.4\) takes 160 steps and also gives 0.774. The two routes share nothing but the equation.

Key idea

The series shows why the profile fades as it does. The grid works for any shape, any material that changes along the rod, and any boundary. Checking one against the other is how a solver is trusted.
