Part I

Build the model

1Components and the resultant

A force has magnitude, direction and a point of application. Choose axes before resolving it. The right triangle formed by a planar force gives \(F_x=F\cos\theta\) and \(F_y=F\sin\theta\), when the angle starts at positive x. A component is signed. It is not a second force added to the original one.

Add forces head to tail. Their x displacements add independently of their y displacements. Therefore \(R_x=\sum F_x\) and \(R_y=\sum F_y\). Applying Pythagoras to that resultant gives \(R=\sqrt{R_x^2+R_y^2}\). Use \(\operatorname{atan2}(R_y,R_x)\) for its direction. Ordinary inverse tangent loses the quadrant.

Derivation · the parallelogram

Put the first force on positive x. The second contributes \(F_2\cos\theta\) horizontally and \(F_2\sin\theta\) vertically. Square the component sums and use \(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1\).

\[R^2=(F_1+F_2\cos\theta)^2+(F_2\sin\theta)^2=F_1^2+F_2^2+2F_1F_2\cos\theta.\]

The force triangle's interior angle is supplementary to the given angle between forces. This explains the plus sign.

Common mistake

Do not add force magnitudes unless the forces point along the same ray. Opposite forces subtract. Perpendicular ones combine by Pythagoras.

Worked example · Parallelogram

For 3000 lb and 2000 lb separated by 60°, resolve first: \(R_x=4000\) lb and \(R_y=1732.05\) lb. Then \(R=4358.90\) lb and \(\phi=23.413°\). The answer lies between the force directions, closer to the larger force.

Worked example · Three directions

Take 300 N at 0°, 200 N at 120°, and 150 N at 250°. Signed sums give \(R_x=148.697\) N and \(R_y=32.251\) N. Thus \(R=152.154\) N at \(12.237°\). The negative x component of the third force matters.

What the model leaves out

Concurrent forces can be replaced by a single resultant. For separated forces, their moments must also be preserved.

2Direction in three dimensions

Coordinates describe geometry before they describe force. From A to B, subtract tail from head: \(\mathbf r_{AB}=\mathbf r_B-\mathbf r_A\). Its length follows from three-dimensional Pythagoras. Dividing by that length gives a vector of magnitude one. \[\hat{\mathbf u}_{AB}=\frac{\mathbf r_{AB}}{\sqrt{(x_B-x_A)^2+(y_B-y_A)^2+(z_B-z_A)^2}},\qquad \mathbf F=F\hat{\mathbf u}_{AB}.\]

A cable pulls the attachment at A toward B. At B, the force reverses. The coordinate direction angles are measured from the positive axes, so \(\cos\alpha=F_x/F\), with corresponding expressions for beta and gamma. Squaring and adding gives \(\cos^2\alpha+\cos^2\beta+\cos^2\gamma=1\). This is a check, not another independent equation.

Common mistake

A negative component gives an obtuse coordinate direction angle. Do not erase that sign or use the angle to the negative axis.

Worked example · Cartesian angles

For \(\mathbf F=(34.3,-22.9,-68.6)\) lb, its length is \(80.0366\) lb. Divide each component by this length, then take inverse cosine: \(\alpha=64.624°\), \(\beta=106.625°\), \(\gamma=148.993°\). Both negative components give obtuse angles.

Worked example · Cable direction

A 700 N cable runs from the origin to (2,3,0) m. Its length is \(\sqrt{13}\) m, so \(\mathbf F=700(2,3,0)/\sqrt{13}=(388.29,582.44,0)\) N. Check the vector magnitude: it returns 700 N.

What the model leaves out

A straight, massless cable has one direction. A hanging cable with significant self-weight needs a curved-cable model.

Part II

Use and check the model

3Projection and a lifting bridle

The dot product extracts the part of a force along a chosen direction. For a unit direction \(\hat{\mathbf u}\), the signed scalar component is \(F_\parallel=\mathbf F\cdot\hat{\mathbf u}\). The vector projection is \(F_\parallel\hat{\mathbf u}\). Subtract it from the original force to obtain the perpendicular vector. Since these parts are orthogonal, \(F_\perp=\sqrt{F^2-F_\parallel^2}\).

For a symmetric lifting bridle, horizontal sling components cancel. Each vertical component carries half the load. Hence \(2T\sin\theta=W\), where theta is above horizontal. The inward force on each lifting point is \(H=T\cos\theta=(W/2)\cot\theta\). As theta approaches zero, both T and H grow without bound. The pallet weight has not changed; the geometry has.

Derivation · why the dot product measures a component

Rotate coordinates until one axis follows the requested direction. In that basis, only the component parallel to the axis contributes. Rotations preserve the dot product, so the same scalar in the original basis is \(F_xu_x+F_yu_y+F_zu_z\). Writing the force angle as psi gives \(F_\parallel=F\cos\psi\). Squaring the perpendicular difference gives \(|\mathbf F-F_\parallel\hat{\mathbf u}|^2=F^2-F_\parallel^2\), because the unit direction has length one.

At 90°, the scalar projection vanishes. At an obtuse angle it is negative. The perpendicular magnitude remains nonnegative in both cases. A projection is not generally a force's Cartesian x component; that happens only when the chosen direction is the x axis.

Common mistake

A projection onto an unnormalised direction vector is too large by that vector’s length. A signed scalar projection can be negative.

Worked example · Parallel and perpendicular

Project (100,200,0) N onto direction (2,0,0). Normalise to (1,0,0). The signed component is 100 N, the perpendicular magnitude is 200 N, and their angle is \(\tan^{-1}(2)=63.435°\).

Worked example · Empty pallet

EPAL gives an approximate pallet mass of 25 kg. With \(g=9.81\) m/s², \(W=245.25\) N. At 30° above horizontal, \(T=245.25\) N in each sling and \(H=212.393\) N. Each sling carries the whole weight in tension, although its vertical component carries only half.

What the model leaves out

The bridle assumes equal lengths, a centred load, and no lifting acceleration. It does not establish a safe working load.
