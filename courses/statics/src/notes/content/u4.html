Part I

Build the model

1Static friction, impending motion and tipping

Dry static friction adjusts to the force needed for equilibrium, subject to \(|F|\le\mu_sN\). Equality holds only at impending slip. First solve equilibrium, then compare the required friction with the limit. If the limit is exceeded, the assumed rest state cannot exist. Kinetic friction uses a different coefficient and does not restore static equilibrium.

For a push parallel to an incline, \(N=W\cos\theta\). At impending upward motion, friction points down and \(P_{up}=W(\sin\theta+\mu_s\cos\theta)\). At impending downward motion it points up and \(P_{hold}=\max[0,W(\sin\theta-\mu_s\cos\theta)]\). The interval between the thresholds contains the resting states. Without a push, the limiting slope obeys \(\tan\theta=\mu_s\).

A horizontal push on a crate can cause tipping first. At the threshold, the normal resultant reaches the front edge. Moments there give \(P_{tip}h_p=Wb/2\). Compare with \(P_{slip}=\mu_sW\). Whichever is smaller controls. Increasing friction can change the failure mode from sliding to tipping.

Common mistake

Friction is not always μN. That is a limiting value for static friction, not the force in every resting state.

Worked example · Incline band

For W = 100 lb, theta = 35° and μs = 0.30, the normal is 81.9152 lb. Upward impending motion needs \(P=57.3576+24.5746=81.9322\) lb. To prevent downward slip needs \(P=32.7831\) lb. Between these values the friction adjusts.

Worked example · Tip before slip

A 200 lb crate has b = 2 ft, push height 3 ft and μs = 0.35. Sliding needs 70 lb. Tipping needs \(200(2)/(2\cdot3)=66.6667\) lb. Tipping occurs first, so the assumed all-base contact fails before the friction limit.

What the model leaves out

Friction coefficients vary with surfaces and contamination. A rigid flat-base model ignores rocking and floor irregularities.

2Belt friction and the bookcase

For a small rope element on a post, radial balance gives \(dN\approx T\,d\theta\). Tangential balance at impending slip gives \(dT=\mu\,dN\). Divide by T and integrate: \(\ln(T_{tight}/T_{slack})=\mu\beta\). Thus \(T_{tight}=T_{slack}e^{\mu\beta}\), with beta in radians. Below impending slip this is an inequality, not an exact force ratio.

The bookcase lab uses the depth, not the front-view width, for forward tipping. A narrow footprint gives little restoring moment. Weight must act through the actual centre of gravity; contents can shift it. The model explains why manufacturers require anchoring even when floor friction is large.

Common mistake

One full wrap is 2π radians. Substituting 360 into an exponential intended for radians gives an absurd answer.

Worked example · Capstan

A 50 lb holding force with μ = 0.30 and half a wrap has \(\beta=\pi\). Therefore \(T_{tight}=50e^{0.3\pi}=128.317\) lb. A full wrap squares the ratio, rather than doubling it.

Worked example · Tall bookcase

The cited bookcase depth is 0.28 m. For assumed W = 400 N, push height 1.5 m and μs = 0.30, \(P_{tip}=400(0.14)/1.5=37.3333\) N, while \(P_{slip}=120\) N. The unanchored model tips first.

What the model leaves out

The rope model ignores stretch and nonuniform contact. The bookcase model omits the wall anchor and assumes centred weight.

Part II

Use and check the model

3Cuts, shear and bending moment

Find external reactions before making a cut. Replace the removed part by an axial force N, shear V and bending couple M. Use the course convention: the plotted V is the sum of upward external forces to the left, and positive M is sagging. On the retained left segment, positive internal shear acts downward at the cut and positive internal moment acts counterclockwise.

Equilibrium of a short segment with downward load intensity w gives \(dV/dx=-w\). Moment balance gives \(dM/dx=V\). Therefore the area under the load diagram changes shear; the area under shear changes moment. A point force makes a shear jump. An applied couple makes a moment jump. Neither is spread over a finite interval.

Under uniform loading, symmetry gives \(A_y=B_y=wL/2\). Cutting at x gives \(V=wL/2-wx\) and \(M=wLx/2-wx^2/2\). The smooth maximum occurs where V vanishes, at L/2, giving \(M_{max}=wL^2/8\). At a point load or applied couple, also check either side of the discontinuity.

Construct a diagram without guessing its shape

Mark every support, point force, distributed-load boundary and applied couple. Work interval by interval. On an unloaded interval, V is constant and M is linear. Under constant downward w, V is linear and M is quadratic. Under a linearly increasing load, V is quadratic and M is cubic. These statements follow by integrating the differential balances, not from memorising sketches.

Across a downward point force P, \(V(x^+)-V(x^-)=-P\). For a counterclockwise external couple C, the convention used here gives \(M(x^+)-M(x^-)=-C\). At a free end without an applied tip force or couple, both V and M vanish. A simple support does not always mean zero moment: an overhanging continuous member can carry moment across a support. A true unloaded internal hinge does impose zero transmitted moment.

For a cantilever with a tip load P, cut a distance x from its fixed end and retain the free-end side. The moment magnitude is \(P(L-x)\). It grows toward the wall. The wall's reaction couple is counterclockwise, while the internal moment diagram is negative for the downward tip load. External reaction moment and internal bending moment describe different free bodies.

Common mistake

A load to the right of the cut does not appear on the left-segment free body. Its effect is already present through the support reactions.

Worked example · Point-load cut

A 12 ft span carries 600 lb at x = 4 ft. Reactions are 400 lb and 200 lb. Cut at x = 2 ft, before the load. Thus N = 0, V = 400 lb and \(M=400(2)=800\) lb·ft. Past the point load, V becomes −200 lb.

Worked example · Uniform-load diagrams

On a 10 ft span at 120 lb/ft, each reaction is 600 lb. At x = 3 ft, \(V=600-360=240\) lb and \(M=600(3)-120(3)^2/2=1260\) lb·ft. The peak is 1500 lb·ft at midspan.

What the model leaves out

This section computes internal resultants, not stress or deflection. Those require geometry and material properties.

4Centroids, holes and Pappus

A centroid preserves the first moment of an area. Imagine a thin uniform plate in gravity. Balancing moments gives \(\bar x A=\int x\,dA\), hence \(\bar x=\int x\,dA/A\), and similarly for y. Partitioning the integral gives \(\bar x=\sum A_i x_i/\sum A_i\). The coordinates x_i belong to each part's centroid in the same global coordinate system.

A hole contributes negative area and negative first moment. Removing material on the right moves the remaining centroid left. Do not subtract the hole in the numerator but add it in the denominator. A line centroid uses length, an area centroid uses area, and a volume centroid uses volume.

Pappus follows by summing circular travel paths. Rotating a small area dA at distance r sweeps \(dV=2\pi r\,dA\). Integrating gives \(V=2\pi\bar r A\). A generating curve gives surface area \(S=2\pi\bar r L\). The axis must lie in the generating plane and not cross the generating interior; otherwise swept regions can overlap.

Derivation · two semicircle centroids

For the right semicircular area, use polar strips: \(dA=r\,dr\,d\theta\), \(x=r\cos\theta\), with theta from −π/2 to π/2. The first moment is \(\int x\,dA=(a^3/3)\int\cos\theta\,d\theta=2a^3/3\). Divide by \(\pi a^2/2\) to obtain \(\bar r_A=4a/(3\pi)\). For the curved arc, \(ds=a\,d\theta\), so \(\int x\,ds=2a^2\). Divide by arc length πa to obtain \(\bar r_L=2a/\pi\). The different weightings explain why the two centroids differ.

Common mistake

Do not average part centroid coordinates without area weights. A small web and a broad flange do not contribute equally.

Worked example · L-section centroid

The base is 6 by 2 in, with centroid (3,1) and area 12 in². The upright is 2 by 4 in above it, with centroid (1,4) and area 8 in². Therefore \(\bar x=(36+8)/20=2.20\) in and \(\bar y=(12+32)/20=2.20\) in.

Worked example · Removed hole

An 8 by 4 in plate has a radius-1 in hole at (6,2). Its net area is \(32-\pi=28.8584\) in². Thus \(\bar x=(128-6\pi)/(32-\pi)=3.78228\) in. Symmetry keeps \(\bar y=2\) in.

What the model leaves out

Geometric centroids coincide with centres of mass only for uniform density and thickness.

5Area moments of inertia

The area moment \(I_x=\int y^2\,dA\) measures the spread of area about an axis. It is not a mass moment of inertia. Its units are length to the fourth power. For a rectangle centred on x, integrate horizontal strips: \(I_x=b\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}y^2dy=bh^3/12\). Interchanging b and h gives \(I_y=hb^3/12\).

Derivation · parallel axes

Write the new coordinate as \(y=\eta+d\), where eta starts at the centroid. Expand before integrating.

\[I_x=\int(\eta+d)^2dA=\bar I_x+2d\int\eta\,dA+d^2A=\bar I_x+Ad^2.\]

The cross term vanishes only because the reference axis is centroidal. A hole subtracts both its own inertia and its transfer term.

The polar area moment is \(J_O=\int(x^2+y^2)dA=I_x+I_y\) about perpendicular axes meeting at O. Radius of gyration \(k_x=\sqrt{I_x/A}\) is the distance at which concentrated area would have the same I. Moving material farther from a bending axis increases I without necessarily adding area. This is why I-sections have separated flanges.

Common mistake

The d in Ad² is the distance between parallel axes, not the distance to the corner of a part. Begin from each part’s centroidal inertia.

Worked example · T-section inertia

The web is 2 by 8 in; the flange is 6 by 2 in above it. Area is 28 in² and \(\bar y=172/28=6.14286\) in. Add each centroidal inertia and its transfer term to get \(I_x=260.762\) in⁴, then \(k_x=\sqrt{260.762/28}=3.05171\) in.

Worked example · I-section subtraction

For depth 10 in, flange width 6 in, flange thickness 1 in and web thickness 0.75 in, area is 18 in². The outer rectangle minus the centred empty strips gives \(I_x=6(10)^3/12-(6-0.75)(8)^3/12=276\) in⁴. All terms use the same centroidal axis.

What the model leaves out

Area inertia alone does not determine capacity. Material strength, local buckling and connection details remain outside statics.
