Part I

Build the model

1Particle equilibrium and the free-body diagram

Isolate one ring or particle. Replace every attachment by the force it exerts on that body. A cable pulls away from the ring. Weight points vertically downward. A spring pulls along its axis when stretched. Do not include the reaction force on a different body.

Newton's second law becomes \(\sum\mathbf F=\mathbf0\) when acceleration is zero. Resolving a planar ring gives \(T_1\cos a_1+T_2\cos a_2=0\) and \(T_1\sin a_1+T_2\sin a_2=W\). These are simultaneous equations. Solving either alone leaves an arbitrary family of tensions. For symmetric cables at angle theta above horizontal, horizontal balance gives equal tensions, and vertical balance gives \(T=W/(2\sin\theta)\).

A spring's force must first satisfy equilibrium. Hooke's law then gives its extension \(s=F/k\), measured from its unloaded length. It does not give the total length. A negative computed cable tension means the assumed taut-cable model is impossible.

Common mistake

The two tensions need not be equal. They are equal only when the geometry and loading provide the required symmetry.

Worked example · Unequal cables

A 100 lb weight has cable directions 150° and 45° from positive x. Horizontal balance gives \(T_2=\sqrt{3/2}T_1\). Vertical balance gives \(T_1=73.2051\) lb and \(T_2=89.6575\) lb. Substitution closes both component equations.

Worked example · Spring extension

A 200 N weight is supported by a horizontal tie and a spring at 30°. Only the spring has a vertical component, so \(F\sin30°=200\), giving 400 N. At \(k=500\) N/m, extension is 0.800 m.

What the model leaves out

A particle has no size and cannot resist a couple. Use rigid-body equilibrium when attachment spacing matters.

2Three-dimensional equilibrium

A spatial ring needs three force equations. Write every cable as its tension times a unit direction from the ring toward its anchor. Put those direction components into columns of a matrix: \(U\mathbf T=-\mathbf W\). Three unknown tensions require three independent directions. Merely counting equations does not guarantee an invertible matrix.

Symmetry can remove unknowns before elimination. If two anchors reflect across a plane containing the load, their tensions are equal. Resolve perpendicular to that plane to prove it. Check the remaining two directions independently. Nearly parallel cables make the system ill-conditioned: small changes in geometry may demand large changes in tension.

Common mistake

Use a unit direction for every cable. Anchor coordinates alone are not force components.

Worked example · Spatial ring

The ring supports 600 lb. Anchors are (3,0,4), (−2,3,4), and (−2,−3,4) ft. Symmetry gives equal forces in the last two cables. Their y components cancel. Solving x and z balance gives \(T_{AB}=300\) lb and \(T_{AC}=T_{AD}=242.332\) lb.

What the model leaves out

Real attachments may move under load. This model fixes anchor locations and ignores cable extension.

Part II

Use and check the model

3Moments, couples and equivalent systems

A force can have zero resultant torque about a point only if its line of action passes through that point, or its magnitude vanishes. Resolve its tendency to rotate with the cross product \(\mathbf M_O=\mathbf r\times\mathbf F\). In a plane, expansion gives \(M_z=xF_y-yF_x\). Counterclockwise is positive. Magnitude is also \(Fd_\perp\), because the component of r parallel to F contributes no cross product.

Moving a force along its line of action adds \(\lambda\mathbf F\) to r. Its added moment is zero. Moving it to a different parallel line requires a compensating couple. Two equal opposite forces have zero net force but a couple \(\mathbf M=\mathbf r_{BA}\times\mathbf F_A\). Shifting the origin adds opposite terms that cancel, so a couple is independent of the moment point.

Derivation · moment about an axis

First form the moment vector about any point on the axis. Then extract its component along the unit axis direction.

\[M_{axis}=\hat{\mathbf u}\cdot(\mathbf r\times\mathbf F).\]

The scalar is signed. Reversing the axis direction reverses it. Moment components perpendicular to the axis do not turn the body about that axis.

Reducing a force system

Choose one reference point O. Translate each applied force to O and add its original moment as a couple. The equivalent system is \(\mathbf R=\sum\mathbf F_i\) and \(\mathbf M_O=\sum\mathbf r_i\times\mathbf F_i+\sum\mathbf C_i\). The couples C are included once. They do not need a lever arm.

To move the reference from O to P, substitute \(\mathbf r_{Pi}=\mathbf r_{Oi}-\mathbf r_{OP}\). Summing gives \(\mathbf M_P=\mathbf M_O-\mathbf r_{OP}\times\mathbf R\). When R is zero, moment is independent of the point. When R is nonzero in a plane, a single force at a suitable perpendicular offset can reproduce the force and moment. In three dimensions, a component of moment parallel to R cannot be created by shifting R. That residual component remains a couple.

Common mistake

The distance in Fd is perpendicular distance to the line of action. Position-vector length is generally the wrong lever arm.

Worked example · Off-axis force

A 50 N force at 30° acts at (4,3) m. Its components are (43.3013,25) N. Thus \(M_z=4(25)-3(43.3013)=-29.9038\) N·m. The perpendicular distance is \(|M_z|/50=0.598076\) m. The moment is clockwise.

Worked example · Cross product

For r = (1,2,3) m and F = (40,50,60) N, expand the determinant to obtain \(\mathbf M=(-30,60,-30)\) N·m. Its dot product with both r and F is zero, checking perpendicularity.

Worked example · Free couple

At A(−1.5,0) m, apply 80 N upward. At B(1.5,0), apply 80 N downward. About P(0,2), each force contributes −120 N·m. Total couple is −240 N·m. Moving P changes the individual terms but not their sum.

What the model leaves out

Rigid-body force equivalence preserves external motion, not local stress near the loading point.

4The wall shelf

A wall attachment often carries forces much larger than the payload. Model a rigid bracket with a lower pin and an upper horizontal tie. The upper tie has moment arm h about the lower pin. A payload at distance x gives moment Wx. Moment balance requires \(H=Wx/h\). Horizontal balance gives an opposite force H at the lower pin. Vertical balance gives W there, so its resultant is \(\sqrt{H^2+W^2}\).

These three equations explain why attachment spacing matters. Moving the load toward the wall reduces the couple. It does not reduce the total vertical reaction. Doubling the attachment spacing halves the upper pull without changing the load.

Common mistake

Weight divided between two anchors is not a complete model. The load also creates a moment that requires a force couple.

Worked example · Shelf edge load

The cited shelf has 20 cm depth and a published 10 kg payload limit. Put that payload at the edge and assume 10 cm anchor spacing. Then \(W=98.1\) N and \(H=98.1(0.20)/0.10=196.2\) N. The lower-pin resultant is \(219.358\) N. This is an illustrative load case, not a fastener rating.

What the model leaves out

Wall material, screw pull-out strength and shelf bending are outside this rigid-body calculation.
