1Model and interpretation

Key relation
\[I=I_0e^{-\mu x},\quad x_{1/2}=\ln2/\mu\]

A narrow monoenergetic beam loses uncollided photons as they interact with matter. The linear attenuation coefficient describes that removal per unit path length. It is not a universal material constant independent of energy.

Work the model

  1. Identify the radiation energy, material and beam geometry before using an attenuation coefficient. The slider supplies a hypothetical coefficient.
  2. Keep μ and path length in reciprocal units. A coefficient in cm⁻¹ needs a path in centimetres.
  3. Compare the exponential with numerical integration of dI/dx = −μI.
  4. Separate removal from the original beam from energy deposited locally. Scattered radiation can still reach other locations, and a transmission fraction is not a dose assessment.
Worked example · reason through the setup

In this idealized model, μ = 0.30 cm⁻¹ and x = 4.0 cm give I/I₀ = exp(−1.2) ≈ 0.3010. The half-value thickness is ln 2 / 0.30 ≈ 2.310 cm. A second half-value thickness halves the remaining uncollided intensity again.

Common mistake

Do not use a mass attenuation coefficient in cm²/g as if it were a linear coefficient in cm⁻¹; density is needed.

Common mistake

The uncollided exponential omits buildup and cannot be treated as a complete protective-design result.

3Limits and further study

What the model leaves out

Ideal narrow, monoenergetic beam with constant μ. Scattering buildup, secondary radiation, energy spectra and dose conversion are outside this model.

Worked reflection · transfer the idea

Compare doubling thickness with doubling initial intensity. Explain what changes the fraction transmitted and what changes only the final magnitude.

Write a prediction, identify the quantity held fixed and test the prediction in the lab. Name one additional physical effect that would require a different model.
