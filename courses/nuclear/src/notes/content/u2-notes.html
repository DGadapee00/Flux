1Model and interpretation

Key relation
\[N=N_0e^{-\lambda t},\quad \lambda=\ln2/T_{1/2},\quad A=\lambda N\]

An unstable nucleus has a decay probability per unit time. The exponential law describes the expected population of many independent nuclei. A half-life does not schedule the decay of each individual nucleus.

Quantities and units

Work the model

  1. Keep the half-life and elapsed time in the same time unit. Use seconds for activity in becquerels.
  2. Calculate λ = ln 2 / T₁/₂ and use it in both the population and activity equations.
  3. Compare the exponential with numerical integration of dN/dt = −λN. The integrated result approaches the same curve as the step is refined.
  4. Distinguish expected population from a possible integer count in one realization. Activity is a rate, not an accumulated dose.
Worked example · reason through the setup

A hypothetical collection starts with 1,600 unstable nuclei and has a half-life of 8 s. After 24 s, the expected remaining population is 200. Its expected activity at that instant is (ln 2 / 8) × 200 ≈ 17.33 Bq.

Common mistake

After two half-lives, one quarter remains; the remaining population is not zero.

Common mistake

A lower activity can reflect fewer nuclei, a smaller decay constant, or both.

3Limits and further study

What the model leaves out

Single-species decay with no production, daughter growth or branching. Real low-count records fluctuate around the expected curve.

Worked reflection · transfer the idea

Hold the initial number fixed and change the half-life. Explain the separate effects on initial activity and the fraction surviving a fixed time.

Write a prediction, identify the quantity held fixed and test the prediction in the lab. Name one additional physical effect that would require a different model.
