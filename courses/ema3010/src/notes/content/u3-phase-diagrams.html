Part I

Two-phase regions

1Reading a binary diagram

A binary phase diagram maps, for each temperature and overall composition, which phases are stable. Copper and nickel mix in any proportion, as liquid and as solid, so between their melting points they share a lens: above the liquidus all liquid, below the solidus all solid α, between them both.

Derivation

In equilibrium each element has the same chemical potential in both phases. Treat both as ideal solutions: then for element \(i\), with melting point \(T_i\) and heat of fusion \(\Delta H_i\),

\[ \frac{x^\alpha_i}{x^L_i} = \exp\!\left[\frac{\Delta H_i}{R}\left(\frac1T - \frac1{T_i}\right)\right]. \]

The two equations, one for each element, fix the two phases' compositions at each temperature. Geometrically, one straight line touches both phases' Gibbs energy curves, and its points of contact are those compositions: the common tangent.

Worked example · the tie line at 1550 K

Draw a horizontal line at 1550 K across the lens. It meets the liquidus at 47.1% nickel and the solidus at 54.1%. Any alloy between them, at this temperature, is a liquid of 47.1% nickel and a solid of 54.1%.

Common mistake

Reading a phase's composition at the alloy's own point. The alloy's point gives the overall composition; each phase's composition is at the end of the tie line, on the boundary of its own region.

2The lever rule

The tie line gives what each phase is made of. How much of each there is follows from a mass balance on nickel.

Derivation

An alloy of overall composition \(x_0\) splits into liquid of \(x_L\) and solid of \(x_\alpha\), with shares \(W_L + W_\alpha = 1\). Nickel balances: \(W_L x_L + W_\alpha x_\alpha = x_0\). So

\[ W_L = \frac{x_\alpha - x_0}{x_\alpha - x_L}, \qquad W_\alpha = \frac{x_0 - x_L}{x_\alpha - x_L}. \]

Each phase's share is the arm of the tie line on the far side of the alloy, like a lever balanced at \(x_0\).

Worked example · 50% nickel at 1550 K

\(W_L = (54.1 - 50)/(54.1 - 47.1) = 0.586\): the alloy is 58.6% liquid and 41.4% solid. The liquid holds 47.1% nickel; the 58.6% is how much of the alloy is liquid. Two different numbers, read from two different places.

Key idea

Ends of the tie line for compositions; opposite arms for amounts. As the alloy cools through the lens, both phases grow richer in copper, and the solid's share rises from 0 to 1.

3Steel: the iron–carbon diagram in practice

Above 727 °C carbon steel is austenite, which holds carbon in solution. Cooled slowly through 727 °C, the eutectoid, it splits into ferrite and cementite: pearlite.

Derivation

Pearlite needs carbon to diffuse, which takes time. Cooled fast enough, austenite cannot make it and shears into martensite, hard and brittle. How fast a point cools depends on where it is. In an end-quenched bar, treated as semi-infinite with its end held at \(T_q\),

\[ T = T_q + (T_i - T_q)\,\mathrm{erf}\frac{x}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}}, \]

so the time to reach 700 °C grows as \(x^2\) and the cooling rate there falls as \(1/x^2\). Alloying slows the change to pearlite, so it lowers the rate needed: the steel hardens deeper.

Worked example · 5 mm from the end

With \(T_i = 850\) °C, \(T_q = 20\) °C and \(\alpha = 6 \times 10^{-6}\) m²/s, \(\mathrm{erf}\,u = 680/830\) gives \(u = 0.946\). At \(x = 5\) mm the bar reaches 700 °C after 1.16 s, cooling at 156 K/s. That is just above 1045's critical 150 K/s, so it is half martensite, 39 HRC; 4340 there is fully hard, 55 HRC.

Common mistake

Assuming a quench hardens the whole part. Only where it cools faster than the critical rate does martensite form; 15 mm into a 1045 bar the rate is 17 K/s and the steel is soft pearlite.
