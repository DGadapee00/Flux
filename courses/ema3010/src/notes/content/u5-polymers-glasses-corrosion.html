1Polymer chains and the glass transition

A polymer is long chains of a repeat unit. Their lengths vary, so a sample has averages, not one molecular weight.

Derivation

If each chain adds one more unit with probability \(p\), a fraction \(x_i = (1 - p)p^{i-1}\) of chains has \(i\) units. Summing,

\[ \bar M_n = \sum x_i M_i = \frac{m_0}{1 - p}, \qquad \bar M_w = \frac{m_0(1 + p)}{1 - p}, \qquad \text{DP} = \frac{\bar M_n}{m_0}. \]

Chain ends loosen the packing, so the glass transition falls with \(\bar M_n\): \(T_g = T_{g,\infty} - K/\bar M_n\) (Flory and Fox).

Worked example · polystyrene at p = 0.99

With \(m_0 = 104\) g/mol and \(p = 0.99\): \(\bar M_n = 10\,400\) g/mol, \(\bar M_w = 20\,700\) g/mol, and the degree of polymerisation is 100. Its glass transition is \(100 - 10^5/10\,400 = 90.4\) °C.

Common mistake

Expecting a plastic to melt at one sharp temperature, like a metal. An amorphous polymer softens through its glass transition: its stiffness falls about a thousandfold over a few tens of degrees, and it never has a sharp melting point.

2Glass and its viscosity

A glass is a solid with no long-range order. Cooled fast enough, a melt grows too stiff for its atoms to line up into a crystal.

Derivation

The viscosity climbs steeply as the melt cools, \(\eta = \eta_0\exp(Q/RT)\). Through soda-lime glass's annealing point (\(10^{12}\) Pa·s at 546 °C) and softening point (\(10^{6.6}\) Pa·s at 726 °C), \(Q \approx 470\) kJ/mol. Near \(10^{12}\) Pa·s the glass stops flowing on human timescales: the glass transition.

A pane of height \(H\) standing on edge carries its own weight, \(\rho g z\) at depth \(z\). In tension the viscosity is \(3\eta\), so the top sinks at \(\int_0^H \rho g z/3\eta\,dz = \rho g H^2/6\eta\), and sinking by \(\Delta\) takes \(t = 6\eta\Delta/\rho g H^2\).

Worked example · a pane at 500 °C

At 500 °C the line gives \(\eta = 10^{13.8}\) Pa·s. A 1 m pane sinks 0.1 mm in \(6 \times 10^{13.8} \times 10^{-4}/(2500 \times 9.81) = 1.5 \times 10^6\) s: about 17 days. At 20 °C the same estimate gives about \(10^{51}\) years.

Common mistake

Believing old window glass is thicker at the bottom because it is still flowing. At room temperature it would take vastly longer than the age of the universe to sink even 0.1 mm; old panes were uneven when made.

3Galvanic corrosion

Corrosion is an electrochemical cell: metal dissolves at an anode, and its electrons reduce something, often oxygen, at a cathode.

Derivation

Two metals joined in water form a cell with \(E_\text{cell} = E_\text{cathode} - E_\text{anode}\) and \(\Delta G = -nFE_\text{cell}\). Each reaction's current grows tenfold for every \(b\) volts of overpotential (Tafel). The couple settles at the potential where the anode's total current equals the cathode's:

\[ A_a\,i_{0,a}10^{(E - E_a)/b_a} = A_c\,i_{0,c}10^{(E_c - E)/b_c}. \]

That is linear in \(E\). The anode's current density then grows as \((A_c/A_a)^{b_c/(b_a + b_c)}\).

Worked example · equal areas

With the model lines here, steel and copper of equal area settle at \(E = -0.335\) V. The steel dissolves at 0.057 A/m², 0.066 mm/yr. A steel rivet in a copper sheet, 100 times its area, corrodes at 1.4 mm/yr.

Common mistake

Painting the anode to protect it. Every scratch becomes a tiny anode beside the whole cathode, and corrodes far faster than bare steel would. Paint the cathode, or both.
