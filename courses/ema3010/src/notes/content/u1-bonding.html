Part I

The bond

1The bond-energy curve

Two atoms far apart do not interact. Brought closer, they attract. Brought too close, their electron clouds overlap and they repel. Add the two and the energy has a well. The atoms settle at its bottom.

Derivation

Model the attraction and the repulsion as two powers of the separation \(r\), the repulsion steeper:

\[ U(r) = -\frac{A}{r^n} + \frac{B}{r^m}, \qquad m > n. \]

The bottom of the well is where \(dU/dr = 0\). Call it the bond length \(r_0\), and call the depth there \(E_0\). Those two conditions fix \(A\) and \(B\):

\[ A = \frac{m}{m-n}\,E_0 r_0^{\,n}, \qquad B = \frac{n}{m-n}\,E_0 r_0^{\,m}. \]

For an ion pair the attraction is Coulomb's law between charges \(+e\) and \(-e\): \(n = 1\) and \(A = ke^2 = 14.40\) eV·Å. Then the depth follows from \(r_0\) and \(m\) alone, \(E_0 = (ke^2/r_0)(1 - 1/m)\).

Worked example · an Na⁺Cl⁻ ion pair

A sodium ion and a chloride ion sit \(r_0 = 2.36\) Å apart. Take \(m = 9\) for the repulsion. The Coulomb energy at that distance is \(14.40/2.36 = 6.10\) eV, and the repulsion takes back a ninth of it:

\[ E_0 = 6.10\left(1 - \tfrac19\right) = 5.42\ \text{eV}. \]

So it takes 5.42 eV to pull the pair apart into separate ions.

Common mistake

Taking the Coulomb energy \(ke^2/r_0\) as the bond energy. At \(r_0\) the clouds already repel, and that repulsion takes back \(1/m\) of it.

2Force is the slope

The force each atom feels is minus the slope of the curve, \(F = -dU/dr\). Outside \(r_0\) the curve rises toward zero, so the force pulls the atoms together. Inside \(r_0\) it rises steeply, so the force pushes them apart.

Derivation \[ F(r) = -\frac{dU}{dr} = -\frac{nA}{r^{n+1}} + \frac{mB}{r^{m+1}}. \]

Positive means apart. The repulsive term grows faster as \(r\) shrinks, which is why solids barely compress.

Worked example · squeezing the ion pair

Squeeze the Na⁺Cl⁻ pair to \(0.85\,r_0 = 2.006\) Å. With \(A = 14.40\) eV·Å and \(B = \tfrac18(5.42)(2.36)^9 = 1540\) eV·Å⁹,

\[ F = -\frac{14.40}{2.006^2} + \frac{9(1540)}{2.006^{10}} = -3.58 + 13.13 = 9.55\ \text{eV/Å}, \]

apart. That is about 15 nN on each ion.

3Stiffness is the curvature

Near the bottom any smooth well looks like a parabola. So a bond stretched a little acts as a spring, and its spring constant is the curvature at the bottom.

Derivation \[ k = U''(r_0) = -\frac{n(n+1)A}{r_0^{n+2}} + \frac{m(m+1)B}{r_0^{m+2}} = \frac{n\,m\,E_0}{r_0^{2}}. \]

The depth \(E_0\) is in it, but so is the shape, \(nm\). A solid's Young's modulus follows \(k\), scaled by how many bonds cross a unit area.

Worked example · a deep well and a shallow one

Two model bonds both sit at \(r_0 = 2.5\) Å. One is 4 eV deep with \(n = 1, m = 3\). The other is 2 eV deep with \(n = 2, m = 6\).

\[ k_\text{deep} = \frac{1\cdot3\cdot4}{2.5^2} = 1.92\ \text{eV/Å}^2, \qquad k_\text{shallow} = \frac{2\cdot6\cdot2}{2.5^2} = 3.84\ \text{eV/Å}^2. \]

The shallow bond is twice as stiff: 61.5 N/m against 30.8 N/m.

Key idea

Depth sets how much energy breaks the bond, and so how hot the solid melts. Curvature sets how far it stretches under load. They go together often, but not always.
