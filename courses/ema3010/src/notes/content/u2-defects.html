Part I

Point defects

1Vacancies in equilibrium

Pulling an atom out of its site costs bonds: an energy \(Q_v\), typically 0.5 to 2 eV in a metal. A perfect crystal would avoid that cost. But a crystal with a few empty sites can arrange them in a vast number of ways, and that disorder lowers the free energy.

Derivation

With a fraction \(c\) of sites empty, the free energy per site is the energy cost minus temperature times the entropy of mixing:

\[ g(c) = cQ_v + kT\,[c\ln c + (1 - c)\ln(1 - c)]. \]

Near \(c = 0\) the entropy term falls faster than the energy term rises, so \(g\) dips below zero before climbing. Setting \(dg/dc = 0\):

\[ \frac{c^*}{1 - c^*} = e^{-Q_v/kT} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad c^* \approx e^{-Q_v/kT}. \]

On a plot of \(\ln c^*\) against \(1/T\) this is a straight line of slope \(-Q_v/k\): an Arrhenius plot.

Worked example · one electronvolt, hot and cold

Take \(Q_v = 1.0\) eV and \(k = 8.617\times10^{-5}\) eV/K. At 1000 K, \(Q_v/kT = 11.6\), so \(c^* = e^{-11.6} = 9.12\times10^{-6}\). In a metal with copper's \(8.49\times10^{22}\) sites per cm³ that is \(7.74\times10^{17}\) vacancies per cm³. At 300 K, \(Q_v/kT = 38.7\) and \(c^* = 1.6\times10^{-17}\): about a million per cm³, still not zero.

Common mistake

Using the temperature in degrees Celsius. The Boltzmann factor needs kelvin: at 727 °C, \(T = 1000\) K.

Key idea

Vacancies are part of equilibrium, not leftovers of a poor process. They are also what lets atoms move through a crystal, so diffusion speeds up with temperature in the same Arrhenius way.

2Diffusion

Atoms in a hot solid hop from site to site. Carbon in iron hops between the gaps among the iron atoms; a metal atom hops into a neighbouring vacancy. Each hop is random, but where there are more atoms on one side, more hop away from it.

Derivation

The net flux runs down the gradient, Fick's first law, the same form as Fourier's law for heat:

\[ J = -D\,\frac{\partial C}{\partial x}. \]

Atoms are conserved, so what flows into a thin slice builds up there: \(\partial C/\partial t = -\partial J/\partial x\). That gives Fick's second law, the heat equation with \(D\) in place of \(\alpha\). For a deep part at \(C_0\) whose surface is held at \(C_s\),

\[ \frac{C_s - C(x,t)}{C_s - C_0} = \operatorname{erf}\frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}. \]

Any chosen concentration sits at a fixed \(x/\sqrt{Dt}\), so it moves in as \(\sqrt t\). Each hop needs energy \(Q_d\), so \(D = D_0\,e^{-Q_d/RT}\) rises steeply with temperature.

Worked example · carburizing at 900 °C

Carbon in austenite has \(D_0 = 2.3\times10^{-5}\) m²/s and \(Q_d = 148\) kJ/mol, a common textbook pair. At 900 °C, 1173 K,

\[ D = 2.3\times10^{-5}\exp\!\left(-\frac{148000}{8.314(1173)}\right) = 5.91\times10^{-12}\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}. \]

After 4 hours \(\sqrt{Dt} = 0.292\) mm. A 0.2 wt% steel with its surface at 1.0 wt% reaches 0.6 wt% where \(\operatorname{erf} z = 0.5\), \(z = 0.4769\): \(x = 2(0.4769)(0.292) = 0.278\) mm.

Common mistake

Scaling the depth with time. Twice as long gives √2 the depth; twice the depth takes four times as long. Raising the temperature does far more.

Part II

Dislocations and strength

3Slip and Schmid’s law

Sliding one whole plane of atoms over the next at once would take a shear stress near a tenth of the shear modulus, far more than metals yield at. They yield sooner because a dislocation, the edge of an extra half-plane, moves through the crystal one row of bonds at a time.

Derivation

Dislocations glide on close-packed planes, along close-packed directions: in FCC metals the four {111} planes, each with three ⟨110⟩ directions. A pull \(\sigma\) along an axis at \(\phi\) to a plane's normal spreads over an area larger by \(1/\cos\phi\), and only \(\cos\lambda\) of the force lies along a slip direction at \(\lambda\) to the axis:

\[ \tau_R = \sigma\cos\phi\cos\lambda. \]

Slip starts when \(\tau_R\) reaches the critical resolved shear stress \(\tau_\text{crss}\), a property of the crystal. The Schmid factor \(\cos\phi\cos\lambda\) is at most ½, at \(\phi = \lambda = 45^\circ\).

Worked example · a crystal at 40°

A crystal with \(\tau_\text{crss} = 1\) MPa is pulled at 2.2 MPa. Its slip plane's normal is at \(\phi = 40^\circ\) to the axis, with the slip direction at \(\lambda = 50^\circ\). Then \(m = \cos 40^\circ\cos 50^\circ = 0.492\) and \(\tau_R = 2.2(0.492) = 1.08\) MPa, enough to slip. It began to slip at \(\sigma_y = 1/0.492 = 2.03\) MPa.

Common mistake

Treating yield as a tensile limit. The same pull slips a crystal at 45° and leaves one at 10° or 80° whole. Yield is a shear event, which is why the yield criteria for parts judge shear.

4Strengthening by grain size

A metal yields when dislocations move. Anything that blocks them makes it stronger: solutes, precipitates, other dislocations, and grain boundaries.

Derivation

Slip cannot cross a grain boundary directly, because the planes do not line up. Dislocations pile up against it, and a pile-up of length \(d\) concentrates the stress on the boundary by about \(\sqrt{d}\). Slip passes to the next grain once that reaches a fixed value, so

\[ \sigma_y = \sigma_0 + k_y\,d^{-1/2}. \]

For a log-normal spread of sizes with median \(d_{50}\) and spread \(s\) in \(\ln d\), the average of \(d^{-1/2}\) is \(d_{50}^{-1/2}e^{s^2/8}\).

Worked example · mild steel

Mild steel with \(\sigma_0 = 70\) MPa and \(k_y = 0.74\) MPa·m\(^{1/2}\): with 100 µm grains, \(\sigma_y = 70 + 0.74/\sqrt{10^{-4}} = 144\) MPa. With 10 µm grains, \(70 + 234 = 304\) MPa.

Common mistake

Thinking that grain boundaries, being defects, make a metal weaker. At room temperature they are obstacles to slip, so more boundary means a stronger metal. Use \(d\) in metres in \(d^{-1/2}\), with \(k_y\) in MPa·m\(^{1/2}\).
