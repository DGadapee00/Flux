Failure

Three ways a part fails below its yield strength: a crack that runs, a crack that grows a little every cycle, and a slow stretch at high temperature.

Part I

How parts fail

1Fracture toughness

A crack concentrates stress at its tip. Whether it runs depends on the crack's length as much as on the stress.

Derivation

Close to the tip of a crack the elastic opening stress goes as \(K/\sqrt{2\pi r}\), whatever the loading: only the stress intensity \(K\) changes. For a crack of length \(2a\) in a wide plate,

\[ K_I = \sigma\sqrt{\pi a}. \]

The crack runs when \(K_I\) reaches the material's toughness \(K_{Ic}\): at \(\sigma_c = K_{Ic}/\sqrt{\pi a}\), or at a given stress when \(a\) reaches \(a_c = (1/\pi)(K_{Ic}/\sigma)^2\).

Worked example · an aluminium plate

7075-T651 aluminium has \(K_{Ic} = 24\) MPa·m\(^{1/2}\) and \(\sigma_y = 495\) MPa. With a crack 20 mm long, \(a = 0.01\) m, it breaks at \(24/\sqrt{\pi \times 0.01} = 135\) MPa: 27% of its yield strength.

Common mistake

Trusting a safety factor on yield. 4340 steel run at half its yield, 735 MPa, breaks once a crack's half-length passes \(a_c = (1/\pi)(50/735)^2 = 1.5\) mm. Strength and toughness are different properties, and high-strength alloys often trade one for the other.

2Fatigue crack growth

A load far below yield, repeated, can still break a part. Each cycle opens and closes a crack and moves its tip a little.

Derivation

The growth per cycle follows the range of stress intensity, \(\Delta K = Y\Delta\sigma\sqrt{\pi a}\), as a power law (Paris):

\[ \frac{da}{dN} = C(\Delta K)^m. \]

Separating and integrating from the starting depth \(a_0\) to the depth \(a_c\) where \(K_\text{max}\) reaches \(K_{Ic}\), for \(m \ne 2\),

\[ N_f = \frac{a_c^{1 - m/2} - a_0^{1 - m/2}}{C(Y\Delta\sigma\sqrt\pi)^m(1 - m/2)}. \]
Worked example · a fifth of yield

Steel with \(C = 10^{-11}\) m/cycle, \(m = 3\), \(Y = 1.12\) and \(K_{Ic} = 60\) MPa·m\(^{1/2}\), cycled from 0 to 100 MPa with a 1 mm edge crack: \(a_c = (1/\pi)(60/112)^2 = 91\) mm, and the life is 724 000 cycles. At 200 MPa it is 80 000.

Common mistake

Taking a stress below yield as safe forever. Every cycle grows the crack; the life is finite and goes roughly as \(\Delta\sigma^{-m}\). Design for a life and inspect before it ends.

3Creep

Hot enough, about 0.4 of the melting point in kelvin, a metal under a steady load keeps stretching, even far below its yield strength.

Derivation

At high temperature atoms diffuse, so dislocations can climb around obstacles that would stop them cold. The steady creep rate follows the stress as a power and the temperature as an Arrhenius term:

\[ \dot\varepsilon_s = A\sigma^n\exp\!\left(-\frac{Q_c}{RT}\right). \]

Before it settles there is a primary stage, here \(\varepsilon_p(1 - e^{-t/\tau})\), as the dislocations arrange themselves.

Worked example · a superalloy at 850 °C

With \(n = 5\), \(Q_c = 300\) kJ/mol and \(A\) set so the alloy creeps at \(1.0 \times 10^{-8}\) per second at 850 °C and 200 MPa, it reaches 2% strain in 460 hours. At 950 °C the rate is \(1.4 \times 10^{-7}\) per second and the life 34 hours.

Common mistake

Taking a stress below yield as giving no permanent strain. That holds cold; at 850 °C the part above is at a quarter of its yield strength and still creeps to retirement in under three weeks. Use kelvin in the Arrhenius term.
