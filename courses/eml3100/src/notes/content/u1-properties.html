Pure substances and ideal gases

Two properties fix the state of a pure substance. Water shows why: below its boiling point it is a liquid, above it a vapour, and on the boiling line it can be any mix of the two. These notes build the saturation dome, read the tables Flux generates from IAPWS-IF97, and find where the ideal-gas law is good enough. Every number is computed by the course's property library and checked by a second method.

Part I

Phases and the dome

01Pure substances and phases

A pure substance has one fixed chemical make-up throughout. Water is pure whether it is ice, liquid, steam, or a mix of all three. Air is treated as pure too, as long as it does not condense or react. Yan §2.1 sets out the definition.

A property is anything measured at a state: pressure \(P\), temperature \(T\), specific volume \(v\), internal energy \(u\), enthalpy \(h\), entropy \(s\). Specific means per unit mass. A specific property is intensive: it does not depend on how much substance there is. Yan §1.3 draws that line.

Why two properties are enough

A simple compressible substance exchanges energy in two ways: heat, and boundary work \(P\,dV\). Each independent way of exchanging energy adds one independent property. So two independent intensive properties fix every other one. This is the state postulate.

The word independent matters. On the boiling line \(T\) and \(P\) are tied together: name one and the other is fixed. There, \(T\) and \(P\) count as one property, and a second one is needed, such as \(v\) or the quality \(x\).

Heat liquid water in a cylinder held at constant pressure. Five states follow in order.

  • Compressed liquid: colder than the boiling point at this pressure.
  • Saturated liquid: at the boiling point, about to boil.
  • Saturated mixture: boiling. Liquid and vapour sit together at one temperature.
  • Saturated vapour: the last drop has boiled.
  • Superheated vapour: hotter than the boiling point.

The boiling point depends on pressure. Call it the saturation temperature, \(T_{sat}(P)\). At 100 kPa it is 99.6 °C. At 500 kPa it is 151.8 °C. At 1 MPa it is 179.9 °C. Read the other way, \(P_{sat}(T)\) is the pressure at which water boils at temperature \(T\).

\[ \begin{aligned} T &< T_{sat}(P):\ \text{liquid}\\ T &> T_{sat}(P):\ \text{vapour} \end{aligned} \]
The rule

Compare the state with the boiling line. At a given pressure, colder than \(T_{sat}\) is compressed liquid, hotter is superheated vapour. At a given temperature, higher pressure than \(P_{sat}\) is liquid, lower is vapour. Both comparisons give the same answer.

Worked example · which phase

Water at 500 kPa and 120 °C. At 500 kPa, \(T_{sat} = 151.8\) °C. The water is colder than that, so it is a compressed liquid. Its enthalpy is 504.0 kJ/kg.

Check the other way. At 120 °C, \(P_{sat} = 198.7\) kPa. The water is at 500 kPa, above that, so it is liquid. The same answer.

Heat it at 500 kPa to 200 °C. Now it is above 151.8 °C: superheated vapour, with \(h = 2856\) kJ/kg and \(v = 0.425\) m³/kg. Its volume grew about 400 times.

Open the Phase diagram lab on the boiling scenario and move the enthalpy slider. The state runs left to right along a horizontal line on the P–v diagram. On the T–v diagram the same path climbs, goes flat across the dome, then climbs again. The flat part is the boiling: heat goes in, and the temperature does not rise.

Worked example · a pressure canner

A weighted-gauge canner at sea level reads 10 psi. Absolute pressure is the gauge plus the standard atmosphere, 101.325 kPa. Ten psi is 68.95 kPa, so the absolute pressure is 170.3 kPa. Water boils there at 115.2 °C, which is 239.4 °F.

The USDA guide states that 10.5 psi gauge at sea level gives 240 °F. The same sum is 173.7 kPa, and the saturation temperature is 115.8 °C, or 240.5 °F. The statement and the formulation agree to about half a degree.

At 5,000 ft the standard atmosphere is 84.31 kPa. The same 10 psi gauge is then only 153.3 kPa, and the boil falls to 112.0 °C. The guide's step for a weighted gauge above 1,000 ft is 15 psi instead of 10. That reading at 5,000 ft is 187.7 kPa and 118.2 °C, hotter than the sea-level 10 psi boil. The extra gauge pressure more than makes up the thinner air.

Common mistake

A gauge reads the pressure above the atmosphere. Using 10 psi as if it were absolute looks up a boiling temperature of 89.53 °C, far below the canner. Altitude belongs in the atmosphere term. Leaving it at 101.325 kPa on a mountain overstates the boil.

What the model leaves out

A pure substance stays one chemical. Dissolved air in tap water, salt, or a reaction breaks that, and the boiling point shifts. The state postulate also counts only heat and boundary work. A magnetic, electric or surface-tension effect would add another independent property, and two would no longer be enough. Ice and the solid phases are not covered here: the library stops at 0 °C. A canner that was not vented still holds air, so the steam is not pure and the temperature at the same gauge reading is lower.

Another view. MIT OCW 16.050 Thermal Energy (Fall 2002) reviews properties in Part 0, before the second-law sessions on its calendar page. The calendar titles start at the second law. Problem sets there carry no printed solutions, so the numbers in these notes are our own.

02The saturation dome

Plot the saturated liquid and saturated vapour states for every pressure. On a T–v or P–v diagram they trace a dome. The left side is saturated liquid, \(v_f\). The right side is saturated vapour, \(v_g\). They meet at the top, the critical point: 373.9 °C and 22.06 MPa for water. Yan §2.3 draws the diagrams.

Inside the dome the substance is a mixture. A horizontal line across it is one temperature and one pressure. Where the state sits along that line says how much of the mass is vapour.

Why the lever rule holds

Take a mixture of mass \(m\): \(m_f\) of liquid and \(m_g\) of vapour. Volumes add, so \(V = m_f v_f + m_g v_g\). Divide by \(m\) and write the quality \(x = m_g/m\):

\[ v = (1-x)\,v_f + x\,v_g = v_f + x\,v_{fg} \]

with \(v_{fg} = v_g - v_f\). Solve for the quality:

\[ x = \frac{v - v_f}{v_g - v_f} \]

Internal energy, enthalpy and entropy add over mass the same way. So each one follows the same rule: \(u = u_f + x\,u_{fg}\), \(h = h_f + x\,h_{fg}\), \(s = s_f + x\,s_{fg}\).

The quality runs from 0 on the liquid side to 1 on the vapour side. It means nothing outside the dome. A compressed liquid does not have \(x = 0\); it has no quality at all.

Worked example · a tank with two phases

A rigid 0.5 m³ tank holds 10 kg of water at 151 °C. The specific volume is \(v = 0.5/10 = 0.05\) m³/kg. At 151 °C, \(v_f = 0.001092\) and \(v_g = 0.3827\) m³/kg. The state is between them, so it is a mixture at \(P_{sat} = 489.0\) kPa.

\[ x = \frac{0.05 - 0.001092}{0.3827 - 0.001092} = 0.1281 \]

Only 1.281 kg of the 10 kg is vapour. Yet that vapour fills 0.4905 m³, 98% of the tank. The 8.72 kg of liquid takes only 0.0095 m³. Quality is a mass fraction, not a volume fraction.

The dome narrows as pressure rises. At 100 kPa, \(v_{fg} = 1.693\) m³/kg. At 1 MPa it is 0.1932 m³/kg. At 10 MPa it is 0.01658 m³/kg. The enthalpy of boiling, \(h_{fg}\), falls too: from 2258 kJ/kg at 100 kPa to 1318 kJ/kg at 10 MPa. At the critical point both reach zero. Liquid and vapour are no longer different, and there is no boiling.

Worked example · halfway through boiling

Water at 100 kPa with \(x = 0.5\). From the table, \(v_f = 0.001043\), \(v_g = 1.694\) m³/kg, \(h_f = 417.4\) and \(h_{fg} = 2258\) kJ/kg. So \(v = 0.001043 + 0.5(1.693) = 0.8475\) m³/kg and \(h = 417.4 + 0.5(2258) = 1546\) kJ/kg.

A rigid tank moves straight up the diagram as it is heated, at fixed \(v\). Which way the quality goes depends on which side of the critical volume, \(v_c = 0.0031\) m³/kg, the tank is on. Try both rigid-tank scenarios in the lab.

Why heating can lower the quality

Heating moves both sides of the dome. As \(T\) rises, \(v_g\) shrinks fast and \(v_f\) grows slowly. In \(x = (v - v_f)/(v_g - v_f)\), the tank's \(v\) stays put. When \(v\) is large, the shrinking \(v_g\) closes in on it, and \(x\) climbs toward 1. When \(v\) is small, close to \(v_f\), the growing \(v_f\) closes in on it instead, and \(x\) falls toward 0.

Worked example · two rigid tanks

Tank A holds \(v = 0.3396\) m³/kg. At 120 °C it is a mixture at 198.7 kPa with \(x = 0.380\). Heated to 140 °C, the pressure rises to 361.5 kPa and the quality to 0.667. More of the water has boiled. Heated further, it ends as superheated vapour.

Tank B holds \(v = 0.0015\) m³/kg, less than \(v_c\). At 250 °C, \(x = 0.00508\) at 3976 kPa. At 300 °C, \(x = 0.00473\) at 8588 kPa. The quality fell. The liquid swells and takes over the tank. Heated further, it ends as compressed liquid, not vapour.

The rule

Find \(v\). Compare it with \(v_f\) and \(v_g\) at the temperature or pressure you know. Between them: a mixture, and \(x = (v - v_f)/(v_g - v_f)\). Below \(v_f\): compressed liquid. Above \(v_g\): superheated vapour.

What the model leaves out

The mixture is taken to be in equilibrium: one temperature, the liquid and vapour well mixed. A real kettle has hot spots and bubbles that are briefly superheated. The library also stops the dome at 350 °C. Above that, near the critical point, water is in IF97's region 3, which this course does not compute. The lab draws that last stretch dashed.

Part II

Reading the tables

03Reading property tables

Property tables list \(v\), \(u\), \(h\) and \(s\) at round values of \(T\) and \(P\). There are three kinds: saturation by temperature, saturation by pressure, and superheated vapour. A fourth lists compressed liquid. Yan §2.4 explains how to read them. Flux prints its own tables from IAPWS-IF97 and copies none.

A state rarely falls exactly on a printed row. Between two rows, a student draws a straight line.

Why linear interpolation works, and where it fails

Between rows \(x_0\) and \(x_1\), take the property \(y\) to vary in a straight line:

\[ y \approx y_0 + \frac{x - x_0}{x_1 - x_0}\,(y_1 - y_0) \]

The error comes from the curvature of \(y\). For a smooth function it is about \(\tfrac18 y''\,(x_1 - x_0)^2\) at the middle of the gap. Enthalpy of a superheated vapour is nearly straight in \(T\), so the error is tiny. The saturation pressure curves upward, so a straight line between rows lands high. Specific volume goes as \(1/P\), so a straight line in pressure lands high too, worst at low pressure.

Worked example · interpolating twice

Find \(v\) and \(h\) for steam at 0.9 MPa and 275 °C. The table has blocks at 0.8 and 1 MPa, with rows at 250 and 300 °C.

  • At 0.8 MPa: \(v\) is 0.2932 and 0.3242 m³/kg, so halfway is 0.3087. \(h\) is 2950.5 and 3056.9 kJ/kg, so 3003.7.
  • At 1 MPa: \(v\) is 0.2327 and 0.2580, so 0.2454. \(h\) is 2943.2 and 3051.7, so 2997.5.
  • Halfway between the blocks: \(v = 0.2770\) m³/kg and \(h = 3000.6\) kJ/kg.

IF97 at the state itself gives \(v = 0.2736\) m³/kg and \(h = 3001\) kJ/kg. The enthalpy agrees to 0.01%. The volume is 1.2% high, because \(v\) curves like \(1/P\) between the blocks.

Worked example · a curved row

Find the saturation pressure at 42.5 °C. The table has rows at 40 °C, 7.384 kPa, and 45 °C, 9.594 kPa. Halfway between gives 8.489 kPa. IF97 at 42.5 °C gives 8.427 kPa. The straight line lands 0.74% high, because \(P_{sat}\) curves upward: it roughly doubles every 12 °C near room temperature. Enthalpy across the same rows is nearly straight, so its interpolation is good to a few hundredths of a percent.

Common mistake

A problem here grades against the exact IF97 value and also accepts the value interpolated from Flux's table, the way a student reads it. So 0.2770 and 0.2736 m³/kg are both marked right in this example. A different interpolation, for example from a printed book with other row spacing, can land outside both. When an answer is marked wrong by a fraction of a percent, check which rows you used.

Compressed liquid needs care. Its properties barely change with pressure. A good shortcut takes the saturated liquid at the same temperature: \(v \approx v_f(T)\) and \(h \approx h_f(T)\).

Worked example · the liquid shortcut

Water at 500 kPa and 120 °C. The shortcut gives \(h \approx h_f(120\ \text{°C}) = 503.78\) kJ/kg. IF97 gives 504.0 kJ/kg. Adding the pressure term, \(h \approx h_f + v_f(P - P_{sat}) = 503.78 + 0.00106(500 - 198.7) = 504.1\) kJ/kg, closes most of the gap. The volumes differ in the fifth figure: 0.0010602 against 0.0010603 m³/kg.

The rule

Decide the phase first. Then open the table for that phase, find the rows around the state, and interpolate. Interpolate in the variable the table steps in. Two unknown coordinates mean two interpolations.

What the model leaves out

A straight line between rows is an approximation of the formulation, not the formulation. Near the dome, near the critical point, and between low-pressure blocks it can be off by a percent or more. IF97 itself is a fit to the scientific formulation IAPWS-95, within about 0.1% in most of regions 1 and 2. Tables printed in different books also round differently in the last digit.

Part III

Ideal and real gases

04The ideal-gas law

A gas whose molecules are far apart obeys \(Pv = RT\). Here \(R\) is the gas constant of that gas, and \(T\) is absolute. Yan §3.1 states the law; OpenStax UP2 §2.1 derives it from the motion of molecules.

Why Pv = RT

A molecule of mass \(m_1\) moving at \(v_x\) hits a wall and reverses. It hands the wall momentum \(2m_1v_x\). In a box of length \(L\) it returns every \(2L/v_x\). So its average force is \(m_1v_x^2/L\). Sum over \(N\) molecules and divide by the wall's area \(A\):

\[ P = \frac{N m_1 \overline{v_x^2}}{AL} = \frac{N m_1 \overline{v^2}}{3V} \]

Temperature measures the mean kinetic energy of translation: \(\tfrac12 m_1\overline{v^2} = \tfrac32 k_BT\). Substitute and \(PV = Nk_BT\). Per mole, \(PV = n\bar R T\). Per kilogram, \(Pv = RT\), with \(R = \bar R/M\).

The universal constant is \(\bar R = 8.314\) kJ/(kmol·K). For air, \(M = 28.97\) kg/kmol, so \(R = 0.2870\) kJ/(kg·K). In English units, \(R = 53.35\) ft·lbf/(lbm·R). Both come from the same \(\bar R/M\), through the exact unit definitions.

\[ PV = mRT, \qquad R = \frac{\bar R}{M} \]
Worked example · air in a tank

A 2 m³ tank holds air at 400 kPa and 25 °C. With \(T = 298.15\) K:

\[ \begin{aligned} m &= \frac{PV}{RT} = \frac{400 \times 2}{0.2870 \times 298.15}\\ &= 9.347\ \text{kg} \end{aligned} \]

Check by moles: \(n = PV/(\bar RT) = 800/(8.314 \times 298.15) = 0.3227\) kmol, and \(m = nM = 0.3227 \times 28.97 = 9.35\) kg.

Worked example · the same tank in English units

A 40 ft³ tank holds air at 100 psia and 80 °F. Convert: \(P = 100 \times 144 = 14\,400\) lbf/ft², \(T = 80 + 459.67 = 539.67\) R. Then \(m = PV/(RT) = 14\,400 \times 40/(53.35 \times 539.67) = 20.0\) lbm.

The same derivation gives the speed of the molecules. From \(\tfrac12 m_1\overline{v^2} = \tfrac32 k_BT\), the root-mean-square speed is \(v_{rms} = \sqrt{3\bar RT/M}\). OpenStax UP2 §2.2 works this out. Speed grows with temperature and falls with molar mass.

Worked example · how fast the molecules move

Nitrogen at 300 K: \(v_{rms} = \sqrt{3 \times 8.314 \times 300/0.02801} = 517\) m/s, faster than sound in the same air. One mole of any ideal gas at 0 °C and 101.325 kPa fills \(\bar RT/P = 8.314 \times 273.15/101\,325 = 0.02241\) m³, the familiar 22.41 L. Neither number depends on what the gas is made of, only on \(T\) and \(P\).

Common mistake

The law needs kelvins or rankines, and absolute pressure. Using 25 instead of 298.15 makes the mass twelve times too big. Using a gauge pressure from a tyre gauge leaves out the atmosphere, about 101 kPa.

A closed rigid tank keeps \(m\), \(R\) and \(V\) fixed. So \(P/T\) stays constant when it is heated: \(P_2 = P_1\,T_2/T_1\). Air at 50 psia and 70 °F, heated to 400 °F, reaches \(50 \times 859.67/529.67 = 81.15\) psia.

The rule

\(PV = mRT\) with absolute \(P\) and \(T\), and \(R = \bar R/M\) for the gas. Density is \(\rho = P/(RT)\): air at 101 kPa and 15 °C has \(\rho = 1.221\) kg/m³.

What the model leaves out

The derivation treats molecules as points that never attract. Real molecules have size and pull on each other. Both matter when the gas is dense: at high pressure, at low temperature, near condensation. The next section measures how much.

05When is a gas ideal?

The compressibility factor compares the real specific volume with the ideal one at the same \(P\) and \(T\). Yan §3.2 defines it:

\[ Z = \frac{Pv}{RT} \]

\(Z = 1\) is an ideal gas. \(Z < 1\) means the molecules pull together, and the gas is denser than ideal. \(Z > 1\) appears at very high pressure, where the molecules' own size dominates.

Why low pressure makes any gas ideal

Expand \(Z\) in powers of the density: \(Z = 1 + B(T)\rho + C(T)\rho^2 + \dots\) The coefficients measure pairs, triples and larger groups of molecules interacting. As \(P \to 0\), \(\rho \to 0\) and every correction vanishes. So \(Z \to 1\) for every gas, steam included.

Worked example · steam at 3 MPa

Steam at 300 °C. At 0.1 MPa, IF97 gives \(v = 2.639\) m³/kg, and \(Z = 0.9976\). The ideal-gas law is within 0.24%.

At 3 MPa, \(v = 0.08118\) m³/kg and \(Z = 0.9206\). The ideal-gas law predicts 0.08817 m³/kg, 8.6% too much. Here the steam is only about 66 °C above its boiling point, 233.9 °C at 3 MPa.

Steam far from the dome is nearly ideal. At 10 kPa and 100 °C, \(Z = 0.9985\). At 10 MPa and 500 °C, \(Z = 0.9196\), still 8% off. Air at room conditions is far from its own critical point, about −140 °C, so it is ideal within a fraction of a percent.

The rule

Low pressure, or high temperature compared with the critical temperature, makes a gas ideal. Close to the dome, use the tables. When in doubt, compute \(Z\) from the table's \(v\).

What the model leaves out

\(Z\) here comes from IF97 for water only. Other gases need their own data, or the generalized compressibility chart, which plots \(Z\) against reduced pressure and temperature. The chart treats all gases alike and is good to a few percent. Mixtures, and gases near their critical point, are not covered by this course yet.
