Integration

01Area as a limit of sums

To find the area under a positive \(f\) on \([a, b]\), cut the interval into \(n\) strips of width \(\Delta x = (b - a)/n\). Over each strip, build a rectangle whose height is \(f\) at a sample point. Add the rectangles.

\[ R_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x. \]

A left sum samples each strip at its left edge. A right sum uses the right edge. A midpoint sum uses the centre. As \(n\) grows, all three approach the same number, the area.

Worked example · four left rectangles

Take \(f(x) = x^2\) on \([0, 1]\) with \(n = 4\), so \(\Delta x = 1/4\). The left edges are \(0, 1/4, 1/2, 3/4\). The heights are \(0, 1/16, 4/16, 9/16\). The sum is \((14/16)(1/4) = 0.21875\).

The right sum uses \(1/4, 1/2, 3/4, 1\), and gives \(0.46875\). The exact area is \(1/3\). It lies between them, because \(x^2\) is increasing.

Worked example · four midpoints

The same function, with each strip sampled at its centre: \(1/8, 3/8, 5/8, 7/8\). The sum is \(0.328125\). With only four strips it is already within \(0.0052\) of \(1/3\).

Why

The right sum for \(x^2\) can be summed exactly. With \(x_i = i/n\), it is \(\tfrac{1}{n^3}\sum_{i=1}^{n} i^2\). The sum of the first \(n\) squares is \(n(n + 1)(2n + 1)/6\). So

\[ R_n = \frac{(n + 1)(2n + 1)}{6n^2} \to \frac{2}{6} = \frac13. \]

At \(n = 100\), \(R_n = 0.33835\). The formula for \(\sum i\) comes from pairing: \(1 + n\), \(2 + (n - 1)\), and so on. There are \(n/2\) pairs, each worth \(n + 1\), so \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} i = n(n + 1)/2\).

Common mistake

A Riemann sum with a fixed \(n\) is an estimate, not the area. Four left rectangles give \(0.219\), off by a third. The area is the limit as \(n \to \infty\).

Key

Height at a sample point, times width, summed over the strips. The area is the limit as the strips get thin. For an increasing \(f\), the left sum is low and the right sum is high.

In the Riemann lab, raise \(n\) and switch between left, right, and midpoint samples. The readout tracks the sum against the exact value. Simpson's rule gives the second opinion.

What it leaves out

Closed forms for sums of powers exist only for simple integrands. For most functions, the limit is not taken by hand. The next sections find it another way.

02The definite integral

The definite integral is the limit of Riemann sums, for any choice of sample points, as the widest strip shrinks to zero.

\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x. \]

A continuous function on \([a, b]\) always has one. Where \(f\) is negative, the rectangles have negative height. So the integral is a signed area: area above the axis minus area below.

Why

The properties come straight from the sums. A constant factor comes out of every term, so \(\int cf = c\int f\). Sums split, so \(\int (f + g) = \int f + \int g\). Strips on \([a, b]\) and on \([b, c]\) together make strips on \([a, c]\). Reversing the limits makes every \(\Delta x\) negative, so \(\int_b^a f = -\int_a^b f\).

Worked example · a trapezoid by geometry

\(\int_0^2 (2x + 1)\,dx\) is the area of a trapezoid with parallel sides \(f(0) = 1\) and \(f(2) = 5\), and width \(2\). So it is \(\tfrac12(1 + 5)(2) = 6\).

The left sum with \(n = 4\) samples \(1, 2, 3, 4\), at width \(1/2\). It gives \(5\). For a line, the left sum misses one triangle per strip. Together they make \(\tfrac12 \cdot (5 - 1) \cdot \tfrac12 = 1\).

The average value of \(f\) on \([a, b]\) is the height of the rectangle with the same area:

\[ f_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx. \]

For \(\sin x\) on \([0, \pi]\), the integral is \(2\), found in the next section. So the average is \(2/\pi = 0.637\). The peak is \(1\).

Common mistake

A definite integral is not always an area. \(\int_0^{2\pi} \sin x\,dx = 0\), because the hump below cancels the hump above. The total area between the curve and the axis is \(4\). For area, integrate \(|f|\).

Key

The definite integral is a limit of sums. It is a signed area. It is linear, it splits at interior points, and it changes sign when the limits swap.

What it leaves out

The proof that every continuous function is integrable is in an analysis course. Functions with infinitely many jumps, or unbounded ones, need more care. Improper integrals come in Calculus II.

03The Fundamental Theorem

Let the upper limit vary. \(A(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt\) is the area accumulated from \(a\) up to \(x\). The theorem has two parts. The first says how fast \(A\) grows. The second turns that into a way to compute integrals.

Why

Part 1. For a small \(h \gt 0\), \(A(x + h) - A(x)\) is the area of a thin strip over \([x, x + h]\). Divided by \(h\), it is the average height of \(f\) on that strip. As \(h \to 0\), a continuous \(f\) has average height \(f(x)\) on the strip. So \(A'(x) = f(x)\).

Part 2. Let \(F\) be any antiderivative of \(f\). Part 1 says \(A\) is one too. So \(A = F + C\). At \(x = a\), \(A(a) = 0\), so \(C = -F(a)\). At \(x = b\), \(A(b) = F(b) - F(a)\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f(t)\,dt &= f(x),\\ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx &= F(b) - F(a). \end{aligned} \]
Worked example · the area up to 1

Let \(A(x) = \int_0^x t^2\,dt\). An antiderivative of \(t^2\) is \(t^3/3\). So \(A(1) = 1/3 - 0 = 1/3\). That is the limit the Riemann sums of Section 1 were approaching. Part 1 gives \(A'(1) = 1^2 = 1\) without integrating.

Worked example · one hump of sine

\(\int_0^{\pi} \sin x\,dx = [-\cos x]_0^{\pi} = -\cos\pi + \cos 0 = 2\). Halfway, \(A(\pi/2) = 1 - \cos(\pi/2) = 1\), and the height there is \(\sin(\pi/2) = 1\).

When the upper limit is a function of \(x\), the chain rule joins in. \(\tfrac{d}{dx}\int_0^{x^2}\cos t\,dt = \cos(x^2) \cdot 2x\). At \(x = 1\) that is \(2\cos 1 = 1.081\).

Common mistake

The order is upper minus lower: \(F(b) - F(a)\). Reversing it flips the sign. For \(\int_0^{\pi}\sin x\,dx\), the reverse order gives \(-2\) for an area that is plainly positive.

Key

Accumulated area grows at the rate \(f\). To integrate \(f\) from \(a\) to \(b\), find any antiderivative, and subtract its value at \(a\) from its value at \(b\).

In the FTC lab, drag \(x\). The shaded area is \(A(x)\), and the readout's height is \(f(x)\). A short step in \(x\) shows the area growing at that height.

What it leaves out

Part 1 needs \(f\) continuous at \(x\). At a jump, \(A\) still exists, but it has a corner and no derivative there.

04Net change

Part 2 of the theorem, applied to \(F'\), says the integral of a rate is the net change:

\[ \int_a^b F'(t)\,dt = F(b) - F(a). \]

The integral of velocity is displacement. The integral of a flow rate is the volume that flowed. Each is a net change, with gains and losses cancelling.

Worked example · displacement and distance

A particle has \(v(t) = t^2 - 4\) m/s on \([0, 3]\). An antiderivative is \(t^3/3 - 4t\). The displacement is \((9 - 12) - 0 = -3\) m.

The velocity is negative until \(t = 2\), then positive. The distance adds the two legs as positive amounts: \(16/3\) m on \([0, 2]\), and \(7/3\) m on \([2, 3]\). The total is \(23/3 = 7.67\) m.

Why

Distance is \(\int_a^b |v(t)|\,dt\). Speed is never negative, so no leg cancels another. Split the interval where \(v\) changes sign, and integrate each piece with the sign that makes it positive.

Common mistake

\(|{-3}| = 3\) m is not the distance. That misses the back-and-forth: the particle went \(16/3\) m one way and \(7/3\) m back. Split at the sign change first.

Worked example · a tank that turns to draining

Water enters a tank at \(r(t) = 10 - 2t\) litres per minute. Over the first \(5\) minutes the rate is positive. The net change is \(\int_0^5 (10 - 2t)\,dt = 50 - 25 = 25\) L gained.

Run it to \(t = 8\). The rate turns negative at \(t = 5\), and the tank drains. The net change from \(0\) to \(8\) is \(80 - 64 = 16\) L. So \(9\) L drained out in the last \(3\) minutes. The integral already subtracted them.

Key

Integrate a rate to get a net change. Integrate its absolute value to get a total.

What it leaves out

The net change needs a starting value to become a position or an amount. The integral only gives the difference. Physical applications such as work and pressure come in Calculus II.

05Substitution

Substitution is the chain rule read backwards. When the integrand holds a function and its derivative, rename the inside.

Why

The chain rule says \(\tfrac{d}{dx}F(g(x)) = f(g(x))\,g'(x)\), where \(F' = f\). So \(F(g(x))\) is an antiderivative of \(f(g(x))\,g'(x)\). With \(u = g(x)\) and \(du = g'(x)\,dx\):

\[ \int f(g(x))\,g'(x)\,dx = \int f(u)\,du. \]
Worked example · a definite substitution

\(\int_0^1 2x(x^2 + 1)^3\,dx\). Let \(u = x^2 + 1\), so \(du = 2x\,dx\). At \(x = 0\), \(u = 1\). At \(x = 1\), \(u = 2\). The integral becomes \(\int_1^2 u^3\,du = (16 - 1)/4 = 3.75\).

Worked example · a missing constant

\(\int x\cos(x^2)\,dx\). Let \(u = x^2\), so \(du = 2x\,dx\), and \(x\,dx = \tfrac12\,du\). The integral is \(\tfrac12\int\cos u\,du = \tfrac12\sin(x^2) + C\). Differentiate to check: \(\tfrac12\cos(x^2) \cdot 2x\).

Worked example · an inside of 3x

\(\int_0^{\pi/3}\sin(3x)\,dx\). Let \(u = 3x\), so \(dx = du/3\), and \(u\) runs from \(0\) to \(\pi\). The integral is \(\tfrac13\int_0^{\pi}\sin u\,du = \tfrac13 \cdot 2 = 2/3\). A linear inside always costs one division by its slope.

Worked example · solving back for x

\(\int_0^3 x\sqrt{x + 1}\,dx\). Let \(u = x + 1\). Then \(du = dx\), but an \(x\) is left over. Solve the substitution for it: \(x = u - 1\). The integral becomes \(\int_1^4 (u - 1)u^{1/2}\,du = \int_1^4 (u^{3/2} - u^{1/2})\,du\).

That is \(\tfrac25(32 - 1) - \tfrac23(8 - 1) = 12.4 - 4.667 = 7.733\). Simpson's rule on the original integral gives the same four figures.

Common mistake

Keeping the old limits after substituting. \(\int_0^1 u^3\,du = 0.25\), not \(3.75\). Either change the limits to \(u\) values, or return to \(x\) before evaluating. Do not mix the two.

Key

Pick \(u\) as the inside function whose derivative is also present. Convert \(dx\), and convert the limits. A constant factor that is off can be divided out. A missing \(x\) cannot.

What it leaves out

Substitution works only when the derivative of the inside is present, up to a constant. Integrals like \(\int x\sin x\,dx\) need integration by parts, the first topic of Calculus II.

06Area between curves

Suppose \(f(x) \ge g(x)\) on \([a, b]\). A thin vertical strip at \(x\) has height \(f(x) - g(x)\) and width \(dx\). Adding the strips gives the area.

\[ A = \int_a^b \bigl(f(x) - g(x)\bigr)\,dx. \]
Why

Over a strip of width \(\Delta x\), the region is almost a rectangle of height \(f(x_i) - g(x_i)\). The sum of those rectangles is a Riemann sum for \(f - g\). As \(\Delta x \to 0\), it becomes the integral. The formula does not care whether either curve is above the axis. Only the difference counts.

Worked example · a line over a parabola

On \([0, 1]\), \(x \ge x^2\). The area between them is \(\int_0^1 (x - x^2)\,dx = 1/2 - 1/3 = 1/6\).

When the curves cross, the top one changes. Split the interval at each crossing, or integrate \(|f - g|\).

Worked example · curves that cross

\(y = x\) and \(y = x^3\) cross at \(-1\), \(0\), and \(1\). On \([0, 1]\), \(x\) is on top, and the area is \(1/2 - 1/4 = 1/4\). On \([-1, 0]\), \(x^3\) is on top, and the area is also \(1/4\). The total is \(1/2\).

Integrating \(x - x^3\) straight across \([-1, 1]\) gives \(0\). The two pieces cancel. That number is not an area.

Some regions are easier in horizontal strips. Then the right curve minus the left curve is the length, and \(dy\) is the width.

Worked example · horizontal strips

The region between \(x = y^2\) and \(x = y + 2\). They meet where \(y^2 = y + 2\), at \(y = -1\) and \(y = 2\). The line is on the right. The area is \(\int_{-1}^{2} (y + 2 - y^2)\,dy = 10/3 + 7/6 = 4.5\).

Vertical strips would need two integrals here, because the bottom boundary changes at \(x = 1\).

Common mistake

Subtracting in the wrong order gives a negative area. For the line over the parabola, \(\int_0^1 (x^2 - x)\,dx = -1/6\). Sketch first, and subtract the lower curve from the upper.

Key

Top minus bottom, in \(dx\). Right minus left, in \(dy\). Split where the curves cross.

What it leaves out

The intersections are found by algebra here. For most pairs of curves they need a numerical root finder, such as Newton's method. The area formula is unchanged.

07Volumes by slicing

Cut a solid into thin slabs across an axis. If the slab at \(x\) has cross-sectional area \(A(x)\) and thickness \(dx\), its volume is about \(A(x)\,dx\). Add them.

\[ V = \int_a^b A(x)\,dx. \]

A solid of revolution has circular slices. Rotating the region under \(y = f(x)\) about the \(x\)-axis makes disks of radius \(f(x)\), so \(A(x) = \pi f(x)^2\).

Why

A cone of radius \(r\) and height \(h\) checks the method. Lay it along the \(x\)-axis with its tip at \(0\). The radius grows linearly, so at \(x\) it is \(rx/h\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} V &= \int_0^h \pi\Bigl(\frac{rx}{h}\Bigr)^2 dx\\ &= \frac{\pi r^2}{h^2}\cdot\frac{h^3}{3} = \frac{\pi r^2 h}{3}. \end{aligned} \]

That is the cone formula from geometry, now derived. With \(r = 3\) and \(h = 4\) it gives \(12\pi = 37.7\).

Worked example · disks under a root

Rotate the region under \(y = \sqrt{x}\) on \([0, 4]\) about the \(x\)-axis. Each disk has area \(\pi(\sqrt{x})^2 = \pi x\). So \(V = \int_0^4 \pi x\,dx = 8\pi = 25.1\). Dividing out \(\pi\) leaves \(8\).

A region with a hole in the middle makes washers. The area of a washer is the outer disk minus the inner one: \(\pi(R^2 - r^2)\).

Worked example · a washer

Rotate the region between \(y = x\) and \(y = x^2\) on \([0, 1]\) about the \(x\)-axis. The outer radius is \(x\), the inner \(x^2\). So \(V = \pi\int_0^1 (x^2 - x^4)\,dx = \pi(1/3 - 1/5) = 2\pi/15 = 0.419\).

Common mistake

\(\pi(R^2 - r^2)\) is not \(\pi(R - r)^2\). For the washer above, squaring the difference gives \(\pi\int_0^1 (x - x^2)^2\,dx = \pi/30 = 0.105\). That is a quarter of the true volume. Square each radius, then subtract.

Key

Volume is the integral of cross-sectional area. Disks: \(\pi R^2\). Washers: \(\pi(R^2 - r^2)\). Radii are measured from the axis of rotation.

In the Solids lab, the Disks scenario stacks slices under \(\sqrt{x}\). The readout gives the volume by the antiderivative and by Simpson's rule.

What it leaves out

Rotation about a line other than an axis shifts every radius. About \(y = -1\), for instance, a disk's radius becomes \(f(x) + 1\). The text works those cases in §2.2.

08Volumes by shells

Instead of slicing across the axis, cut the solid into thin cylindrical shells around it. A shell of radius \(r\), height \(h\), and thickness \(dr\) has volume about \(2\pi r h\,dr\): unroll it into a thin slab of length \(2\pi r\).

Why

The exact volume of a shell is the outer cylinder minus the inner one: \(\pi h\bigl((r + dr)^2 - r^2\bigr) = 2\pi r h\,dr + \pi h\,dr^2\). The second term is a thin slab times a thin slab. In the Riemann sum it vanishes faster than \(dr\), so only \(2\pi r h\,dr\) survives the limit.

\[ V = \int 2\pi\,(\text{radius})(\text{height})\,d(\text{radius}). \]
Worked example · the same solid by shells

The solid under \(\sqrt{x}\) on \([0, 4]\), rotated about the \(x\)-axis. Shells around that axis are horizontal. At height \(y\), a shell has radius \(y\), and it runs from \(x = y^2\) to \(x = 4\). So

\[ \begin{aligned} V &= \int_0^2 2\pi y\,(4 - y^2)\,dy\\ &= 2\pi\,(8 - 4) = 8\pi. \end{aligned} \]

Disks gave \(8\pi\) too. Two slicings of one solid must agree.

Worked example · around the y-axis

Rotate the region under \(y = x - x^2\) on \([0, 1]\) about the \(y\)-axis. A vertical strip at \(x\) sweeps a shell of radius \(x\) and height \(x - x^2\). So \(V = 2\pi\int_0^1 (x^2 - x^3)\,dx = 2\pi(1/3 - 1/4) = \pi/6 = 0.524\).

Washers here would need \(x\) as a function of \(y\) on both sides of the hump. Shells avoid that.

Common mistake

The radius is the distance to the axis, not the coordinate. Around the line \(x = 2\), a strip at \(x\) has radius \(2 - x\). Using \(x\) builds the solid around the wrong line.

Key

Shells: \(2\pi \times\) radius \(\times\) height \(\times\) thickness. Slice parallel to the axis for shells and across it for disks. Pick the one whose integrand needs no solving.

In the Solids lab, the Both ways scenario shows disks and shells for one solid. The two readouts agree for every right end \(b\).

What it leaves out

The shell argument assumes the height is a single segment for each radius. A region that folds back on itself needs to be split first.

09Work

A constant force \(F\) over a distance \(d\) does work \(W = Fd\). A force that varies is constant over a short enough step. Add the steps.

\[ W = \int_a^b F(x)\,dx. \]
Worked example · a spring

A spring pulls back with force \(kx\) when stretched \(x\) from rest (Hooke's law). With \(k = 4\) lb/ft, stretching it \(2\) ft takes \(\int_0^2 4x\,dx = 8\) ft·lb.

The second foot costs three times the first: \(\int_1^2 4x\,dx = 6\), against \(\int_0^1 4x\,dx = 2\). The force grows as the spring stretches.

When different parts of a body move different distances, slice the body instead of the path. Each slice has its own weight and its own lift.

Worked example · pumping out a tank

A cylindrical tank has radius \(2\) m and height \(5\) m, full of water. Pump it all out over the top. A slab at height \(y\) has volume \(4\pi\,dy\), weight \(\rho g \cdot 4\pi\,dy\), and must rise \(5 - y\) m.

\[ \begin{aligned} W &= \int_0^5 \rho g\,(4\pi)(5 - y)\,dy\\ &= \rho g \cdot 4\pi \cdot 12.5. \end{aligned} \]

With \(\rho = 1000\) kg/m³ and \(g = 9.81\) m/s², \(W = 1.54 \times 10^6\) J.

Worked example · winding up a chain

A chain \(20\) m long, of mass \(2\) kg per metre, hangs from a winch. Winding it in lifts the piece \(y\) m below the winch by \(y\) m. That piece weighs \(2 \cdot 9.81\,dy\) N. So \(W = \int_0^{20} 19.62\,y\,dy = 19.62 \cdot 200 = 3920\) J.

The answer equals the chain's whole weight, \(392\) N, lifted \(10\) m. That is the distance its centre of mass rises, which Section 10 explains.

Common mistake

Lifting the whole tank's weight the full \(5\) m doubles the answer, to \(3.08 \times 10^6\) J. The top slab barely moves. Only the bottom slab rises the full height. The integral weighs each slab by its own distance.

Key

Work is force integrated over distance. When the distance varies through the body, slice the body and integrate weight times lift.

What it leaves out

The water is lifted slowly, so it gains no speed and no kinetic energy. Friction in the pipe and the pump's efficiency are ignored. A real pump does more work than this integral.

10Centres of mass

The centre of mass is the balance point. For point masses \(m_i\) at positions \(x_i\), it is the weighted average \(\bar{x} = \sum m_i x_i / \sum m_i\). A thin plate of uniform density is the limit of many small masses.

Why

Cut the region under \(y = f(x)\) into strips. A strip at \(x\) has area \(f(x)\,dx\), so its mass is proportional to that. Its own centre is at \(x\) across and at \(f(x)/2\) up. Weight each position by the strip's area and add.

\[ \bar{x} = \frac{\int x f(x)\,dx}{\int f(x)\,dx}, \] \[ \bar{y} = \frac{\int \tfrac12 f(x)^2\,dx}{\int f(x)\,dx}. \]
Worked example · under a parabola

The region under \(y = x^2\) on \([0, 1]\) has area \(1/3\). Then \(\bar{x} = (1/4)/(1/3) = 0.75\), and \(\bar{y} = (1/10)/(1/3) = 0.3\). The balance point sits toward the tall end, and low, because most of the area is near the axis there.

Common mistake

The centroid is not the midpoint of the interval. For the parabola it is at \(0.75\), not \(0.5\). The tall end carries more area, and the average leans toward it.

Key

A centre of mass is a weighted average. Weight each strip by its area, and place it at the strip's own centre.

What it leaves out

The plate is uniform. A density that varies across it enters each integral as a weight. Pappus's theorem, which links centroids to volumes of revolution, is in §2.6 of the text.

11Numerical integration

Most integrals have no antiderivative in closed form. Numerical rules estimate them from samples of the integrand. Split \([a, b]\) into \(n\) panels of width \(h = (b - a)/n\).

The trapezoidal rule joins neighbouring samples with straight lines. Simpson's rule fits a parabola through each pair of panels, and needs \(n\) even.

\[ \begin{aligned} T_n &= \tfrac{h}{2}\,(f_0 + 2f_1 + \cdots + 2f_{n-1} + f_n),\\ S_n &= \tfrac{h}{3}\,(f_0 + 4f_1 + 2f_2 + \cdots + 4f_{n-1} + f_n). \end{aligned} \]
Why

A trapezoid over one panel has area \(\tfrac{h}{2}(f_i + f_{i+1})\). Adding them counts every interior sample twice. That is also the average of the left and right Riemann sums.

The parabola through three equally spaced points has area \(\tfrac{h}{3}(f_0 + 4f_1 + f_2)\). Integrate it with its vertex at the middle sample to check. Chaining those blocks gives the weights \(1, 4, 2, 4, \ldots, 4, 1\).

Worked example · four panels on sine

Estimate \(\int_0^{\pi}\sin x\,dx = 2\) with \(n = 4\). The trapezoidal rule gives \(1.8961\), off by \(0.1039\). Simpson's rule gives \(2.00456\), off by \(0.00456\). The same five samples feed both.

Worked example · doubling the panels

With \(n = 8\), the trapezoid error falls to \(0.0258\), about a quarter. Simpson's falls to \(0.000269\), about a sixteenth. The trapezoid error shrinks like \(1/n^2\). Simpson's shrinks like \(1/n^4\).

Error bounds make that precise. If \(|f''| \le M\) on \([a, b]\), then \(|E_T| \le M(b - a)^3/(12n^2)\). If \(|f^{(4)}| \le K\), then \(|E_S| \le K(b - a)^5/(180n^4)\). For sine with \(n = 4\), both \(M\) and \(K\) are \(1\). The bounds are \(0.162\) and \(0.0066\). The true errors sit under them.

Common mistake

Simpson's rule with an odd \(n\) is not defined. The parabolas pair the panels. With \(n = 5\), the last panel has no partner, and the weights do not close with a \(1\).

Key

Trapezoid: weights \(1, 2, \ldots, 2, 1\), times \(h/2\), with error like \(1/n^2\). Simpson: weights \(1, 4, 2, \ldots, 4, 1\), times \(h/3\), with error like \(1/n^4\).

In the Numerical lab, step from \(4\) to \(8\) to \(16\) panels. The readout shows both errors falling at their own rates.

What it leaves out

Both bounds need a bound on a derivative, which is often unknown. In practice, the estimate is recomputed with \(n\) doubled, and the change between the two is taken as the error.
