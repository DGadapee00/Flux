Applications of derivatives

The derivative approximates, locates peaks, describes shape, and settles hard limits. Two theorems carry most of the weight: Fermat's, and the Mean Value Theorem. The numbers below are recomputed.

01Linear approximation

Near a point where \(f\) is differentiable, its graph is almost straight. The tangent line is the best straight stand-in. Using it in place of \(f\) is linear approximation.

\[ L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a). \]
Why

The tangent line matches \(f\) in value and in slope at \(a\). The error \(f(x) - L(x)\), divided by \(x - a\), is the difference quotient minus \(f'(a)\). That goes to \(0\) as \(x \to a\). So the error shrinks faster than the distance from \(a\). No other line has that property.

Worked example · a square root

Estimate \(\sqrt{4.1}\). Take \(f(x) = \sqrt{x}\) at \(a = 4\). Then \(f(4) = 2\) and \(f'(4) = 1/(2\sqrt{4}) = 1/4\). So \(L(4.1) = 2 + 0.1/4 = 2.025\). The true value is \(2.02485\). The error is \(0.00015\).

The estimate is high. \(\sqrt{x}\) bends downward, so its tangent lines run above it.

The differential writes the same idea as a change. With \(dx\) a small step in \(x\), \(dy = f'(x)\,dx\) is the change along the tangent. It approximates the true change \(\Delta y\).

Worked example · an error in a radius

A sphere's radius is measured as \(10\) cm, give or take \(0.1\) cm. The volume is \(V = \tfrac43\pi r^3\), so \(dV = 4\pi r^2\,dr\). With \(r = 10\) and \(dr = 0.1\): \(dV = 4\pi \cdot 100 \cdot 0.1 = 126\) cm³. The volume itself is \(4190\) cm³.

The relative error is \(dV/V = 3\,dr/r = 3\%\). A \(1\%\) error in the radius becomes a \(3\%\) error in the volume. The power \(3\) is the multiplier.

Common mistake

Linear approximation is good near \(a\) and poor far away. The same tangent at \(4\) estimates \(\sqrt{9}\) as \(2 + 5/4 = 3.25\). The true value is \(3\). Pick \(a\) close to the point you need.

Key

Near \(a\), \(f(x) \approx f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)\). The sign of \(f''\) says whether the estimate is high or low.

What it leaves out

The size of the error is estimated here only by example. A bound on it, in terms of \(f''\), comes with Taylor polynomials in Calculus II.

02Extreme values

An absolute maximum is the largest value \(f\) takes on its domain. A local maximum is the largest value nearby. Minimums are the same with "smallest".

Extreme Value Theorem. A continuous function on a closed interval \([a, b]\) has an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum there. Both conditions matter. \(1/x\) on \((0, 1]\) has no maximum, and the interval is not closed.

Why

Fermat's theorem says where to look. Suppose \(f\) has a local maximum at an interior point \(c\), and \(f'(c)\) exists. For a small \(h \gt 0\), \(f(c + h) \le f(c)\). So the quotient \((f(c + h) - f(c))/h\) is \(\le 0\). Its limit is \(f'(c) \le 0\).

For a small \(h \lt 0\), the top is still \(\le 0\) but the bottom is negative. The quotient is \(\ge 0\), so \(f'(c) \ge 0\). Both hold. So \(f'(c) = 0\).

A critical point is an interior point where \(f' = 0\) or \(f'\) does not exist. Fermat's theorem says every interior extreme is at a critical point. So on \([a, b]\), the absolute extremes are among the critical points and the two endpoints.

Worked example · the closed-interval method

Find the absolute extremes of \(f(x) = x^3 - 3x\) on \([-2, 3]\). \(f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3 = 3(x - 1)(x + 1)\). The critical points are \(\pm 1\).

Evaluate at the candidates: \(f(-2) = -2\), \(f(-1) = 2\), \(f(1) = -2\), \(f(3) = 18\). The absolute maximum is \(18\), at the endpoint \(3\). The absolute minimum is \(-2\), reached twice: at \(-2\) and at \(1\).

Common mistake

A critical point is a candidate, not an answer. \(f'(-1) = 0\) and \(f(-1) = 2\) is only a local maximum. The endpoint \(3\) beats it. Skipping the endpoints loses the largest value here.

Key

On a closed interval, list the critical points and the endpoints. Evaluate \(f\) at each. The largest is the maximum. The smallest is the minimum.

What it leaves out

The Extreme Value Theorem is used without proof. Like the Intermediate Value Theorem, it rests on the completeness of the real numbers. On an open interval or an unbounded one, the method needs a separate argument.

03The Mean Value Theorem

Over an interval, some instant's rate equals the average rate. That is the Mean Value Theorem.

Rolle's theorem. If \(f\) is continuous on \([a, b]\), differentiable on \((a, b)\), and \(f(a) = f(b)\), then \(f'(c) = 0\) for some \(c\) in \((a, b)\). The function takes an extreme value inside the interval, unless it is constant, and Fermat's theorem applies there.

Mean Value Theorem. Under the first two conditions alone, some \(c\) in \((a, b)\) has

\[ f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}. \]
Why

Subtract the secant line from \(f\). The difference \(g(x) = f(x) - S(x)\) is zero at both ends, because the secant meets \(f\) there. Rolle's theorem gives a \(c\) with \(g'(c) = 0\). That says \(f'(c)\) equals the secant's slope.

Worked example · a parabola on [1, 5]

For \(f(x) = x^2\) on \([1, 5]\), the secant slope is \((25 - 1)/4 = 6\). Set \(f'(c) = 2c = 6\): \(c = 3\). For a parabola the point is always the midpoint.

Worked example · a speed trap

A car covers \(30\) miles in \(24\) minutes. Its average speed is \(30/0.4 = 75\) mph. Position is differentiable in time, so at some instant the speedometer read exactly \(75\) mph.

The theorem's main use is to read shape from the sign of \(f'\). If \(f' \gt 0\) on an interval, then for any \(x_1 \lt x_2\) in it, \(f(x_2) - f(x_1) = f'(c)(x_2 - x_1) \gt 0\). So \(f\) is increasing. If \(f' = 0\) everywhere on an interval, then \(f\) is constant there.

Common mistake

The hypotheses are not decoration. \(|x|\) on \([-1, 1]\) has equal end values, but no point has slope \(0\). It is not differentiable at \(0\), so Rolle's theorem does not apply.

Key

Somewhere inside, the slope equals the average slope. Positive derivative means increasing. Zero derivative means constant.

What it leaves out

The theorem says a \(c\) exists. It does not say where, or how many. For most functions, finding \(c\) means solving \(f'(c) = \text{slope}\), which may need Newton's method.

04The shape of a graph

The first derivative says where \(f\) rises and falls. The second says which way it bends.

First derivative test. At a critical point \(c\): if \(f'\) changes from \(+\) to \(-\), \(f\) has a local maximum. From \(-\) to \(+\), a local minimum. No change of sign, no extreme.

\(f\) is concave up where \(f'' \gt 0\): its slopes increase, and the graph lies above its tangents. It is concave down where \(f'' \lt 0\). An inflection point is where the concavity changes.

Second derivative test. If \(f'(c) = 0\) and \(f''(c) \gt 0\), the graph curves up at a flat point: a local minimum. If \(f''(c) \lt 0\): a local maximum. If \(f''(c) = 0\), the test says nothing.

Worked example · a cubic

Let \(f(x) = x^3 + 3x^2\). Then \(f'(x) = 3x^2 + 6x = 3x(x + 2)\), so the critical points are \(-2\) and \(0\). And \(f''(x) = 6x + 6\).

\(f''(-2) = -6 \lt 0\), a local maximum, \(f(-2) = 4\). \(f''(0) = 6 \gt 0\), a local minimum, \(f(0) = 0\). \(f''\) changes sign at \(x = -1\). The inflection point is \((-1, 2)\).

Why

Both tests follow from the Mean Value Theorem. If \(f' \gt 0\) on one side of \(c\) and \(f' \lt 0\) on the other, \(f\) rises to \(c\) and falls after it. For the second test, \(f''(c) \gt 0\) means \(f'\) is increasing through \(c\). Since \(f'(c) = 0\), \(f'\) is negative just before and positive just after. That is the first test's minimum.

Common mistake

\(f''(c) = 0\) does not make an inflection point. For \(x^4\), \(f''(0) = 0\), yet \(f'' = 12x^2 \ge 0\) on both sides. The graph is concave up throughout, and \(0\) is a minimum. Check that the sign of \(f''\) changes.

Key

Sign of \(f'\): rising or falling. Sign of \(f''\): bending up or down. An extreme needs \(f'\) to change sign. An inflection needs \(f''\) to change sign.

What it leaves out

A full sketch also needs intercepts, symmetry, and asymptotes. The next section gives the asymptotes. The rest is in the text's curve-sketching checklist, §4.6.

05Limits at infinity

\(\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = L\) means \(f(x)\) is as close to \(L\) as we like for all large enough \(x\). The line \(y = L\) is then a horizontal asymptote.

For a rational function, divide the top and the bottom by the highest power of \(x\) in the bottom. Every term with \(x\) left in a denominator goes to \(0\).

Worked example · equal degrees

Find \(\lim_{x \to \infty} (3x^2 + 1)/(2x^2 - x)\). Divide by \(x^2\): \((3 + 1/x^2)/(2 - 1/x)\). As \(x \to \infty\), this goes to \(3/2\). At \(x = 1000\) the quotient is \(1.5008\).

Why

The rule by degree follows from the division. Equal degrees leave the ratio of the leading coefficients. A higher degree on the bottom leaves \(0\). A higher degree on top leaves a term that grows, so there is no horizontal asymptote.

Exponentials outgrow every power, and logarithms grow slower than every power. So \(x^{10} e^{-x} \to 0\) and \((\ln x)/x \to 0\) as \(x \to \infty\). Section 7 proves both with L'Hôpital's rule.

Common mistake

The leading terms decide the limit, not the constants. \((3x^2 + 1)/(2x^2 - x)\) is not near \(1/0\) for large \(x\). Dropping lower terms is fine at infinity. It is not fine at a finite point.

Key

At infinity, divide by the highest power in the bottom. Compare degrees. Exponentials beat powers, and powers beat logarithms.

What it leaves out

Slant asymptotes, which appear when the top's degree is one more than the bottom's, are in the text. So is the precise \(\varepsilon\)–\(N\) definition of a limit at infinity.

06Optimization

An optimization problem asks for the best value of a quantity under a constraint. The method:

  • Draw it and name the variables.
  • Write the quantity to optimize.
  • Use the constraint to reduce it to one variable.
  • Find that variable's allowed interval.
  • Find the critical points, and compare them with the endpoints.
Worked example · a small cut

A \(12\) in square sheet has a square of side \(x\) cut from each corner, and the sides fold up. The box's base is \(12 - 2x\), its height \(x\), so \(V(x) = x(12 - 2x)^2\) on \(0 \le x \le 6\).

At \(x = 1\): \(V = 1 \cdot 10^2 = 100\) in³. The product rule gives \(V'(x) = (12 - 2x)(12 - 6x)\). At \(x = 1\): \(V' = 10 \cdot 6 = 60\). A deeper cut still adds volume.

Worked example · the best cut

\(V' = 0\) at \(x = 2\) and \(x = 6\). At the endpoints, \(V(0) = V(6) = 0\). At \(x = 2\): \(V = 2 \cdot 8^2 = 128\) in³, with \(V' = 0\). That is the maximum.

A cut of \(4\) in goes past it: \(V = 64\) in³ and \(V' = 4 \cdot (-12) = -48\). The volume is falling.

Common mistake

Not every root of \(V'\) is the answer. \(x = 6\) also gives \(V' = 0\), but there the base has width \(0\) and \(V = 0\). It is the minimum. Compare every candidate against the others.

Worked example · a fence against a wall

\(40\) m of fence encloses a rectangle on three sides, with a wall as the fourth. With sides \(x\) out from the wall, the length along it is \(40 - 2x\). So \(A = x(40 - 2x)\) on \([0, 20]\). \(A' = 40 - 4x = 0\) at \(x = 10\). There \(A = 200\) m². Both endpoints give \(0\).

Key

Reduce to one variable, find its interval, and compare the critical points with the endpoints. The answer is a value of the quantity, with units.

In the Open box lab, slide the cut. The readout's \(dV/dx\) changes sign at \(2\) in. A centred difference in the lab gives the same rate as the product rule.

What it leaves out

The sheet has no thickness and the folds cost nothing. A real box needs flaps to hold its corners, and a real fence has a gate. Each changes the constraint, and the optimum moves with it.

07L'Hôpital's rule

Suppose \(f(x)\) and \(g(x)\) both go to \(0\), or both to \(\pm\infty\), as \(x \to a\). If \(\lim f'(x)/g'(x)\) exists, then

\[ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}. \]
Why

Take the simplest case: \(f(a) = g(a) = 0\), with \(f'\) and \(g'\) continuous and \(g'(a) \ne 0\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} &= \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{g(x) - g(a)}\\ &= \frac{(f(x) - f(a))/(x - a)}{(g(x) - g(a))/(x - a)}. \end{aligned} \]

The top and the bottom are difference quotients. They go to \(f'(a)\) and \(g'(a)\). Near \(a\), both functions look like their tangent lines, and the ratio of two lines through the same zero is the ratio of their slopes.

Worked example · a scaled sine again

\(\lim_{x \to 0} \sin(3x)/x\) is \(0/0\). The rule gives \(\lim 3\cos(3x)/1 = 3\). Unit 1 found the same \(3\) with the squeeze.

Worked example · twice in a row

\(\lim_{x \to 0} (e^x - 1 - x)/x^2\) is \(0/0\). Once gives \((e^x - 1)/(2x)\), still \(0/0\). Twice gives \(e^x/2 \to 1/2\). At \(x = 0.001\) the original quotient is \(0.50017\).

Other forms convert. \(0 \cdot \infty\) becomes a quotient: \(x\ln x = (\ln x)/(1/x)\) is \(-\infty/\infty\) as \(x \to 0^+\). The rule gives \((1/x)/(-1/x^2) = -x \to 0\). At \(x = 0.01\), \(x\ln x = -0.046\).

Common mistake

The rule needs \(0/0\) or \(\infty/\infty\). \(\lim_{x \to 0} (x + 1)/(x + 2)\) is just \(1/2\), by substitution. Differentiating anyway gives \(1/1 = 1\), which is wrong. Check the form before using the rule.

Key

Confirm \(0/0\) or \(\infty/\infty\). Differentiate the top and the bottom separately, not as a quotient. Repeat if the form persists.

What it leaves out

The general proof uses Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem and handles \(g'(a) = 0\) and infinite limits. The text gives it. The exponent forms \(1^\infty\), \(0^0\), and \(\infty^0\) need logarithms first.

08Newton's method

To solve \(f(x) = 0\), start at a guess \(x_0\). Replace \(f\) by its tangent line there, and solve that line instead. Its root is the next guess.

\[ x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}. \]
Why

The tangent at \(x_n\) is \(y = f(x_n) + f'(x_n)(x - x_n)\). Set \(y = 0\) and solve for \(x\). That is the formula. Near a simple root, each step roughly doubles the number of correct digits.

Worked example · the square root of 2

Solve \(x^2 - 2 = 0\) from \(x_0 = 2\). Here \(x_{n+1} = x_n - (x_n^2 - 2)/(2x_n)\). The steps are \(1.5\), \(1.416667\), \(1.4142157\), and \(1.4142136\). The last matches \(\sqrt{2} = 1.41421356\) to eight figures, after four steps.

Common mistake

Newton's method can fail. Where \(f'(x_n) = 0\) the step divides by zero. For \(f(x) = \sqrt[3]{x}\), the step is \(x_{n+1} = -2x_n\): from \(1\) it goes \(-2, 4, -8, 16\), away from the root at \(0\). A good start and a quick check of each step matter.

Key

Each step solves the tangent line. Close to a simple root it converges very fast. Far away, or where \(f'\) is small, it can wander.

What it leaves out

The convergence rate is stated, not proved. Its proof uses the second derivative. Bisection, from Unit 1, is slower but cannot run away from a root it has bracketed.

09Formula sheet

  • \(L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)\). \(dy = f'(x)\,dx\).
  • Extreme Value Theorem: continuous on \([a, b]\) means an absolute maximum and minimum exist.
  • Fermat: an interior extreme where \(f'\) exists has \(f' = 0\).
  • Closed-interval method: compare \(f\) at the critical points and the endpoints.
  • Mean Value Theorem: \(f'(c) = (f(b) - f(a))/(b - a)\) for some \(c\) in \((a, b)\).
  • \(f' \gt 0\): increasing. \(f'' \gt 0\): concave up. An inflection needs \(f''\) to change sign.
  • Second derivative test: \(f'(c) = 0\) with \(f''(c) \gt 0\) is a minimum, with \(f''(c) \lt 0\) a maximum.
  • At infinity: divide by the highest power in the bottom.
  • L'Hôpital: for \(0/0\) or \(\infty/\infty\), \(\lim f/g = \lim f'/g'\).
  • Newton: \(x_{n+1} = x_n - f(x_n)/f'(x_n)\).
