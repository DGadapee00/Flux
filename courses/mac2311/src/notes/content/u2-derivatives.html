Derivatives

The derivative is the limit of a slope. Every rule in this unit comes from that one limit, and each one is derived before it is used. The numbers below are recomputed.

01The derivative as a limit

A secant line joins two points on a graph. Through \((a, f(a))\) and \((a + h, f(a + h))\), its slope is the difference quotient. As \(h\) shrinks, the second point slides toward the first. The secant turns into the tangent line, if the slopes settle on a limit.

\[ f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}. \]

When the limit exists, \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\). The tangent line at \(a\) is the line through \((a, f(a))\) with that slope: \(y = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)\).

Worked example · a shrinking secant

Take \(f(x) = x^2\) at \(a = 1\). The quotient is \(((1 + h)^2 - 1)/h = (2h + h^2)/h = 2 + h\), for \(h \ne 0\). With \(h = 0.4\) the secant slope is \(2.4\). With \(h = 0.1\) it is \(2.1\). With \(h = 0.01\) it is \(2.01\). The limit is \(2\), so \(f'(1) = 2\).

Letting \(a\) vary makes \(f'\) a function. For \(x^2\) the same algebra at a general \(x\) gives \(2x + h\), so \(f'(x) = 2x\). Other names for it are \(dy/dx\) and \(\tfrac{d}{dx}f(x)\). Differentiating again gives \(f''\), the rate at which the slope changes.

Why

A differentiable function is continuous. Write the change in \(f\) as a product, for \(h \ne 0\):

\[ f(a + h) - f(a) = h \cdot \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}. \]

As \(h \to 0\), the first factor goes to \(0\) and the second to \(f'(a)\). The product goes to \(0 \cdot f'(a) = 0\). So \(f(a + h) \to f(a)\), which is continuity at \(a\).

The converse fails. A corner is continuous but has no tangent.

Worked example · a corner

Take \(f(x) = |x|\) at \(0\). For \(h \gt 0\) the quotient is \(|h|/h = 1\). For \(h \lt 0\) it is \(-1\). The one-sided limits of the quotient are \(-1\) and \(1\). They disagree, so \(f'(0)\) does not exist. Yet \(|x|\) is continuous at \(0\).

Common mistake

A secant slope with a small \(h\) is an estimate, not the derivative. For \(x^2\) at \(1\), \(h = 0.1\) gives \(2.1\), which is off by \(h\) itself. Report the limit, not the last quotient you computed.

Key

\(f'(a)\) is the limit of secant slopes. Differentiable implies continuous. Continuous does not imply differentiable.

In the Secant lab, slide \(h\) toward zero. The secant slope in the readout approaches \(f'(x_0)\), which the lab also computes by a second route.

What it leaves out

A function can also fail to be differentiable at a vertical tangent, such as \(\sqrt[3]{x}\) at \(0\), where the quotient grows without bound. This section shows only the corner.

02Power, product, and quotient rules

The limit definition is slow. A few rules, each proved once from it, do almost every derivative in the course.

A constant has derivative \(0\): its difference quotient is \(0/h\). The derivative of \(x\) is \(1\). Sums and constant multiples pass straight through, because the limit laws do.

Why

For a positive whole number \(n\), expand \((x + h)^n\) with the binomial theorem:

\[ \begin{aligned} (x + h)^n &= x^n + n x^{n-1} h\\ &\quad + (\text{terms with } h^2, h^3, \ldots). \end{aligned} \]

Subtract \(x^n\) and divide by \(h\). Every remaining term but \(n x^{n-1}\) still holds a factor of \(h\). Let \(h \to 0\). Section 7 extends the rule to every real exponent.

\[ \frac{d}{dx} x^n = n x^{n-1}. \]
Worked example · secant on a cubic

For \(f(x) = x^3\), the power rule gives \(f'(1) = 3\). Check against the definition. The quotient is \(((1 + h)^3 - 1)/h = 3 + 3h + h^2\). At \(h = 0.4\) that is \(4.36\). At \(h = 0.1\) it is \(3.31\). The limit is \(3\).

A product needs its own rule. The derivative of a product is not the product of the derivatives.

Why

Add and subtract \(f(x + h)g(x)\) in the top of the quotient:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\frac{f(x+h)g(x+h) - f(x)g(x)}{h}\\ &\quad = f(x+h)\,\frac{g(x+h) - g(x)}{h}\\ &\qquad + g(x)\,\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}. \end{aligned} \]

Let \(h \to 0\). \(f(x + h) \to f(x)\), because \(f\) is differentiable and so continuous. The quotients become \(g'\) and \(f'\).

\[ (fg)' = f'g + fg', \qquad \left(\frac{f}{g}\right)' = \frac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}. \]

The quotient rule follows from the product rule. Write \(q = f/g\), so \(f = qg\). Then \(f' = q'g + qg'\). Solve for \(q'\), and replace \(q\) by \(f/g\).

Worked example · a product

Let \(f(x) = x^2(3x + 1)\). Take \(u = x^2\) and \(v = 3x + 1\). Then \(f' = 2x(3x + 1) + 3x^2\). At \(x = 2\): \(4 \cdot 7 + 12 = 40\).

Check by expanding first. \(f(x) = 3x^3 + x^2\), so \(f'(x) = 9x^2 + 2x\). At \(2\): \(36 + 4 = 40\).

Worked example · a quotient

Let \(f(x) = x^2/(x + 1)\). The rule gives \(f' = (2x(x + 1) - x^2)/(x + 1)^2\). At \(x = 1\): \((4 - 1)/4 = 0.75\).

Common mistake

\((fg)' \ne f'g'\). For \(x^2(3x + 1)\) at \(2\), the product of the derivatives is \(2x \cdot 3 = 12\). The true derivative is \(40\). In the quotient rule, the order on top matters: \(f'g - fg'\), not the reverse, which flips the sign.

Key

Power rule: bring the exponent down and lower it by one. Product rule: differentiate one factor at a time and add. Quotient rule: bottom times top-prime, minus top times bottom-prime, over the bottom squared.

In the Secant lab, the \(x^3\) scenario shows the secant slope above \(3\) at \(x_0 = 1\). It falls toward \(3\) as \(h\) shrinks.

What it leaves out

The power rule is proved here only for whole-number exponents. Negative and fractional exponents, such as \(x^{-1}\) and \(\sqrt{x}\), wait for logarithmic differentiation in section 7.

03Rates of change on a line

A derivative is a rate. If \(s(t)\) is a position on a line at time \(t\), then \(v(t) = s'(t)\) is the velocity, and \(a(t) = v'(t) = s''(t)\) is the acceleration. The sign of \(v\) gives the direction. Speed is \(|v|\).

Why

The average velocity over \([t, t + h]\) is \((s(t + h) - s(t))/h\): distance moved over time taken. That is the difference quotient of \(s\). Its limit as \(h \to 0\) is the instantaneous velocity. The same argument, applied to \(v\), gives acceleration.

Worked example · a particle that turns back

Let \(s(t) = t^3 - 6t^2 + 9t\), in metres, for \(0 \le t \le 4\) seconds. Then \(v(t) = 3t^2 - 12t + 9 = 3(t - 1)(t - 3)\), and \(a(t) = 6t - 12\).

The particle stops at \(t = 1\) and \(t = 3\). It moves forward on \([0, 1)\), back on \((1, 3)\), and forward again after \(3\). The positions are \(s(0) = 0\), \(s(1) = 4\), \(s(3) = 0\), and \(s(4) = 4\).

The distance travelled is \(4 + 4 + 4 = 12\) m. The displacement is \(s(4) - s(0) = 4\) m. At \(t = 2\) the velocity is \(-3\) m/s and the acceleration is \(0\).

Common mistake

Distance is not \(|s(4) - s(0)|\) when the particle turns back. Here that gives \(4\) m and misses \(8\) m of travel. Find where \(v = 0\), split the interval there, and add the lengths of the pieces.

Key

\(v = s'\) and \(a = v'\). The particle turns where \(v\) changes sign. Distance adds the pieces between turns.

The same reading applies outside motion. A marginal cost is the derivative of cost with respect to quantity. A growth rate is the derivative of a population with respect to time.

What it leaves out

This section starts from a known \(s(t)\). Going the other way, from \(v(t)\) back to \(s(t)\), is integration, in Unit 4. Why a body has a particular acceleration is a question about force. That belongs to physics.

04Sine and cosine

The two trigonometric limits from Unit 1 are exactly what the derivative of sine needs.

Why

Use the angle-sum formula, \(\sin(x + h) = \sin x \cos h + \cos x \sin h\). The difference quotient becomes

\[ \begin{aligned} &\frac{\sin(x + h) - \sin x}{h}\\ &\quad = \sin x \cdot \frac{\cos h - 1}{h} + \cos x \cdot \frac{\sin h}{h}. \end{aligned} \]

As \(h \to 0\), the first fraction goes to \(0\) and the second to \(1\). What is left is \(\cos x\). The same steps with \(\cos(x + h) = \cos x \cos h - \sin x \sin h\) give \(-\sin x\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dx}\sin x &= \cos x,\\ \frac{d}{dx}\cos x &= -\sin x,\\ \frac{d}{dx}\tan x &= \sec^2 x. \end{aligned} \]

The tangent follows from the quotient rule. \(\tan x = \sin x / \cos x\), so its derivative is \((\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x)/\cos^2 x = 1/\cos^2 x\).

Worked example · the slope of sine at π/3

The rule gives \(\cos(\pi/3) = 0.5\). The secant slopes from \(x_0 = \pi/3\) approach it from below: \(0.316\) at \(h = 0.4\), \(0.456\) at \(h = 0.1\), and \(0.478\) at \(h = 0.05\). Sine bends downward here, so each secant runs under the tangent.

Common mistake

The rule needs radians. Measured in degrees, \(\sin(x^\circ) = \sin(\pi x/180)\), and its derivative is \((\pi/180)\cos(x^\circ)\). That extra factor is \(0.01745\). Leaving a calculator in degree mode scales every slope by it.

Key

Sine's derivative is cosine. Cosine's derivative is minus sine. Both depend on the limit \(\sin h / h \to 1\), which holds in radians.

In the Secant lab, the sin x scenario puts the tangent and the secant on the same curve. At the top of a hump the tangent is flat and \(f'(x_0) = 0\).

What it leaves out

The other three functions, secant, cosecant, and cotangent, follow from the quotient rule and are listed in §3.5 of the text. They are not derived again here.

05The chain rule

A composition \(y = f(g(x))\) changes because \(g\) changes, and \(f\) responds to that change. The rates multiply.

\[ \frac{d}{dx} f(g(x)) = f'(g(x))\, g'(x). \]

In Leibniz notation, with \(u = g(x)\):

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}. \]
Why

Let \(u = g(x)\). A step \(\Delta x\) changes \(u\) by \(\Delta u\), and that changes \(y\) by \(\Delta y\). When \(\Delta u \ne 0\),

\[ \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta u}\cdot\frac{\Delta u}{\Delta x}. \]

As \(\Delta x \to 0\), \(\Delta u \to 0\) too, because \(g\) is continuous. The two factors become \(f'(u)\) and \(g'(x)\). The case where \(\Delta u = 0\) for small steps needs a separate argument. The text gives it in §3.6, and the rule still holds.

Worked example · a power of a line

Let \(f(x) = (2x + 1)^3\). The outside is \(u^3\), with derivative \(3u^2\). The inside is \(u = 2x + 1\), with derivative \(2\). So \(f'(x) = 3(2x + 1)^2 \cdot 2 = 6(2x + 1)^2\). At \(x = 1\): \(6 \cdot 9 = 54\).

Worked example · sine of a square

Let \(f(x) = \sin(x^2)\). The outside is \(\sin u\), the inside \(u = x^2\). So \(f'(x) = \cos(x^2) \cdot 2x\). At \(x = \sqrt{\pi}\), \(\cos\pi = -1\), and \(f' = -2\sqrt{\pi} = -3.545\).

Common mistake

Dropping the inner derivative. For \((2x + 1)^3\) at \(1\), writing only \(3(2x + 1)^2\) gives \(27\). The true value is \(54\). The factor \(2\) is how fast the inside changes, and it multiplies everything.

Key

Differentiate the outside, leave the inside alone, then multiply by the derivative of the inside. For a chain of three, multiply three rates.

What it leaves out

The \(\Delta u = 0\) case of the proof is only named here. Chains of partial derivatives, where the inside has several variables, come in Calculus III.

06Implicit differentiation and related rates

Some curves are not graphs of one function. The circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 25\) has two \(y\) values for most \(x\). Near a single point, though, it is a graph. Treat \(y\) as an unknown function of \(x\) there, and differentiate both sides of the equation with the chain rule.

Worked example · a slope on a circle

Differentiate \(x^2 + y^2 = 25\) in \(x\). The term \(y^2\) gives \(2y\,y'\) by the chain rule. So \(2x + 2y\,y' = 0\), and \(y' = -x/y\). At \((3, 4)\) the slope is \(-3/4 = -0.75\).

Check with the upper half written out: \(y = \sqrt{25 - x^2}\), so \(y' = -x/\sqrt{25 - x^2}\). At \(x = 3\): \(-3/4\).

Related rates use the same move with time as the variable. Two quantities are tied by an equation. Differentiate the equation in \(t\). The result ties their rates.

Why

The method has four steps:

  • Name every changing quantity, and say which rate is known.
  • Write an equation that holds at every instant.
  • Differentiate it in \(t\), with the chain rule on every changing quantity.
  • Only then substitute the values at the instant asked about.

Substituting last matters. A value holds at one instant. The equation holds at every instant, and only the second can be differentiated.

Worked example · the ladder at 6 ft

A \(10\) ft ladder leans on a wall. Its foot is \(x\) ft out and slides away at \(x' = 2\) ft/s. The top is \(y\) ft up. The ladder fixes \(x^2 + y^2 = 100\) at every instant.

Differentiate in \(t\): \(2x\,x' + 2y\,y' = 0\), so \(y' = -x\,x'/y\). When \(x = 6\), \(y = 8\), and \(y' = -12/8 = -1.5\) ft/s. The top slides down at \(1.5\) ft/s.

Worked example · the ladder at 2 ft

The same ladder, with the foot \(2\) ft out. Then \(y = \sqrt{96} = 9.80\) ft, and \(y' = -4/9.80 = -0.408\) ft/s. Near the wall the top barely moves. Far out it falls fast. At \(x = 8\) the rate is \(-16/6 = -2.67\) ft/s.

Common mistake

Substituting \(x = 6\) before differentiating. The equation becomes \(36 + y^2 = 100\), with nothing left that changes. Its derivative says \(y' = 0\), which is false. Differentiate first, then substitute.

Key

Implicit differentiation is the chain rule on an equation. Related rates are implicit differentiation in time. Substitute after differentiating.

In the Ladder lab, move the foot from \(2\) ft to \(8\) ft at a fixed speed. The readout shows \(y'\) growing in size. A finite step in time gives the same rate as the formula.

What it leaves out

The model says \(y' = -x\,x'/y\) grows without bound as \(y \to 0\). A real ladder does not do that. It leaves the wall first, because nothing holds the top against it. The geometry knows no forces. Physics does.

07Inverses, exponentials, and logarithms

If \(f\) has an inverse, the inverse's slope is the reciprocal of \(f\)'s slope at the matching point. Reflecting a graph in \(y = x\) swaps rise and run.

Why

Differentiate \(f(f^{-1}(x)) = x\) with the chain rule: \(f'(f^{-1}(x)) \cdot (f^{-1})'(x) = 1\). Divide.

\[ (f^{-1})'(x) = \frac{1}{f'(f^{-1}(x))}. \]

For \(\arcsin x\): \(y = \arcsin x\) means \(\sin y = x\). Then \(\cos y \cdot y' = 1\), and \(\cos y = \sqrt{1 - x^2}\) on the arcsine's range. So \((\arcsin x)' = 1/\sqrt{1 - x^2}\).

Worked example · the slope of arcsine at 1/2

\((\arcsin)'(0.5) = 1/\sqrt{0.75} = 1.1547\). A centred difference with step \(0.0001\) gives the same four figures.

For an exponential \(a^x\), the difference quotient factors: \((a^{x+h} - a^x)/h = a^x \cdot (a^h - 1)/h\). So the derivative is \(a^x\) times the constant \(\lim (a^h - 1)/h\). For \(a = 2\) that constant is \(0.693\). For \(a = 3\) it is \(1.099\). The base where it equals exactly \(1\) is \(e = 2.71828\ldots\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dx} e^x &= e^x,\\ \frac{d}{dx} \ln x &= \frac{1}{x},\\ \frac{d}{dx} a^x &= a^x \ln a. \end{aligned} \]
Why

Let \(y = \ln x\), so \(e^y = x\). Differentiate: \(e^y y' = 1\), so \(y' = 1/e^y = 1/x\). For \(a^x\), write \(a^x = e^{x\ln a}\), and the chain rule gives the factor \(\ln a\). That explains the constants above: \(\ln 2 = 0.693\) and \(\ln 3 = 1.099\).

Logarithmic differentiation finishes the power rule. Let \(y = x^n\) for any real \(n\) and \(x \gt 0\). Then \(\ln y = n\ln x\). Differentiate: \(y'/y = n/x\). So \(y' = n x^{n-1}\), for every real exponent.

Worked example · x to the x

Let \(y = x^x\) for \(x \gt 0\). Then \(\ln y = x\ln x\). Differentiate: \(y'/y = \ln x + 1\). So \(y' = x^x(\ln x + 1)\). At \(x = 2\): \(4(0.6931 + 1) = 6.773\). A centred difference agrees to four figures.

Common mistake

\(x^x\) is neither a power nor an exponential. The power rule alone gives \(x \cdot x^{x-1}\), which is \(4\) at \(x = 2\). The exponential rule alone gives \(x^x\ln x\), which is \(2.773\). The true derivative is their sum, \(6.773\). Both the base and the exponent vary.

Key

An inverse's slope is the reciprocal of the original slope at the matching point. \(e^x\) is its own derivative. \(\ln x\) has derivative \(1/x\). When a variable sits in an exponent, take logarithms first.

What it leaves out

This section assumes that \(e\) exists: a base with \(\lim (e^h - 1)/h = 1\). The text defines \(e\) through a limit and makes that precise. Hyperbolic functions, also in Chapter 3, are not covered.

08Formula sheet

  • \(f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} (f(a + h) - f(a))/h\). Tangent line: \(y = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)\).
  • Differentiable implies continuous. \(|x|\) at \(0\) shows the converse fails.
  • \((x^n)' = n x^{n-1}\), for every real \(n\). \((c)' = 0\).
  • \((fg)' = f'g + fg'\). \((f/g)' = (f'g - fg')/g^2\).
  • \(v = s'\), \(a = v' = s''\). Speed is \(|v|\).
  • \((\sin x)' = \cos x\), \((\cos x)' = -\sin x\), \((\tan x)' = \sec^2 x\), in radians.
  • Chain rule: \((f(g(x)))' = f'(g(x))\,g'(x)\).
  • Implicit: differentiate both sides. Every \(y\) term picks up a factor \(y'\).
  • Related rates: write the equation, differentiate in \(t\), then substitute.
  • \((f^{-1})'(x) = 1/f'(f^{-1}(x))\). \((\arcsin x)' = 1/\sqrt{1 - x^2}\).
  • \((e^x)' = e^x\), \((\ln x)' = 1/x\), \((a^x)' = a^x\ln a\).
