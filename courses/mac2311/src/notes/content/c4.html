Techniques of integration

01Integration by parts

The product rule says \((uv)' = u'v + uv'\). Integrate both sides and rearrange. One integral trades for another.

\[ \int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du. \]
Why

Integrating \((uv)' = u'v + uv'\) gives \(uv = \int v\,du + \int u\,dv\), since \(du = u'\,dx\) and \(dv = v'\,dx\). Solve for \(\int u\,dv\). For a definite integral, the \(uv\) term is evaluated at the two limits.

The choice of \(u\) decides whether the new integral is easier. Choose for \(u\) the factor that gets simpler when differentiated. A common order of preference is: logarithms, inverse trig functions, algebraic powers, trig functions, exponentials.

Worked example · x times e to the x

\(\int_0^1 x e^x\,dx\). Take \(u = x\) and \(dv = e^x\,dx\). Then \(du = dx\) and \(v = e^x\). So the integral is \([x e^x]_0^1 - \int_0^1 e^x\,dx = e - (e - 1) = 1\).

Worked example · a logarithm alone

\(\int_1^e \ln x\,dx\). There seems to be one factor, but \(dv = dx\) is the other. Take \(u = \ln x\), so \(du = dx/x\) and \(v = x\). The integral is \([x\ln x]_1^e - \int_1^e dx = e - (e - 1) = 1\).

Worked example · x sin x over one hump

\(\int_0^{\pi} x\sin x\,dx\). Take \(u = x\), \(dv = \sin x\,dx\), so \(v = -\cos x\). The integral is \([-x\cos x]_0^{\pi} + \int_0^{\pi}\cos x\,dx = \pi + 0 = \pi\).

Sometimes parts returns the original integral. Then solve for it like an unknown. Two rounds on \(\int e^x \sin x\,dx\) give \(e^x\sin x - e^x\cos x - \int e^x\sin x\,dx\). Moving the integral across gives \(\tfrac12 e^x(\sin x - \cos x) + C\).

Common mistake

Choosing \(u = e^x\) and \(dv = x\,dx\) for \(\int x e^x\,dx\) makes it worse. The new integral is \(\int \tfrac12 x^2 e^x\,dx\), with a higher power. If the new integral is harder, swap the roles.

Key

\(\int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du\). Let \(u\) be the factor that simplifies when differentiated. If the original integral comes back, solve for it.

What it leaves out

Repeated parts on \(x^n e^x\) can be organised in a table, the tabular method. It is a bookkeeping shortcut for the same formula, and the text shows it.

Choosing a useful reduction

Parts needs differentiable factors and an integrable remaining product. It never promises shorter work. For \(I_n=\int x^ne^x\,dx\), taking \(u=x^n\) and \(dv=e^x dx\) gives \(I_n=x^ne^x-nI_{n-1}\). The exponent falls by one. Repeating eventually reaches \(I_0=e^x\). This reduction explains the alternating coefficients instead of asking you to memorize a table.

Worked example · repeated parts

For \(n=2\), substitute \(I_1=(x-1)e^x\). Then \(I_2=x^2e^x-2(x-1)e^x=(x^2-2x+2)e^x+C\). Differentiation produces \((2x-2)e^x+(x^2-2x+2)e^x=x^2e^x\). This reproduces 18.01SC, Fall 2010, exercise solutions 5F-2(b).

Worked example · closing the cycle

Let \(I=\int e^x\cos x\,dx\). First take \(u=e^x\), \(dv=\cos x\,dx\). Then \(I=e^x\sin x-\int e^x\sin x\,dx\). Parts on the remaining integral gives \(-e^x\cos x+I\). Thus \(I=e^x\sin x+e^x\cos x-I\), and \(2I=e^x(\sin x+\cos x)\). One primitive is half this expression.

On \([0,1]\), subtract its value \(1/2\) at zero. The definite integral is \(e(\sin1+\cos1)/2-1/2\), approximately \(1.378\). The integral workbench independently integrates the original function. This is exercise 5F-4, followed by a chosen definite-interval check.

Common mistake

A returning integral cannot disappear into the integration constant. Its derivative is the original integrand, usually nonzero. Keep it as an algebraic unknown until both copies have been collected.

For definite integrals, write \(\int_a^b u v'\,dx=[uv]_a^b-\int_a^b vu'\,dx\). Both endpoint terms belong to the boundary product. Subtracting only the upper product can silently change an otherwise correct calculation.

What the method leaves out

An elementary integrand may have no elementary primitive. Repeating parts forever is not a proof of failure. Numerical integration still evaluates definite integrals; special functions can name primitives beyond this course.

02Trigonometric integrals

Integrals of products of powers of \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\) yield to two identities: \(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1\), and the half-angle forms

\[ \begin{aligned} \sin^2 x &= \tfrac12(1 - \cos 2x),\\ \cos^2 x &= \tfrac12(1 + \cos 2x). \end{aligned} \]
Why

If either power is odd, save one factor of that function for \(du\). Convert the rest with \(\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1\). The integral becomes a polynomial in the other function. If both powers are even, nothing can be saved. Lower the powers with the half-angle forms instead.

Worked example · an odd power of sine

\(\int_0^{\pi/2} \sin^3 x\cos^2 x\,dx\). Save one \(\sin x\). Write \(\sin^2 x = 1 - \cos^2 x\). With \(u = \cos x\), \(du = -\sin x\,dx\), and \(u\) runs from \(1\) to \(0\). The integral is \(\int_0^1 (u^2 - u^4)\,du = 1/3 - 1/5 = 2/15\).

Worked example · an even power

\(\int_0^{\pi} \sin^2 x\,dx = \tfrac12\int_0^{\pi}(1 - \cos 2x)\,dx = \tfrac12\bigl[x - \tfrac12\sin 2x\bigr]_0^{\pi} = \pi/2\). That is half the interval's length, since \(\sin^2\) and \(\cos^2\) share it equally.

Common mistake

Substituting \(u = \sin x\) in \(\int \sin^2 x\,dx\) needs a \(\cos x\,dx\) that is not there. Even powers of a single function do not reduce by substitution. Use the half-angle form.

Key

Odd power: save one factor and convert the rest. Even powers: half-angle formulas. Check the answer by differentiating.

What it leaves out

Powers of \(\tan\) and \(\sec\) follow the same idea, with \(\sec^2 x = 1 + \tan^2 x\) and \(du = \sec^2 x\,dx\). These cases follow from the derivative pairs developed below.

Tangent and secant powers

The useful derivative pairs are \((\tan x)'=\sec^2x\) and \((\sec x)'=\sec x\tan x\). If a secant power is even, save \(\sec^2x\,dx\), then replace the remaining \(\sec^2x\) by \(1+\tan^2x\). For example, \(\int\sec^4x\,dx=\int(1+u^2)\,du=\tan x+\tan^3x/3+C\). This agrees with exercise 5C-6.

If the tangent power is odd and a secant factor is available, save \(\sec x\tan x\,dx\). With \(u=\sec x\), the leftover \(\tan^2x=u^2-1\). The rule follows from a derivative, not a checklist divorced from the differential. When neither pattern fits, split identities or use a reduction formula.

Worked example · reducing twice

Squaring \(\sin^2x=(1-\cos2x)/2\) gives \(\sin^4x=(1-2\cos2x+\cos^22x)/4\). Reduce \(\cos^22x\) again. The result is \(3/8-\cos2x/2+\cos4x/8\). Integrating gives \(3x/8-\sin2x/4+\sin4x/32+C\), the posted answer to exercise 5C-3.

Common mistake

The angle multiplier matters twice. An identity changes \(x\) to \(2x\), and integration contributes another factor \(1/2\). Writing \(-\sin2x/2\) instead of \(-\sin2x/4\) fails a derivative check.

On \([0,\pi/2]\), the odd-power example gives \(2/15\). The workbench can show the original trigonometric integrand while the substitution uses a polynomial. These are two descriptions of one integral. The transformed limits reverse because \(\cos x\) decreases; its negative differential reverses the sign as well.

Domain limits

Tangent, secant and their primitives have poles. Apply identities on intervals where every expression exists. A logarithmic primitive needs an absolute value unless the chosen interval fixes its argument positive. Never integrate through a pole by subtracting two finite endpoint formulas.

03Trigonometric substitution

A root of a quadratic is a side of a right triangle in disguise. Substituting a trigonometric function turns the root into a single trig term.

  • \(\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}\): let \(x = a\sin\theta\). Then the root is \(a\cos\theta\).
  • \(\sqrt{a^2 + x^2}\): let \(x = a\tan\theta\). Then the root is \(a\sec\theta\).
  • \(\sqrt{x^2 - a^2}\): let \(x = a\sec\theta\). Then the root is \(a\tan\theta\).
Why

Each choice makes an identity do the work. With \(x = a\sin\theta\), \(a^2 - x^2 = a^2(1 - \sin^2\theta) = a^2\cos^2\theta\). On \(-\pi/2 \le \theta \le \pi/2\), \(\cos\theta \ge 0\), so the root is \(a\cos\theta\) with no sign trouble. The other two use \(1 + \tan^2 = \sec^2\).

Worked example · part of a circle

\(\int_0^1 \sqrt{4 - x^2}\,dx\). Let \(x = 2\sin\theta\), \(dx = 2\cos\theta\,d\theta\). Then \(\theta\) runs from \(0\) to \(\pi/6\), and the integrand becomes \(4\cos^2\theta\). The half-angle form gives \([2\theta + \sin 2\theta]_0^{\pi/6} = \pi/3 + \sqrt{3}/2 = 1.913\).

Geometry agrees. The region is a sector of angle \(\pi/6\) in a circle of radius \(2\), with area \(\pi/3\), plus a triangle of area \(\sqrt{3}/2\).

Worked example · the arcsine

\(\int dx/\sqrt{1 - x^2}\). With \(x = \sin\theta\), \(dx = \cos\theta\,d\theta\) and the root is \(\cos\theta\). The integrand becomes \(1\), so the integral is \(\theta = \arcsin x\), plus \(C\). From \(0\) to \(1/2\) it is \(\pi/6 = 0.524\).

Common mistake

Forgetting \(dx = a\cos\theta\,d\theta\). For part of the circle, dropping it leaves \(\int_0^{\pi/6} 2\cos\theta\,d\theta = 1\), far from \(1.913\). Every substitution converts \(dx\), not only the integrand.

Key

Match the root to a Pythagorean identity. Convert \(x\), \(dx\), and the limits. Read the answer back in \(x\) from a right triangle.

What it leaves out

A quadratic that is not in one of the three forms needs completing the square first. \(\sqrt{x^2 + 2x + 5}\) becomes \(\sqrt{(x + 1)^2 + 4}\), and then the tangent substitution applies.

Completing the square and choosing the inverse branch

Write \(x^2+4x+13=(x+2)^2+9\). Translation comes first; scaling comes second. With \(x+2=3\tan\theta\), \(dx=3\sec^2\theta\,d\theta\). Choose \(-\pi/2\lt\theta\lt\pi/2\), where secant is positive. Then \(\sqrt{(x+2)^2+9}=3\sec\theta\). The same interval lets us return to \(\theta=\arctan((x+2)/3)\) without ambiguity.

Worked example · a shifted quadratic power

Evaluate \(\int (x^2+4x+13)^{-3/2}\,dx\). The transformation above gives \(\int\cos\theta\,d\theta/9=\sin\theta/9+C\). A right triangle gives \(\sin\theta=(x+2)/\sqrt{x^2+4x+13}\). Thus the primitive is \((x+2)/(9\sqrt{x^2+4x+13})+C\). Differentiation returns the entire original integrand. This checks exercise 5D-10.

For \(\sqrt{x^2-a^2}\), work on one connected branch at a time. On \(x\ge a>0\), let \(x=a\sec\theta\) with \(0\le\theta\lt\pi/2\). The root is \(a\tan\theta\). Negative \(x\) requires a different branch and sign analysis. The triangle alone does not establish those signs.

Worked example · two pieces of one numerator

Split \((x+1)/(x^2+4)\) into \(x/(x^2+4)+1/(x^2+4)\). The first primitive is \(\tfrac12\ln(x^2+4)\), since the numerator is half the denominator's derivative. For the second, use \(x=2\tan\theta\); it becomes \(d\theta/2\). The combined answer is \(\tfrac12\ln(x^2+4)+\tfrac12\arctan(x/2)+C\). This is exercise 5D-3.

Common mistake

Changing the algebra but keeping the original limits mixes two coordinates. Either transform both limits into angles, or return the primitive to \(x\) before evaluating. Both routes agree when used consistently.

The inverse substitutions are useful because their derivatives restore the necessary differential. A hyperbolic route also exists: \(x=a\sinh u\) turns \(\sqrt{a^2+x^2}\) into \(a\cosh u\). From the exponential definitions, \(\cosh^2u-\sinh^2u=1\). Integrating \(a^2\cosh^2u\) gives \(a^2(\sinh2u+2u)/4\). In \(x\), this is \(\tfrac12x\sqrt{a^2+x^2}+\tfrac12a^2\operatorname{arsinh}(x/a)\). Both terms have positive signs. Differentiate before trusting a remembered formula.

04Partial fractions

A proper rational function can be split after its denominator is factored. Each piece integrates to a logarithm or an arctangent.

Why

For distinct linear factors, write

\[ \frac{p(x)}{(x - r_1)(x - r_2)} = \frac{A}{x - r_1} + \frac{B}{x - r_2}. \]

Multiply through by \((x - r_1)\) and let \(x \to r_1\). The \(B\) term vanishes, leaving \(A = p(r_1)/(r_1 - r_2)\). This is the cover-up rule: cover the factor, and evaluate the rest at its root.

Worked example · two linear factors

\(\int_0^1 \dfrac{3x + 5}{(x + 1)(x + 3)}\,dx\). Cover \(x + 1\) and set \(x = -1\): \(A = 2/2 = 1\). Cover \(x + 3\) and set \(x = -3\): \(B = -4/(-2) = 2\). The integral is \([\ln(x + 1) + 2\ln(x + 3)]_0^1 = \ln 2 + 2\ln(4/3) = 1.269\).

Worked example · a difference of squares

\(\int_2^3 dx/(x^2 - 1)\). The bottom is \((x - 1)(x + 1)\). The cover-up gives \(A = 1/2\) and \(B = -1/2\). The integral is \(\tfrac12[\ln|x - 1| - \ln|x + 1|]_2^3 = \tfrac12(\ln\tfrac24 - \ln\tfrac13) = \tfrac12\ln 1.5 = 0.203\).

Worked example · a constant on top

\(\int_0^1 \dfrac{dx}{(x + 1)(x + 2)}\). The cover-up gives \(A = 1\) and \(B = -1\). The integral is \([\ln(x + 1) - \ln(x + 2)]_0^1 = \ln\tfrac23 - \ln\tfrac12 = \ln\tfrac43 = 0.288\).

Common mistake

Partial fractions need the top's degree below the bottom's. For \(x^2/(x^2 - 1)\), divide first: \(1 + 1/(x^2 - 1)\). Splitting without dividing produces constants that do not exist.

Key

Divide if the top's degree is not lower. Factor the bottom. Find each constant by cover-up. Integrate each piece as a logarithm.

What it leaves out

A repeated factor \((x - r)^2\) needs both \(A/(x - r)\) and \(B/(x - r)^2\). An irreducible quadratic needs \((Ax + B)/(x^2 + c^2)\), which integrates to a logarithm plus an arctangent. Both are developed below and in OpenStax §3.4.

Repeated factors need a full basis

The denominator \(x(x+1)^2\) requires \(A/x+B/(x+1)+C/(x+1)^2\). Multiplying through gives \(3x+2=A(x+1)^2+Bx(x+1)+Cx\). Setting \(x=0\) gives \(A=2\). Setting \(x=-1\) gives \(C=1\). Matching the squared term gives \(B=-2\). Substituting these values back checks every coefficient.

Worked example · integrating the repeated factor

For \(x>0\), the primitive of \((3x+2)/[x(x+1)^2]\) is \(2\ln x-2\ln(x+1)-1/(x+1)+C\). The last term comes from the power rule, not a logarithm. On the chosen interval \([1,2]\), its difference is \(2\ln(4/3)+1/6\), approximately \(0.742\). This follows the printed primitive in exercise 5E-5, with a new definite-interval check.

Irreducible quadratics need linear numerators

Over the real numbers, \(x^2+9\) has no linear factors. Use \((Ax+B)/(x^2+9)+C/(x+2)\). The general linear numerator lets one term integrate as a logarithm and another as an arctangent. A constant alone cannot represent every proper rational function with this denominator.

Worked example · a quadratic factor

For \((2x-9)/[(x^2+9)(x+2)]\), clearing denominators gives \(2x-9=(Ax+B)(x+2)+C(x^2+9)\). Substituting \(x=-2\) gives \(C=-1\). Then the squared coefficient gives \(A=1\), and the constant gives \(B=0\). The primitive is \(\tfrac12\ln(x^2+9)-\ln|x+2|+C_0\). This reproduces exercise 5E-6.

Common mistake

Cover-up isolates coefficients at simple roots. At a repeated root it finds only the highest-power coefficient directly. Solve the remaining identity as well. Omitting the lower power can produce a decomposition that agrees at selected inputs but fails everywhere else.

Long division remains necessary when the numerator degree is too large. For \(x^3/(x^2-1)\), divide to get \(x+x/(x^2-1)\). The polynomial part integrates separately. Factorization and decomposition are algebraic steps; the integral still inherits every excluded denominator root.

What algebra leaves out

A primitive is valid separately on each connected interval excluding poles. Its integration constant can differ between intervals. An absolute-value logarithm does not make the original rational function continuous through a pole.

05Arc length and surface area

Cut a curve into short pieces. Each piece is nearly a straight segment. Its length is \(\sqrt{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2}\).

Why

Over a step \(\Delta x\), the Mean Value Theorem gives a point where \(\Delta y = f'(x_i^*)\,\Delta x\). So the segment's length is \(\sqrt{1 + f'(x_i^*)^2}\,\Delta x\). The sum of the segments is a Riemann sum.

\[ L = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + f'(x)^2}\,dx. \]
Worked example · a parabola to x = 1

For \(y = x^2\) from \(0\) to \(1\), \(f'(x) = 2x\), so \(L = \int_0^1 \sqrt{1 + 4x^2}\,dx\). The antiderivative is \(\tfrac{x}{2}\sqrt{1 + 4x^2} + \tfrac14\ln\bigl(2x + \sqrt{1 + 4x^2}\bigr)\). At \(1\) that is \(1.118 + 0.361 = 1.479\).

The straight chord from \((0, 0)\) to \((1, 1)\) is \(\sqrt{2} = 1.414\). The curve is longer, as it must be.

Worked example · a line, by both routes

For \(y = x\) from \(0\) to \(1\), \(f' = 1\), and the integrand is the constant \(\sqrt{2}\). So \(L = \sqrt{2}\). The distance formula gives the same number. A formula that works must agree with geometry on a line.

Worked example · a parabola to x = 2

Run the same parabola to \(x = 2\). The antiderivative gives \(L = 4.647\). The chord to \((2, 4)\) is \(\sqrt{20} = 4.472\). The gap has grown from \(0.065\) to \(0.175\), because the curve bends more over the longer run.

Rotating the curve about the \(x\)-axis sweeps a surface. Each short piece sweeps a band of radius \(f(x)\) and slant width \(\sqrt{1 + f'^2}\,dx\).

\[ S = \int_a^b 2\pi f(x)\sqrt{1 + f'(x)^2}\,dx. \]
Worked example · a sphere

Take \(f(x) = \sqrt{r^2 - x^2}\) on \([-r, r]\). Then \(f' = -x/f\), so \(f\sqrt{1 + f'^2} = \sqrt{f^2 + x^2} = r\). The integrand is the constant \(2\pi r\). So \(S = 2\pi r \cdot 2r = 4\pi r^2\). For \(r = 2\) that is \(50.3\).

Common mistake

Using \(2\pi f(x)\,dx\) for the band, without the slant factor, treats each band as a flat ring. For a sphere that gives \(\int_{-r}^{r} 2\pi\sqrt{r^2 - x^2}\,dx = \pi^2 r^2\), which is \(39.5\) for \(r = 2\) instead of \(50.3\). The slant is not small where the curve is steep.

Key

Arc length: \(\int\sqrt{1 + f'^2}\,dx\). Surface area: \(\int 2\pi f\sqrt{1 + f'^2}\,dx\). The square root is the slant of each piece.

In the Arc length lab, the readout gives the length by the antiderivative and by Simpson's rule, next to the chord. Push the right end out and watch the gap grow.

What it leaves out

Many arc-length integrals have no elementary primitive. \(\int\sqrt{1 + \cos^2 x}\,dx\), the length of a sine wave, is one. They are evaluated numerically, as in Unit 3.

A suspension cable uses the same arc-length integral

Consider one cable above a level bridge deck. Let the support spacing be \(L\), and let the midpoint lie \(d\) below the supports. Under a uniformly distributed deck load per horizontal metre, an ideal flexible cable is parabolic. With the midpoint at horizontal zero, \(y=y_0+4dx^2/L^2\). The vertical offset \(y_0\) changes the drawing but not the cable length.

Differentiate: \(y'=8dx/L^2\). Set \(u=8dx/L^2\), so the endpoints become \(\pm k\), where \(k=4d/L\). Then \(\ell=L^2/(8d)\int_{-k}^k\sqrt{1+u^2}\,du\). The integrand is even. A tangent or hyperbolic substitution gives the primitive \([u\sqrt{1+u^2}+\operatorname{arsinh}u]/2\). Therefore

\[\ell=\frac L2\left(\sqrt{1+k^2}+\frac{\operatorname{arsinh}k}{k}\right),\qquad k=4d/L.\]

As \(d\) tends to zero, the quotient tends to one, giving \(\ell=L\). Expanding both terms for small \(k\) gives \(\ell=L+8d^2/(3L)+O(d^4/L^3)\). This predicts the excess length's approximate quadratic dependence on sag.

Worked example · main span with assumed shallow sag

The Golden Gate Bridge district lists a main span of 4,200 ft, also rounded to 1,280 m. Exact foot conversion gives \(L=1280.16\) m. Choose an illustrative sag of 50 m. The formula gives \(\ell=1285.349\) m, only \(5.189\) m beyond the span. The lab draws a cable, deck, supports and hangers. Its shape is schematic.

Official span specification. The sag is an assumption, not a measured Golden Gate dimension.

Worked example · doubling assumed sag

Keep the same span but choose \(d=100\) m. The length is \(1300.696\) m, with excess \(20.536\) m. Total length rises only about 1.19%, while the excess nearly quadruples. Applying the quadratic approximation to the whole length would confuse a small correction with the dominant term.

Worked example · refining cable chords

Use the 100 m sag and sixteen straight segments. Each chord has length \(\sqrt{\Delta x^2+\Delta y^2}\). Their sum is below \(1300.696\) m. Doubling the segment count reduces the shortfall to about one quarter. The readout computes the polygon from its actual vertices; the smooth value comes from the antiderivative.

Worked example · a geometrically similar small model

A 10 m teaching span with 1 m sag has length \(10.2606\) m. Doubling both dimensions doubles the length. Doubling sag alone changes the slopes and does not have that scaling. Similarity preserves \(d/L\), which is why the dimensionless bracket stays fixed.

Common mistake

The bridge's total length, suspension length and main span are different specifications. The cable model uses the distance between its two towers. Do not substitute the full bridge length, which also includes approaches.

What the course model leaves out

Equal support heights, a flexible inextensible cable and uniform deck load per horizontal metre are assumptions. Cable self-weight instead produces a catenary. This model ignores temperature, stretch, wind, uneven traffic, anchors and material strength. Geometric cable length cannot establish a safe cable size or allowable load.

Surface bands measure a different quantity

Worked example · one line makes two cones

Rotate \(y=1-2x\), \(0\le x\le1/2\), about the \(x\) axis. Since \(ds=\sqrt5 dx\), the lateral area is \(2\pi\sqrt5\int_0^{1/2}(1-2x)dx=\pi\sqrt5/2\). Rotation about the \(y\) axis uses radius \(x\), giving \(2\pi\sqrt5\int_0^{1/2}x dx=\pi\sqrt5/4\). These agree with the final values in exercises 4G-2 and 4G-3. End caps are excluded.

For a sphere, the generating derivative becomes unbounded at the poles. Interpret the displayed cancellation first on an interior interval. Its area integral has a finite endpoint limit. The band radius is a distance: use \(|f(x)|\) if the graph lies below the rotation axis. A self-overlapping parametrization can count a surface more than once.
