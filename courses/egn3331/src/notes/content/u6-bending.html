Bending

Shear and moment are the internal loads that bending leaves on a cut. The moment peaks where the shear changes sign. Stress at a fiber is the moment times its distance from the centroid, over I. Every number below is computed by the course library and checked a second way.

Part 1 · The diagrams | point | uniform | cantilever | Reference

01A point load

Cut a simple beam and keep the left piece. The shear on the cut balances the vertical forces on that piece. The moment balances their moments about the cut. Sign: shear is positive when those forces push the left piece up. Moment is positive when they sag the beam, which puts compression on top.

A single downward load P at distance a from the left has two reactions. The left one is P times (L − a) over L. Between the left support and the load, the shear is that reaction and the moment is the reaction times x. The moment is largest at the load:

\[ M = \frac{Pa(L-a)}{L} \]

At midspan, a is L/2 and the moment is PL/4. Each shear is P/2. Roylance §4.1 draws these diagrams. The check is the same cut, done by summing the left-hand forces one station at a time.

Worked example · the midspan load

The lab's point-load beam is 4 m long and carries 10 kN at midspan. Each reaction is 5 kN. The peak moment is

\[ M = 10\ \mathrm{kN\cdot m} \]

The gold curve is the moment. It rises as a straight line to midspan and falls as a straight line after it. The teal line marks the load, which is where the shear jumps from +5 kN to −5 kN.

Common mistake

Using PL/4 when the load is not at midspan overstates the moment. Reporting the whole load as the left reaction forgets the right support. The moment is P times a, only if the right support carries nothing, and it does not.

Another view: 2.001 lecture 5 builds the same diagrams from the load.

02A uniform load

A uniform downward load w is a force per length. On a simple span the two reactions are each wL/2. Shear then falls as a straight line, from +wL/2 at the left support to −wL/2 at the right, so dV/dx = −w. It is zero at midspan. The moment is a parabola:

\[ M = \frac{wL^2}{8} \]

That value is the peak, and it sits at the same station where the shear is zero. The relation is dM/dx = V. A smooth peak inside the span can occur only where V crosses zero. Doubling the span doubles the support shear and quadruples the moment, because the moment carries L squared.

Worked example · the uniform load

The lab's uniform case is 5 kN/m on a 4 m span. The support shear is 10 kN. At midspan the shear is zero and the moment is

\[ M = 10\ \mathrm{kN\cdot m} \]

Move the cut along the beam. The moment readout is largest exactly where the shear readout passes through zero.

Why the peak and the crossing coincide

Between two nearby cuts the moment changes by V times the step. If V is positive the moment is still rising. If V is negative the moment has started to fall. The turn from rising to falling is the zero of V. A diagram that puts the peak somewhere else has broken dM/dx = V.

03A cantilever

A cantilever fixed at the left and loaded downward at the tip has a different shape. The wall pushes up with P and applies a counterclockwise couple PL. Shear is +P at every cut. It never changes sign. The moment is zero at the tip and

\[ M = -PL \]

at the wall. The sign is negative: the top fiber is in tension. This is hogging. Because the shear does not cross zero, the extreme moment is at the end of the member, not in the span. dM/dx = V still holds. V is constant, so the moment is a straight line.

Worked example · the tip load

The lab's cantilever is 2 m long with 8 kN at the tip. The shear is 8 kN everywhere along the span. The wall moment is

\[ M = -16\ \mathrm{kN\cdot m} \]

The gold curve starts at −16 kN·m and reaches zero at the tip. The teal line is at the wall, the only station that can hold the peak.

Common mistake

Reporting the cantilever moment as positive puts compression on the top, which is the simple-beam sign. Using PL/4 here has no basis: there is no second support, and the moment arm of the tip load about the wall is the whole length.

Part 2 · The fibers | rectangle | tee | joist | Reference

04A rectangular section

Plane sections are assumed to stay plane, so the strain is linear in y. For elastic bending the stress is linear too. Equilibrium of the axial force puts the zero-stress line through the centroid. Equilibrium of the moment then gives

\[ \sigma = -\frac{My}{I} \]

y is positive upward from that axis. A positive, sagging moment compresses the top. For a rectangle of width b and height h, I is bh³/12 and the outer fiber is at h/2, so the magnitude is 6M/(bh²). The top and bottom stresses are equal and opposite. The integral check is ∫σ dA = 0 and −∫y σ dA = M.

Worked example · the 40 by 80 mm rectangle

The lab's rectangle is 40 mm wide and 80 mm deep, with a sagging moment of 2 kN·m. The two fibers are

\[ \sigma_{\mathrm{top}} = -46.9\ \mathrm{MPa} \] \[ \sigma_{\mathrm{bot}} = 46.9\ \mathrm{MPa} \]

The gold line on the chart runs from compression at the top, through zero at mid-height, to tension at the bottom. Blue is compression. Red is tension.

Worked example · twice the depth

The same moment on a section 40 mm by 160 mm drops both stresses to a quarter:

\[ \sigma_{\mathrm{top}} = -11.72\ \mathrm{MPa} \] \[ \sigma_{\mathrm{bot}} = 11.72\ \mathrm{MPa} \]

I grows with the cube of the depth and the fiber distance only with the depth, so the stress goes with 1/h². Doubling the width, instead, would only halve the stress.

Common mistake

Using the full height as the fiber distance doubles the stress. Dropping the sign reports compression as tension. Reversing the moment swaps the fibers: hogging tensions the top.

Another view: 2.001 lecture 20 derives the same linear stress from the plane-sections assumption.

05A tee

A tee is not symmetric. The flange adds area near the top, so the centroid sits above mid-height. The two outer fibers are then different distances from the axis, and the stresses differ. The farther fiber carries the larger stress. The formula is still σ = −My/I. Only y and I have changed. I is the sum of each rectangle's own bh³/12 plus the parallel-axis shift to the centroid.

Worked example · the tee at 2 kN·m

The lab's tee has a 120 mm by 12 mm flange on a 12 mm by 100 mm web. The section is 112 mm deep. The centroid is 80.5 mm up from the bottom, not at the 56 mm mid-height. At 2 kN·m sagging,

\[ \sigma_{\mathrm{top}} = -20.5\ \mathrm{MPa} \] \[ \sigma_{\mathrm{bot}} = 52.5\ \mathrm{MPa} \]

The bottom of the web is the farther fiber, so it carries the larger stress. The chart is no longer symmetric about the origin of y.

Why the stresses refuse to match

Equal top and bottom stress needs equal fiber distances, and that needs a centroid at mid-height. The flange pulls the centroid up. Using one c = h/2 for both fibers invents a symmetry the area does not have.

06A floor joist

A nominal 2×10 is not 2 in by 10 in. The American Softwood Lumber Standard, PS 20, dresses it to 1.5 in by 9.25 in. Southern Pine No. 2, spaced 16 in on center, is listed at a 14 ft maximum span for 40 psf live load and 10 psf dead load when deflection is limited to the span over 360. That span is Table 2 of the Southern Forest Products Association maximum spans, 1 June 2013. The joist carries the strip of floor between its neighbors, so the line load is 50 psf times 16/12 ft.

\[ w = 66.7\ \mathrm{lb/ft} \]

On a simple span the moment is wL²/8. The bending stress is that moment times half the dressed depth, over I. The section modulus is 21.4 in³.

Worked example · the table span

At 14 ft the moment and the bending stress are

\[ M = 1633\ \mathrm{lb\cdot ft} \] \[ \sigma = 916\ \mathrm{psi} \]

The gold curve is this stress against the span. It bends upward because of L squared. The teal line marks 14 ft, the span the table allows. The stress at that line is the number above.

Worked example · a 10 ft span

The same floor on a 10 ft span multiplies the moment by (10/14)². The stress and the moment become

\[ \sigma = 467\ \mathrm{psi} \] \[ M = 833\ \mathrm{lb\cdot ft} \]

Nothing about the board changed. Only the lever arm of the load changed, and it changed with the square of the length.

Worked example · the nominal rectangle

Using 2 in by 10 in, at the same 14 ft load, leaves the moment at 1633 lb·ft and drops the stress to

\[ \sigma = 588\ \mathrm{psi} \]

The nominal name is larger than the dressed board, so it understates the stress. The moment does not care which rectangle was assumed.

What the course model leaves out

916 psi is the elastic bending stress on the dressed section. It is not a published allowable Fb. The 14 ft listing can be set by the deflection limit L/360 rather than by bending. Shear, bearing at the supports, the repetitive-member factor, and load duration are all outside this calculation. The dead load is the 10 psf in the span table, not a measured floor.
