Bending

Shear and moment are the internal loads that bending leaves on a cut. The load fixes their slopes, so the diagrams can be drawn from the loads alone. A moment curves the beam, and the curvature sets the strain in every fiber. Stress is the moment times the distance from the neutral axis, over I. A tilted load or a second material moves that axis.

01Shear and moment by sections

Cut a beam and keep the left piece. The cut face carries a shear \(V\) and a moment \(M\) that hold that piece in equilibrium. \(V\) balances the vertical forces on it. \(M\) balances their moments about the cut. The sign convention is the course's. Shear is positive when the forces on the left piece push it up. Moment is positive when it sags the beam, which puts compression on top.

One load, two pieces

A simple beam of length \(L\) carries a downward load \(P\) a distance \(a\) from the left. Moments about the right support give the left reaction \(P(L-a)/L\). Cut at \(x < a\). The left piece holds only that reaction, so \(V = P(L-a)/L\) and \(M = P(L-a)x/L\). Cut at \(x > a\). The piece now holds the load too, so \(V\) drops by \(P\). The moment is largest at the load:

\[ M_{\max} = \frac{Pa(L-a)}{L} \]

At midspan \(a = L/2\), which gives \(PL/4\). Roylance §4.1 draws these diagrams.

Worked example · the midspan load

The Shear and moment lab's point-load beam is 4 m long and carries 10 kN at midspan. Each reaction is 5 kN. The peak moment is

\[ M = 10\ \mathrm{kN\cdot m} \]

The gold curve is the moment. It rises as a straight line to midspan and falls as a straight line after it. The shear jumps from +5 kN to −5 kN under the load.

Worked example · the load moved off centre

Slide the same 10 kN to 1 m from the left. The left reaction is \(10\times 3/4 = 7.5\) kN, and the right is 2.5 kN. The peak moment, at the load, is \(10\times 1\times 3/4\):

\[ M = 7.5\ \mathrm{kN\cdot m} \]

Moving the load toward a support shortens one lever arm faster than the other grows, so the peak falls.

Common mistake

Using \(PL/4\) when the load is not at midspan overstates the moment: 10 kN·m instead of 7.5. Reporting the whole load as the left reaction forgets the right support. \(Pa\) alone is the moment only if the right support carries nothing, and it does not.

Another view: 2.001 lecture 5 builds the same diagrams from the load.

02How load, shear and moment are linked

Cutting at every station works, but the diagrams have a shortcut. The load sets the slope of the shear, and the shear sets the slope of the moment.

A slice of beam

Take a slice of length \(dx\) with a downward load \(w\) per length on it. The left face carries \(V\) and \(M\). The right face carries \(V + dV\) and \(M + dM\). Vertical equilibrium: \(V - w\,dx - (V + dV) = 0\). Moments about the right face, keeping first-order terms: \(M + V\,dx - (M + dM) = 0\). So

\[ \frac{dV}{dx} = -w \] \[ \frac{dM}{dx} = V \]

A downward load makes the shear fall. A positive shear makes the moment rise. A smooth peak of \(M\) inside a span sits where \(V\) crosses zero.

Worked example · the uniform load

The lab's uniform case is 5 kN/m on a 4 m simple span. Each reaction is \(wL/2 = 10\) kN. The shear falls at 5 kN per metre, from +10 kN to −10 kN, and crosses zero at midspan. The moment there is the area under the shear from the left support, a triangle of base 2 m and height 10 kN:

\[ M = 10\ \mathrm{kN\cdot m} \]

That is \(wL^{2}/8\). Move the cut along the beam: the moment readout is largest exactly where the shear readout passes zero. Doubling the span quadruples the moment, because the moment carries \(L^{2}\).

Worked example · the tip load

The lab's cantilever is 2 m long, fixed at the left, with 8 kN at the tip. The wall pushes up 8 kN and applies a counterclockwise couple. The shear is +8 kN everywhere and never crosses zero. The moment rises at a slope of 8 kN·m per metre and reaches zero at the tip, so at the wall it is

\[ M = -16\ \mathrm{kN\cdot m} \]

The sign is negative: the top is in tension. This is hogging. Since \(V\) never crosses zero, the extreme moment sits at the end of the member, at the wall.

Common mistake

A peak placed anywhere but at a zero of \(V\), a point load or a support has broken \(dM/dx = V\). Reporting the cantilever moment as positive puts compression on the top, which is the simple-beam sign. \(PL/4\) has no place on a cantilever: the tip load's arm about the wall is the whole length.

03Diagrams by areas and jumps

Integrate the two slope rules from one station to the next. The change in shear is minus the load between them. The change in moment is the area under the shear between them:

\[ V_2 - V_1 = -\int_{x_1}^{x_2} w\,dx \] \[ M_2 - M_1 = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} V\,dx \]
What a concentrated load does

A point force \(P\) down is a load packed into no length. Across it the shear drops by \(P\), and the moment only bends a corner. A couple \(M_0\) is a moment packed into no length. Across it the moment jumps and the shear does not change. A clockwise couple raises \(M\) by \(M_0\). A counterclockwise couple lowers it. Walk the beam from the left end, where \(V\) and \(M\) start at the end conditions, and apply these rules load by load. The diagrams close at zero at a free or pinned right end; that is the check on the reactions.

Worked example · an overhang

The lab's overhang is 6 m long, pinned at the left and on a roller at 4 m. A uniform 4 kN/m covers the first 4 m, and 5 kN hangs at the free tip. Moments about the pin: \(4R_B = 16(2) + 5(6)\), so \(R_B = 15.5\) kN and the left reaction is 5.5 kN. The shear starts at +5.5 kN and falls 4 kN per metre. It crosses zero at 1.375 m, where the area under it gives

\[ M = 3.78\ \mathrm{kN\cdot m} \]

The shear reaches \(-10.5\) kN at the roller and jumps up 15.5 kN to +5 kN. The area under it from 1.375 m to 4 m is \(-13.78\) kN·m, so the moment over the roller is

\[ M = -10\ \mathrm{kN\cdot m} \]

Over the overhang the shear is a flat +5 kN, and its area, 10 kN·m, brings the moment back to zero at the tip. The beam sags in the span and hogs over the roller.

Worked example · a couple on the span

The lab's couple case is a 4 m simple span with a 6 kN·m counterclockwise couple at 1 m. Moments about the left pin give the right reaction \(-1.5\) kN, a pull down, and the left reaction +1.5 kN. The shear is +1.5 kN everywhere. The moment rises to 1.5 kN·m at 1 m, then drops by 6 to

\[ M = -4.5\ \mathrm{kN\cdot m} \]

and climbs at 1.5 kN·m per metre back to zero at the right support. The shear diagram never shows the couple. Only the moment does.

Common mistake

A couple does not change the shear, so a diagram that steps \(V\) at a couple is wrong. The direction of the moment jump follows the couple's sense: counterclockwise lowers \(M\) in this convention. Forgetting the roller's reaction on an overhang makes the shear jump the wrong way, and the moment then fails to close at the tip.

What the model leaves out

Loads act on the beam's axis, supports are points, and the beam is straight. A real support spreads its reaction over a bearing length, which rounds the corner in \(M\). Axial force is a separate diagram and does not enter these two.

04Moment and curvature

A moment bends a beam into an arc. Two cross-sections a length \(dx\) apart stay plane and turn toward each other. The fiber on the neutral axis keeps its length. A fiber a distance \(y\) above it lies on a smaller arc.

From geometry to stress

Let the neutral axis bend to a radius \(\rho\). The two faces meet at an angle \(d\theta = dx/\rho\). A fiber \(y\) above the axis has length \((\rho - y)\,d\theta\), so its strain is

\[ \varepsilon = -\frac{y}{\rho} \]

That is pure geometry. Add Hooke's law: \(\sigma = -Ey/\rho\). Sum the stress over the section for its moment about the axis: \(M = \int -y\sigma\,dA = (E/\rho)\int y^{2}\,dA\). The integral is \(I\), so

\[ \frac{1}{\rho} = \frac{M}{EI} \]

A stiffer material or a deeper section bends less under the same moment. \(EI\) is the beam's bending stiffness, and Unit 8 integrates this curvature to find deflections.

Worked example · a band-saw blade

A common blade for a 14 in band saw is 0.025 in thick and runs on 14 in wheels. Its mid-thickness bends to \(\rho = 7.0125\) in at every pass, and its outer fiber strains \((t/2)/\rho = 1{,}783\ \mu\varepsilon\). The blade is steel, \(E = 29{,}000\) ksi:

\[ \sigma = E\,\frac{t/2}{\rho} = 51.7\ \mathrm{ksi} \]

A 1/2 in wide blade has \(I = 6.51\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{in}^4\), so the moment that holds it round the wheel is \(EI/\rho = 2.69\) lbf·in. The moment is small and the stress large, because the wheel fixed the curvature, not the moment. With its 15 ksi set tension the outer fiber reaches 66.7 ksi. On a 10 in wheel the bending alone would be 72.3 ksi. That is why small saws take thin blades, and why blades fail in fatigue at the wheels.

Worked example · the lab's rectangle, curved

The flexure lab's rectangle is 40 mm by 80 mm, steel at 200 GPa, carrying 2 kN·m. \(I = 1.707\times 10^{6}\) mm⁴. The curvature \(M/EI\) gives a radius

\[ \rho = 171\ \mathrm{m} \]

The outer fiber, 40 mm out, has strain \(0.040/171 = 2.34\times 10^{-4}\). Times \(E\), that is 46.9 MPa. Section 5 reaches the same stress from the flexure formula.

Common mistake

The strain uses the distance from the neutral axis, not from the bottom of the section. The radius is that of the neutral axis. Using the wheel's diameter in place of its radius halves the stress.

What the model leaves out

Plane sections stay plane, which is exact under a constant moment and close otherwise. The material is elastic, so the stress follows the strain on a straight line. A deep curve, with \(\rho\) near the depth, is a curved beam, which is outside this course (D37).

05The flexure formula

Section 4 gave \(\sigma = -Ey/\rho\) and \(1/\rho = M/EI\). Put the second into the first, and \(E\) and \(\rho\) leave together:

\[ \sigma = -\frac{My}{I} \]
Where the neutral axis sits

Pure bending carries no axial force: \(\int\sigma\,dA = 0\). With \(\sigma = -Ey/\rho\) that is \(\int y\,dA = 0\), so \(y\) is measured from the centroid. The neutral axis passes through the centroid of the section, whatever its shape, as long as there is one material.

For a rectangle of width \(b\) and depth \(h\), \(I = bh^{3}/12\) and the outer fiber is \(h/2\) out. The outer stresses are equal and opposite, of size \(6M/(bh^{2})\). Roylance §4.2 derives the same formula.

Worked example · the 40 by 80 mm rectangle

The lab's rectangle is 40 mm wide and 80 mm deep, with a sagging moment of 2 kN·m. The two fibers are

\[ \sigma_{\mathrm{top}} = -46.9\ \mathrm{MPa} \] \[ \sigma_{\mathrm{bot}} = 46.9\ \mathrm{MPa} \]

The gold line runs from compression at the top, through zero at mid-height, to tension at the bottom.

Worked example · twice the depth

The same moment on a section 40 mm by 160 mm drops both stresses to a quarter:

\[ \sigma_{\mathrm{top}} = -11.72\ \mathrm{MPa} \] \[ \sigma_{\mathrm{bot}} = 11.72\ \mathrm{MPa} \]

\(I\) grows with the cube of the depth and the fiber distance only with the depth, so the stress goes as \(1/h^{2}\). Doubling the width would only halve it. That is why joists stand on edge.

Common mistake

Using the full depth as the fiber distance doubles the stress. Dropping the sign reports compression as tension. Reversing the moment swaps the fibers: hogging puts the top in tension.

Another view: 2.001 lecture 20 derives the same linear stress from the plane-sections assumption.

06A neutral axis off the middle

A section with no horizontal axis of symmetry has its centroid off mid-height. The two outer fibers then sit at different distances from the neutral axis, and their stresses differ. The farther fiber carries the larger stress. The formula is still \(-My/I\). Only \(y\) and \(I\) change. \(I\) is the sum of each rectangle's own \(bh^{3}/12\) plus its area times the square of its shift to the centroid.

Worked example · the tee at 2 kN·m

The lab's tee has a 120 mm by 12 mm flange on a 12 mm by 100 mm web. The section is 112 mm deep. The flange's area is 1440 mm² at 106 mm up, and the web's is 1200 mm² at 50 mm. The centroid is \((1440\times 106 + 1200\times 50)/2640\), 80.5 mm up from the bottom, not at the 56 mm mid-height. At 2 kN·m sagging,

\[ \sigma_{\mathrm{top}} = -20.5\ \mathrm{MPa} \] \[ \sigma_{\mathrm{bot}} = 52.5\ \mathrm{MPa} \]

The bottom of the web is 80.5 mm out, against 31.5 mm for the top. It carries the larger stress in the ratio of those distances.

Common mistake

Taking \(c = h/2\) for both fibers invents a symmetry the area does not have. Leaving out the parallel-axis terms gives a far smaller \(I\). A cast-iron tee belongs with its flange on the tension side, because the farther fiber carries more and iron is weak in tension.

07A floor joist

A nominal 2×10 is not 2 in by 10 in. The American Softwood Lumber Standard, PS 20, dresses it to 1.5 in by 9.25 in. Southern Pine No. 2, spaced 16 in on center, is listed at a 14 ft maximum span for 40 psf live load and 10 psf dead load when deflection is limited to the span over 360. That span is Table 2 of the Southern Forest Products Association maximum spans, 1 June 2013. The joist carries the strip of floor between its neighbors, so the line load is 50 psf times 16/12 ft.

\[ w = 66.7\ \mathrm{lb/ft} \]

On a simple span the moment is \(wL^{2}/8\). The bending stress is that moment times half the dressed depth, over \(I\). The section modulus is 21.4 in³.

Worked example · the table span

At 14 ft the moment and the bending stress are

\[ M = 1633\ \mathrm{lb\cdot ft} \] \[ \sigma = 916\ \mathrm{psi} \]

The gold curve is this stress against the span. It bends upward because of \(L^{2}\). The teal line marks 14 ft, the span the table allows.

Worked example · a 10 ft span

The same floor on a 10 ft span multiplies the moment by \((10/14)^{2}\). The stress and the moment become

\[ \sigma = 467\ \mathrm{psi} \] \[ M = 833\ \mathrm{lb\cdot ft} \]

Nothing about the board changed. Only the lever arm of the load changed, and it changed with the square of the length.

Worked example · the nominal rectangle

Using 2 in by 10 in, at the same 14 ft load, leaves the moment at 1633 lb·ft and drops the stress to

\[ \sigma = 588\ \mathrm{psi} \]

The nominal name is larger than the dressed board, so it understates the stress. The moment does not care which rectangle was assumed.

What the course model leaves out

916 psi is the elastic bending stress on the dressed section. It is not a published allowable \(F_b\). The 14 ft listing can be set by the deflection limit \(L/360\) rather than by bending. Shear, bearing at the supports, the repetitive-member factor, and load duration are all outside this calculation. The dead load is the 10 psf in the span table, not a measured floor.

08A moment off the principal axes

The flexure formula assumed the moment turns about an axis of symmetry. A load that leans, like the weight on a roof purlin laid on a slope, puts the moment at an angle. Split it into parts about the two principal axes of the section. Each part bends about its own axis, and the stresses add.

Two bendings at once

Take \(y\) up and \(z\) across, both through the centroid and both axes of symmetry. A moment \(M\) tilted \(\theta\) from the \(z\) axis has parts \(M_z = M\cos\theta\) and \(M_y = M\sin\theta\). Each obeys the flexure formula about its own axis:

\[ \sigma = -\frac{M_z\,y}{I_z} + \frac{M_y\,z}{I_y} \]

The sign of the second term follows the right-hand rule for \(M_y\). The neutral axis is where \(\sigma = 0\). It is a line through the centroid at an angle \(\alpha\) from \(z\), with

\[ \tan\alpha = \frac{I_z}{I_y}\tan\theta \]

Unless \(I_z = I_y\), the neutral axis does not line up with the moment. It swings toward the weak axis, so a small tilt of the load makes a large tilt of the axis.

Worked example · the rectangle with a 10° lean

Turn the 2 kN·m on the 40 by 80 mm rectangle 10° off vertical. Then \(M_z = 1.970\) kN·m and \(M_y = 0.347\) kN·m. \(I_z = 1.707\times 10^{6}\) mm⁴ and \(I_y = 0.427\times 10^{6}\) mm⁴, a quarter. At the worst corner the strong-axis part gives 46.2 MPa and the weak-axis part adds 16.3 MPa:

\[ \sigma_{\max} = 62.4\ \mathrm{MPa} \]

Upright, the same moment gave 46.9 MPa. A 10° lean raised the peak by a third. The neutral axis turns to \(\arctan(4\tan 10^\circ) = 35.2^\circ\).

Common mistake

Putting the whole moment into \(Mc/I_z\) misses the weak-axis bending, which is the larger danger on a deep narrow section. The neutral axis is not perpendicular to the load unless the section's two \(I\) values match. Both corner stresses have to be checked: the worst one is where both terms have the same sign.

What the model leaves out

The section here has two axes of symmetry, so its principal axes are known. A Z or angle section has no axis of symmetry. Its principal axes come from the product of inertia, by a Mohr's circle for \(I\), and the same formula then applies on those axes.

Worked example · a purlin on a 4:12 roof

A 2×6 purlin, 1.5 × 5.5 in, spans 8 ft between trusses, set square to a 4:12 roof: \(\theta = 18.4^\circ\). It carries 25 psf on a 2 ft strip, 50 lb/ft, so \(M = wL^{2}/8 = 4{,}800\) lbf·in. Split it on the board's own axes:

\[ \begin{aligned}M_z &= 4800\cos 18.4^\circ = 4554\ \mathrm{lbf\cdot in} \\ M_y &= 4800\sin 18.4^\circ = 1518\ \mathrm{lbf\cdot in}\end{aligned} \]

With \(I_z = 20.8\) and \(I_y = 1.55\ \mathrm{in}^4\), the corner where both add carries

\[ \begin{aligned}\sigma &= \frac{4554(2.75)}{20.8} + \frac{1518(0.75)}{1.55} \\ &= 602 + 736 = 1338\ \mathrm{psi}\end{aligned} \]

Upright the same purlin sees \(6M/bh^{2} = 635\) psi. The neutral axis sits \(\tan^{-1}[(I_z/I_y)\tan\theta] = 77.4^\circ\) from the board's width, almost along its depth.

09Beams of two materials

A timber beam with a steel plate bolted under it bends as one piece. Plane sections still stay plane, so the strain is still linear in \(y\). The stress is not: at the same strain steel carries \(E_s/E_w\) times the stress wood does. The trick is to replace the steel by wood that carries the same force.

The transformed section

Let \(n = E_s/E_w\). A strip of steel of width \(b\) carries the force of a strip of wood of width \(nb\) at the same strain and the same height. So widen every steel part by \(n\) and treat the whole section as wood. The force balance puts the neutral axis at the centroid of this transformed section. Its \(I\) gives the curvature. The stress in the wood is \(-My/I_t\). The stress in the steel is \(n\) times that at its height, because the real steel is narrower and carries the force on less area.

Worked example · a timber with a steel plate

A timber 100 mm wide and 200 mm deep, \(E_w = 12.5\) GPa, has a steel plate 100 mm by 10 mm under it, \(E_s = 200\) GPa. Then \(n = 16\), and the plate becomes 1600 mm of wood 10 mm deep. The transformed centroid is \((20{,}000\times 110 + 16{,}000\times 5)/36{,}000 = 63.3\) mm above the bottom. Parallel-axis sums give

\[ I_t = 164.8\times 10^{6}\ \mathrm{mm}^{4} \]

Under 20 kN·m sagging, the top of the wood is 146.7 mm up from the axis and sees \(-17.8\) MPa. The bottom of the steel is 63.3 mm down and sees \(16\times 7.69\):

\[ \sigma_s = 123\ \mathrm{MPa} \]

The timber alone would see \(6M/bh^{2} = 30\) MPa at each face. The plate cut the wood's peak by 41%.

Common mistake

Reading the steel stress straight off the transformed section misses the factor \(n\): it gives 7.7 MPa, not 123. Widening the wood instead of the steel, or using \(1/n\) on the wrong part, moves the neutral axis the wrong way. The axis is at the transformed centroid, not the geometric one.

What the model leaves out

The two materials are perfectly joined, so they share one strain at the interface. Real bolts slip a little, and a glued joint can creep. Reinforced concrete adds cracking: the concrete below the axis carries no tension. That cracked section is outside this course.

Worked example · a flitch beam

Two No. 2 Southern Pine 2×10s, 1.5 × 9.25 in, sandwich a 3/8 × 9.25 in A36 plate, bolted through. The beam spans 14 ft and carries 8 ft of floor at 50 psf: 400 lb/ft, so \(M = 9{,}800\) lbf·ft. All three parts bend to one curvature, so they add their stiffness:

\[ \begin{aligned}\textstyle\sum EI &= (1.4\times10^{6})(197.9) \\ &\quad + (29\times10^{6})(24.7) \\ &= 994\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{lbf\cdot in^{2}}\end{aligned} \]

The plate carries \(717/994 = 72\%\) of the moment. At the top fiber, 4.625 in from the axis:

\[ \begin{aligned}\sigma_w &= \frac{E_w M c}{\sum EI} = 766\ \mathrm{psi} \\ \sigma_s &= n\,\sigma_w = 20.7(766) = 15.9\ \mathrm{ksi}\end{aligned} \]

Without the plate the two boards would carry 2,749 psi, three times their 920 psi Fb.
