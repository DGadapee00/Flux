Energy methods

Strain energy is the work stored in a member. For a linear material that work is half the load times the travel. The travel is the slope of the energy. A falling weight stores the same work over a short extra stretch, so the peak force rises. A unit load, carried through the real deformation, gives any displacement by one sum.

Part 1 · Stored energy | bar | beam | shaft

01Strain energy in a bar

An axial force on a uniform bar grows from zero when the load is applied slowly. The elongation grows with the force. The work done is the area under that line, and an elastic bar stores all of it.

Energy per volume, then per bar

A small cube under a stress that rises to \(\sigma\) stretches to a strain \(\varepsilon\). The force on a face is \(\sigma\,dA\), and it moves through \(\varepsilon\,dx\), rising linearly, so it does \(\tfrac12\sigma\varepsilon\) of work per unit volume. In a bar \(\sigma = N/A\) and \(\varepsilon = N/AE\) everywhere, and the volume is \(AL\):

\[ U = \frac{N^2 L}{2AE} \]

The same energy is half of \(N\) times the elongation. Differentiating with respect to \(N\) returns the elongation, which Section 3 turns into a method.

\[ \delta = \frac{NL}{AE} \]

E is 200 GPa here, a round value for steel. Doubling N doubles the elongation. The energy multiplies by four, because the force is squared. The gold curve in the lab is that parabola. The pink dot is the present load.

Worked example · the 40 kN bar

The bar is 1.5 m long. Its area is 400 mm². The axial force is 40 kN. The energy and the elongation are

\[ U = 15\ \mathrm{J} \] \[ \delta = 0.75\ \mathrm{mm} \]

Half of 40 kN times 0.75 mm is 15 J. That product is the second path. Doubling the force makes the energy 60 J and the elongation 1.5 mm.

Common mistake

Writing the energy as N times δ forgets the half, and the energy doubles. Dropping E from the elongation leaves a number that is not a length. The energy is not the force times the length over the area.

02Energy in bending and torsion

Bending stores energy in the moment. The bending stress is \(My/I\), so the energy per volume is \(\sigma^{2}/2E = M^{2}y^{2}/(2EI^{2})\). Integrate over the section: \(\int y^{2}\,dA = I\). That leaves \(M^{2}/2EI\) per length of beam:

\[ U = \int_0^L \frac{M^{2}}{2EI}\,dx \]

On a cantilever with an end load \(P\), the moment at a section is \(P\) times the distance back from the tip. The integral gives

\[ U = \frac{P^2 L^3}{6EI} \]

The tip drop is the derivative of that energy with respect to P.

\[ \delta = \frac{PL^3}{3EI} \]

Half of P times the tip drop returns the same energy. A rectangle has \(I = bh^3/12\). Shear stores energy as well. For this rectangle the shear energy is about \(0.78(h/L)^2\) times the bending energy. Here \(h/L\) is 0.04, so shear is about 0.125 percent of the bending energy. The lab curve leaves that share out.

Worked example · the 40 by 80 mm cantilever

The section is 40 mm wide and 80 mm deep. E is 200 GPa. The end load is 800 N and the length is 2 m. The tip drop and the energy are

\[ \delta = 6.25\ \mathrm{mm} \] \[ U = 2.5\ \mathrm{J} \]

Doubling the load sends the drop to 12.5 mm and the energy to 10 J. Doubling the length multiplies the drop by eight. The check is a trapezoid of M squared, and the elastic curve from Unit 8.

A round shaft stores energy in the torque. The same step with \(\tau = T\rho/J\) and \(\tau^{2}/2G\) per volume gives \(T^{2}/2GJ\) per length: \(G\) replaces \(E\), and \(J\) replaces \(I\).

\[ U = \frac{T^2 L}{2GJ} \]

The angle, in radians, is TL/(GJ). Half of T times that angle is the energy again. G in this lab is 75 GPa, the round value from Unit 9. For a solid circle, J is twice I.

Worked example · the 40 mm shaft

The shaft is 40 mm in diameter and 1.2 m long. The torque is 500 N·m. The energy and the angle are

\[ U = 7.96\ \mathrm{J} \] \[ \phi = 1.824^\circ \]

Using E in place of G multiplies both numbers by G/E, which is 0.375. Using I in place of J doubles the angle. Forgetting the half doubles the energy.

Common mistake

The formula with 48 in the denominator is the midspan drop of a beam on two supports. This cantilever uses 3. Shaft energy written with E, or with I, mixes up the section and the modulus.

Part 2 · The derivative | dummy | truss

03Castigliano and the dummy load

Why the slope of the energy is the deflection

Load a linear structure with \(P\), and let the point under \(P\) move \(\delta = P/k\). The energy is \(U = \tfrac12P\delta = P^{2}/2k\). Its derivative is \(dU/dP = P/k = \delta\). With several loads the same holds load by load: raising one load by \(dP\) adds \(\delta\,dP\) of work at its own point, to first order, so \(\partial U/\partial P_i\) is the displacement under \(P_i\), along it. That is Castigliano's theorem.

The derivative has to name the point you want. If the real load sits somewhere else, add a dummy load at the point, differentiate, and then set the dummy to zero. That step is virtual work. The dummy force times the real displacement equals the change in stored energy.

\[ \delta = \frac{\partial U}{\partial Q} \]
Worked example · midspan

The same cantilever carries 800 N at the tip. The question is the drop at midspan, where no real load acts. A dummy at midspan, removed after the derivative, gives

\[ \delta = 1.953\ \mathrm{mm} \]

The tip drop is still 6.25 mm. Half of the tip drop is 3.125 mm, and that is not the midspan value. Moving the dummy out to the tip recovers 6.25 mm. The closed form is P over EI, times L a squared over 2 minus a cubed over 6. The teal dot is that station. The pink dot stays at the tip.

Why the dummy sits at the point

A derivative with respect to the tip load is the tip motion. It cannot name a station the tip load does not mark. Put the dummy on the station you want. Keep it through the derivative. Set it to zero only after that.

Two bars can carry a vertical load the same way. The pins are a span apart. The joint sits a rise above the midpoint. Each bar force is P divided by 2 sin θ, with θ measured from the horizontal. The joint drop is that force times the bar length, divided by AE sin θ. Both bars store energy, so U is the bar force squared times the length, over AE. Half of P times the joint drop matches that energy. The check is a joint solution, and a numerical slope of U.

Worked example · the two bars

P is 4 kN. The span is 4 m and the rise is 0.5 m. Each bar's area is 100 mm². E is 200 GPa. The bar force and the joint drop are

\[ F = 8.25\ \mathrm{kN} \] \[ \delta = 3.5\ \mathrm{mm} \]

The stored energy is 7.01 J. Half of 4 kN times 3.5 mm is that same 7.01 J. Raising the rise to 1 m makes the force 4.47 kN and the drop 1.118 mm. Both get smaller, because the bars stand steeper.

Common mistake

Reading the midspan drop as the tip drop, or as half the tip drop, skips the dummy. On the truss, P/2 is the answer only for a vertical pair. Cos θ in place of sin θ uses the horizontal projection. One vertical bar carrying all of P is a different structure.

Part 3 · A falling load | rod | rope

04Impact: a falling weight

A weight \(W\) hung slowly on a support stretches it by \(\delta_{st} = WL/(AE)\). Now drop it instead. It is released from rest a height \(h\) above the point where it first touches the unloaded support, such as a collar at the end of a rod. It falls \(h\), then stretches the support until it stops. The work done is \(W(h+\delta)\), with \(\delta\) the largest stretch. That work equals \(\tfrac12 k\delta^2\), and \(k = W/\delta_{st}\). Dividing the largest stretch by \(\delta_{st}\) gives the impact factor.

\[ n = 1 + \sqrt{1 + \frac{2h}{\delta_{st}}} \]

The peak force is n times W. A height of zero is not the static case. The square root of 1 is 1, so n is 2. The load arrives suddenly, and the peak is twice the static force. The second check integrates the mass on the spring. The factor itself does not contain g.

Worked example · the 16 mm rod

A steel rod, 16 mm in diameter and 2 m long, carries 1500 N. E is 200 GPa. The weight is a collar released 2 mm above the rod's end stop, with the rod unloaded. The static stretch is 0.0746 mm. The factor and the peak stress are

\[ n = 8.39 \] \[ \sigma = 62.6\ \mathrm{MPa} \]

The static stress is 7.46 MPa. Holding the peak at 80 MPa requires a drop of 3.49 mm, and then n is 10.72. That height comes from the energy balance solved for h.

Worked example · released from contact

The crane rope of the next section is 30 ft long, and the hook load is 400 lbf. Let the rope be just taut but carrying nothing, then let go of the load. The load is released from contact, so the height is zero. The factor is still

\[ n = 2 \]

The peak stress is 6.78 ksi. The static stress is 3.39 ksi. The peak does not fall to the static value. The static stretch of that rope is 0.1 in.

Common mistake

Using the static stress on a real drop takes n as 1. Using a factor of 2 for a drop from a height treats every fall as sudden. A longer support stretches more under the same weight, so the same fall produces a smaller peak.

05A crane rope

A crane rope is a spring with a published area and a published modulus. The Caltrans Falsework Manual, Appendix D, Example 18, lists a new 1/2 in 6×19 IWRC rope. The metallic area is 0.118 in². The nominal strength is 11.5 tons, which is 23000 lbf. The weight is 0.46 lb/ft. That line prints 13.5 million psi and, for loads up to 20 percent of ultimate, 12.2 million psi. The hook loads here stay under that 20 percent, so the model uses 12.2 million psi. The 13.5 million psi figure is not used.

Worked example · the 1 in drop

The length is taken as 30 ft, the hook load as 400 lbf, and the drop as 1 in. None of those three is in the example. The static stretch is 0.1 in. The factor, the peak force, and the peak stress are

\[ n = 5.58 \] \[ F = 2230\ \mathrm{lbf} \] \[ \sigma = 18.92\ \mathrm{ksi} \]

That force is 0.0971 of the 23000 lbf nominal strength. The static stress is 3.39 ksi. Doubling the drop, to 2 in, raises the factor to 7.4 and the force to 2960 lbf. The static stretch stays 0.1 in. The inch-pound path and the SI path are the same rope.

Worked example · 60 ft of rope

Keep the 400 lbf and the 1 in drop, and set the length to 60 ft. The static stretch doubles, to 0.2 in. The peak force and the factor become

\[ F = 1726\ \mathrm{lbf} \] \[ n = 4.32 \]

The longer rope absorbs the fall with a smaller peak. The stress falls with the force, because the area did not change.

What the course model leaves out

Constructional stretch, the sheave, the hook, and the rope's own weight are not in the energy. A design factor of 5 is not applied. The modulus used is 12.2 million psi. The 13.5 million psi number on the same line is not used. The 30 ft length, the 400 lbf hook load, and the 1 in drop are assumptions.

06Unit loads on a truss

Castigliano needs the energy written with a load at the point of interest. The unit-load method reaches the same answer by a shorter road, and it is how truss displacements are found by hand.

Two loadings, one structure

First put a single unit load at the joint, along the direction you want, and nothing else. It sets up member forces \(n\). Keep it there. Now apply the real loads. The joint moves by \(\Delta\) and each member stretches by \(NL/AE\). The unit load rides through \(\Delta\), doing work \(1\cdot\Delta\). Each member's force \(n\), already present, rides through its real stretch, storing \(n\,NL/AE\). Those two works balance:

\[ 1\cdot\Delta = \sum \frac{n\,N\,L}{AE} \]

Two statics solutions, one with the real loads and one with the unit load, give every term. No geometry of the moved joint is needed. Roylance §2.1 finds the same displacements from the stiffness of the bars.

Worked example · the bracket again

Unit 3's bracket has a level bar in 40 kN compression, 2 m long, and a slanted bar in 50 kN tension, 2.5 m long, both with \(AE = 80\) MN. A unit load down at the joint gives \(n = -1.333\) in the level bar and \(+1.667\) in the slanted one. With forces in kN, lengths in m and \(AE\) in kN, each term is in metres:

\[ \begin{aligned}\Delta &= \frac{(-1.333)(-40)(2)}{80{,}000} \\ &\quad + \frac{(1.667)(50)(2.5)}{80{,}000} \\ &= 3.94\ \mathrm{mm}\end{aligned} \]

That is Unit 3's drop, found without solving for the joint's position. A unit load across, outward, puts \(n = 1\) in the level bar and 0 in the slanted one, which returns the 1.00 mm move toward the wall.

Worked example · a five-bar roof truss

A truss spans 4 m with a 1.5 m apex. Bottom chord AB and BC are 2 m each, the rafters AD and DC are 2.5 m, and a hanger BD is 1.5 m. Every bar has \(AE = 80\) MN. 20 kN hangs at B. Statics gives 13.3 kN in each chord, \(-16.7\) kN in each rafter and 20 kN in the hanger. A unit load at B gives the same forces divided by 20 kN. Summing \(nNL/AE\) over the five bars:

\[ \Delta_B = 1.69\ \mathrm{mm} \]

A unit load at the apex gives 1.31 mm there; the difference is the hanger's own 0.375 mm stretch. A unit load across at the roller shows it slides out 0.667 mm. A direct stiffness solve of the whole truss returns the same three numbers.

Common mistake

The signs of \(n\) and \(N\) both matter. A rafter in compression under both loadings contributes a positive term. Using the real force twice, \(N^{2}L/AE\), gives the energy, not the displacement, unless the unit load points along the only real load and is scaled by it. A negative \(\Delta\) means the joint moves opposite to the unit load, not that the work is wrong.

What the model leaves out

The truss is determinate, so both statics solutions exist. For an indeterminate truss the unit load still works but needs a compatibility step, and the direct stiffness solve is simpler. Pins are frictionless and the bars stay elastic.

07Unit loads on a beam

A beam's virtual work replaces the bars' sum with an integral along the length. A unit load at the station sets up a moment \(m(x)\). The real loads bend each slice through an angle \(M\,dx/EI\), and \(m\) rides through it:

\[ 1\cdot\Delta = \int_0^L \frac{m\,M}{EI}\,dx \]

For a slope, use a unit couple at the station instead of a unit force. The same integral returns the rotation in radians.

Worked example · the cantilever at midspan

The 2 m cantilever of Section 3 carries 800 N at the tip, so \(M = -800(2 - x)\). A unit load down at \(a = 1\) m gives \(m = -(1 - x)\) for \(x < 1\) and zero beyond. Then

\[ \begin{aligned}\Delta &= \frac{800}{EI}\int_0^1 (1 - x)(2 - x)\,dx \\ &= \frac{800}{EI}\cdot\frac{5}{6}\end{aligned} \]

With \(EI = 341{,}333\) N·m² that is 1.95 mm, Section 3's dummy-load answer. The unit load did not need a derivative.

Worked example · the end slope of a simple span

The 4 m simple span carries 5 kN/m. A unit couple at the left support gives \(m = 1 - x/L\). The integral of \(m\,M/EI\) is

\[ \theta = \frac{wL^{3}}{24EI} = 0.0391\ \mathrm{rad} \]

That is the end rotation Unit 8 released when it fixed the ends.

Common mistake

\(m\) and \(M\) must use the same sign convention and the same origin of \(x\). Integrating over only the part where \(M\) is nonzero is fine; integrating \(m\) beyond the unit load's reach is not, because \(m\) is zero there for a cantilever but not for a simple span. A unit force gives a displacement, a unit couple a slope.
