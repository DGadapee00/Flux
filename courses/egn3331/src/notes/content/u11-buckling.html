Buckling of columns

A slender column can bow sideways under a load far below yield. That Euler load falls with the square of the effective length, and it follows the weak axis. It is the failure load only while its stress stays under yield. A load off centre bends the column from the start, and a stocky column buckles on the softened part of its stress–strain curve.

Part 1 · Effective length | pinned | free | fixed

01Euler and effective length

A straight elastic column, pinned at both ends, carries its load with no bending at all, until one load where a bowed shape is also in equilibrium. That load is the critical load.

When a bowed shape can stand

Suppose the column has bowed by \(y(x)\). The axial load \(P\) then has a lever arm \(y\), so the moment at a section is \(-Py\), and Unit 8's curve equation reads \(EI\,y'' = -Py\). With \(k^{2} = P/EI\) the solutions are \(y = A\sin kx + B\cos kx\). The pin at \(x = 0\) gives \(B = 0\). The pin at \(x = L\) gives \(A\sin kL = 0\). Either \(A = 0\), the straight column, or \(kL = \pi\), a half sine of any size:

\[ P_{cr} = \frac{\pi^2 EI}{L^2} \]

Below that load only the straight shape balances. At it, the column can hold a bow, and the smallest push sets one off.

Other ends use the same formula on an effective length KL.

\[ P_{cr} = \frac{\pi^2 EI}{(KL)^2} \]

Doubling E doubles the load. Doubling I does the same. Doubling the length divides the load by four.

Fixed-free is a quarter sine. It matches a pinned column of length 2L, so K = 2. Fixed-fixed is a half sine packed into half the length, so K = 1/2. Fixed-pinned solves \(\tan\mu = \mu\). The first root gives K = 0.699. The lab rounds that root to 0.7.

For a rectangle, \(I = bh^3/12\). The depth h is the side the column bows about. The stress is the load divided by the area. The lab checks the pinned formula against a finite-difference eigenvalue on the effective length. The drawing uses the closed form.

The gold curve is the weak-axis load the course model uses, Euler until yield. The teal curve is the strong axis. The yellow line is the yield load. The pink dot is the column on the screen.

Worked example · the 2 m column

The bar is steel, with E = 200 GPa and a yield stress of 250 MPa. The section is 20 mm by 60 mm. The length is 2 m. Both ends are pinned, so K = 1. The weak depth is the 20 mm side.

\[ I = 4.00\times 10^{-8}\ \mathrm{m}^4 \] \[ P_{cr} = 19.74\ \mathrm{kN} \] \[ \sigma_{cr} = 16.45\ \mathrm{MPa} \]

The yield load is 300 kN. The Euler stress is under 250 MPa, so Euler still governs. Doubling the length divides the load by four, to 4.93 kN. The stress falls to 4.11 MPa.

Worked example · the flagpole end

The same bar is fixed at the base and free at the top. K = 2. The load and the stress are a quarter of the pinned values.

\[ P_{cr} = 4.93\ \mathrm{kN} \] \[ \sigma_{cr} = 4.11\ \mathrm{MPa} \]

Fixed at both ends, K = 1/2, the load is 79 kN and the stress is 65.8 MPa. That is still under 250 MPa. Treating the flagpole as pinned multiplies the load by four.

Common mistake

The square sits on KL, the whole effective length. Dropping it, or swapping K with 1/K, moves the load by a large factor. A free end is K = 2. Both pins are K = 1.

Part 2 · The section | weak axis | yield

02Weak axis and yield

The bar can bow about either axis. The weak axis is the smaller depth. I follows the cube of the depth, and the width only once. Turn the 20 mm by 60 mm section and the sides swap. The side ratio is 3. The load ratio is 3 squared, which is 9. The width shrinks by that same 3, so the cube does not all survive.

Set the Euler stress equal to yield and solve for the length. The weak-axis radius of gyration is the depth over the square root of 12. The width cancels.

\[ r = \frac{h}{\sqrt{12}} \] \[ L = \frac{\pi r}{K}\sqrt{\frac{E}{\sigma_Y}} \]

Shorter than that length, the Euler stress is above yield. The course model then stops at the yield load, the area times 250 MPa. It does not report the Euler load as the failure load.

Worked example · the strong axis

The same 2 m bar stays pinned. The depth is now the 60 mm side.

\[ P_{cr} = 177.7\ \mathrm{kN} \] \[ \sigma_{cr} = 148\ \mathrm{MPa} \]

That load is nine times the weak-axis load. The stress is still under 250 MPa, so Euler still governs. A factor of 3 is the side ratio, not the load ratio.

Worked example · the short column

The weak bar stays pinned and the length becomes 0.3 m.

\[ P_{cr} = 877\ \mathrm{kN} \] \[ \sigma_{cr} = 731\ \mathrm{MPa} \]

The yield load is still 300 kN. The Euler stress is above 250 MPa, so the course model uses 300 kN. The weak-axis transition length at K = 1 is 0.513 m. Shorter than that, yield is first.

Why the width cancels

I is \(bh^3/12\) and the area is \(bh\). Their ratio is \(h^2/12\). The radius of gyration is the square root of that ratio, so only the depth remains. A wider bar of the same depth buckles at the same stress.

Common mistake

The first buckle is the weak axis. A factor of 3 is the side ratio. The load ratio is 9. On a short column the Euler load can sit far above yield. That number is not the failure load in this model.

03A basement column

The design-wall area is 1.016 in². I is 1.061 in⁴. The yield load is 42.7 kip. The gold curve is the Euler load. The yellow line is that yield load. The pink dot is the length on the screen. The teal ring is the inside of the tube.

Worked example · the 124 in column

At 124 in the Euler load and the Euler stress are

\[ P_{cr} = 19.75\ \mathrm{kip} \] \[ \sigma_{cr} = 19.44\ \mathrm{ksi} \]

The stress is under 42 ksi, so Euler still governs. The report lists an ASD of 8.7 kip at this length. The Euler load is 2.27 times that allowable. The published ASD is not this Euler load.

At 145 in the Euler load is 14.44 kip and the stress is 14.21 ksi. The report lists an ASD of 6.6 kip. Both lengths are still elastic.

Worked example · the 76 in column

At 76 in the Euler load and the Euler stress are

\[ P_{cr} = 52.6\ \mathrm{kip} \] \[ \sigma_{cr} = 51.7\ \mathrm{ksi} \]

The stress is above 42 ksi. The course model stops at the yield load, 42.7 kip. The report still lists an ASD of 13.5 kip. Those two loads answer different questions.

What the course model leaves out

The AISC column curve and the factors on the allowable are not in this formula. Eccentricity and the end plates are left out. E of 29000 ksi is assumed, because the report does not print a modulus. The design wall is 0.112 in, not the nominal 0.120 in. The listed length includes up to 4 in of adjusting screw, and the screw is not the tube. The ends are free to rotate, so K is 1. The model stops at yield. The published ASD is not the Euler load.

Part 4 · Real columns | eccentric load | tangent modulus | formulas

04An off-centre load: the secant formula

Euler's column is perfectly straight and loaded exactly on its axis. A real column carries its load a little off centre, from a bracket, a bolt hole or a crooked end. Then it bends from the first newton, and the bending grows faster than the load.

The bent column's equation

Let the load \(P\) act a distance \(e\) off the axis of a pinned column. At a section that has bowed by \(y\), the moment is \(P(y + e)\), so \(EI\,y'' = -P(y + e)\). With \(k^{2} = P/EI\), the solution that is zero at both pins bows at the middle to

\[ y_{\max} + e = e\sec\frac{kL}{2} \]

The largest moment is \(Pe\sec(kL/2)\). Add the axial stress and the bending stress at the outer fiber, \(c\) from the axis, and write \(I = Ar^{2}\):

\[ \sigma_{\max} = \frac{P}{A}\left[1 + \frac{ec}{r^{2}}\sec\left(\frac{KL}{2r}\sqrt{\frac{P}{EA}}\right)\right] \]

As \(P\) nears the Euler load, \(kL/2\) nears \(\pi/2\) and the secant runs to infinity. The stress is not proportional to the load, so a factor of safety goes on \(P\), inside the formula, not on the stress.

Worked example · the 2 m bar, 5 mm off centre

Section 1's 20 by 60 mm steel bar, 2 m and pinned, carries 10 kN placed 5 mm off its weak axis. Then \(kL/2 = 1.118\) rad and \(\sec(kL/2) = 2.29\). The middle bows 6.43 mm more, and the peak stress is

\[ \sigma_{\max} = 36.9\ \mathrm{MPa} \]

The average \(P/A\) is only 8.33 MPa. Raise the load until the peak reaches the 250 MPa yield: that happens at 17.5 kN, below the 19.7 kN Euler load. The small offset cost 11% of the load, and far more of the margin.

Common mistake

Using \(P/A + Pec/I\) leaves out the bow: the moment grows by \(\sec(kL/2)\), here 2.29 times. The \(r\) and \(c\) belong to the axis the column bends about, set by where the offset is. Halving the load does not halve the peak stress, so an allowable load is found by solving the formula for \(P\), not by scaling.

What the model leaves out

The offset is the same at both ends and the material stays elastic. Design codes fold eccentricity and initial crookedness into a column curve rather than using the secant formula directly.

Worked example · a deck post with a side-bolted beam

An 8 ft 4×4 post, 3.5 in square, carries 48 ft² of deck at 50 psf: 2,400 lb. Its beam, two 2×10s 3 in thick, is bolted to one face, so the load acts \(e = 1.75 + 1.5 = 3.25\) in off the axis. Here \(A = 12.25\ \mathrm{in}^{2}\), \(r^{2} = I/A = 1.02\ \mathrm{in}^{2}\) and \(E = 1.4\times10^{6}\) psi:

\[ \begin{aligned}\frac{L}{2r}\sqrt{\frac{P}{EA}} &= 47.5\sqrt{\frac{2400}{1.715\times10^{7}}} = 0.562 \\ \sec 0.562 &= 1.18\end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned}\sigma_{\max} &= 196\left[1 + \frac{3.25(1.75)}{1.02}(1.18)\right] \\ &= 1486\ \mathrm{psi}\end{aligned} \]

That is 7.6 times \(P/A\), though the load is only 13% of the 18,750 lb Euler load. On a post cap the same post sees 196 psi.

05Past the elastic line: the tangent modulus

A stocky column reaches a stress where the material's curve is already bending over. Euler's \(E\) is the slope of the straight part of that curve. Near yield the column resists a small bow with the local slope instead, the tangent modulus \(E_t = d\sigma/d\varepsilon\).

Engesser's rule

Replace \(E\) by \(E_t\) in the Euler stress:

\[ \sigma_{cr} = \frac{\pi^{2}E_t}{(KL/r)^{2}} \]

\(E_t\) itself depends on \(\sigma_{cr}\), so the equation is solved by trying stresses. A smooth curve such as Ramberg–Osgood, \(\varepsilon = \sigma/E + 0.002(\sigma/\sigma_Y)^{n}\), gives \(1/E_t = 1/E + 0.002\,n\,\sigma^{n-1}/\sigma_Y^{n}\). For slender columns \(E_t = E\) and the rule is Euler. For stocky ones it bends the curve down toward yield instead of letting it climb.

Worked example · an aluminium strut

Take an aluminium alloy with \(E = 70\) GPa, a 0.2% yield of 240 MPa and \(n = 20\), assumed values typical of a heat-treated grade. At a slenderness \(KL/r = 40\), Euler gives 432 MPa, far above yield. Solving Engesser's rule gives

\[ \sigma_{cr} = 211\ \mathrm{MPa} \]

with \(E_t = 34.3\) GPa there, about half of \(E\). At \(KL/r = 60\) the two are close, 182 against 192 MPa. At 80 they agree at 108 MPa: the column buckles while the material is still on its straight line.

Common mistake

Capping Euler at yield, as Section 2 does, overstates a column near the transition, where the curve has already softened. Using \(E_t\) at the yield stress rather than at the buckling stress answers a different column. The slenderness is \(KL/r\), with \(r\) on the weak axis.

What the model leaves out

Engesser's rule ignores the unloading on the convex side of the bow, which a reduced-modulus theory adds; tests fall close to the tangent-modulus value. Residual stresses from rolling or welding soften steel columns the same way, and design curves include them. Inelastic behaviour beyond this rule is outside the course (D37).

Worked example · a 6061-T6 tube column

A 6061-T6 tube, 2 in across with a 0.125 in wall, stands 36 in tall with pinned ends. Its \(r = 0.664\) in, so \(KL/r = 54.2\). Euler gives \(\pi^{2}(10{,}000)/54.2^{2} = 33.6\) ksi, close to the 35 ksi yield, where the alloy has already softened. Take the Ramberg–Osgood curve with \(n = 20\). The stress that satisfies

\[ \sigma = \frac{\pi^{2}E_t(\sigma)}{(KL/r)^{2}} \]

is 28.2 ksi, where \(E_t = 8{,}400\) ksi. The tube buckles at 20.8 kip, not Euler's 24.7.

06Formula sheet

Pinned Euler load. Other ends replace L with KL. K is 1 for both pins, 2 for fixed-free, and 1/2 for both ends fixed. The fixed-pinned root is 0.699, and the lab rounds it to 0.7.

\[ P_{cr} = \frac{\pi^2 EI}{(KL)^2} \]

A rectangle uses the depth h perpendicular to the axis. The weak axis is the smaller side.

\[ I = \frac{bh^3}{12} \]

The transition length is where the Euler stress equals yield. Shorter than this, stop at the yield load \(\sigma_Y A\).

\[ L = \frac{\pi r}{K}\sqrt{\frac{E}{\sigma_Y}} \]

An eccentric load, and the tangent modulus:

\[ \sigma_{\max} = \frac{P}{A}\left[1 + \frac{ec}{r^{2}}\sec\left(\frac{KL}{2r}\sqrt{\frac{P}{EA}}\right)\right] \] \[ \sigma_{cr} = \frac{\pi^{2}E_t}{(KL/r)^{2}} \]

The adjustable column uses that same Euler expression. With E in ksi and I in in⁴, the load is already in kip.
