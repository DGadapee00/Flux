Multiaxial stress and pressure vessels

A thin closed cylinder carries twice as much stress around the wall as along it. A sphere carries only the smaller of those two. A stress in a second direction changes the strain along the first. Three gauges at three angles recover every strain on a surface, and from them the stresses.

Part 1 · Pressure in a thin wall | cylinder | sphere

01A closed thin cylinder

Cut a closed cylinder lengthwise and look at one half. The pressure acts on a projected rectangle of width \(2r\) and length \(L\). The force is \(2prL\). Two walls, each of thickness \(t\), carry that force. The stress around the wall is the hoop stress:

\[ \sigma_h = \frac{pr}{t} \]

A second cut, across the cylinder, exposes a circle of radius \(r\). The pressure on that circle is \(\pi r^{2}p\). The wall around the rim has area \(2\pi rt\). The stress along the cylinder is half the hoop stress:

\[ \sigma_l = \frac{pr}{2t} \]

Roylance §2.2 derives both from these free bodies. The radius is the inside radius. The wall is thin when \(r/t\) is large, so the stress is nearly the same through the thickness. Double the pressure or the radius and both stresses double. Double the thickness and both halve. The ratio of hoop to longitudinal stays 2.

Why the hoop is the larger one

The lengthwise split sees the pressure on a diameter. The cross cut sees the pressure on a circle, and the circle's area grows with \(r^{2}\) while the rim grows with \(r\). Those two balances differ by exactly a factor of two for a closed tube. An open tube has no longitudinal stress from the pressure, because nothing closes the ends.

Worked example · a closed cylinder at 2 MPa

The Thin-walled vessel lab's cylinder has an inside radius of 200 mm and a wall of 5 mm. The pressure is 2 MPa. Then \(r/t = 40\), which is thin. With pressure in MPa and radius and thickness in mm, \(pr/t\) is the hoop stress in MPa:

\[ \sigma_h = 80\ \mathrm{MPa} \]

The longitudinal stress is half of that:

\[ \sigma_l = 40\ \mathrm{MPa} \]

The gold line on the lab chart is the hoop stress. The teal line is the longitudinal stress. Both pass through the origin, because a gauge pressure of zero leaves both stresses at zero.

Worked example · the thinner cylinder

The same pressure and radius with a wall of 2 mm gives \(r/t = 100\). Then \(pr/t\) is 2 MPa times \(200/2\):

\[ \sigma_h = 200\ \mathrm{MPa} \]

The longitudinal stress is

\[ \sigma_l = 100\ \mathrm{MPa} \]

Only the thickness changed. The hoop stress is still twice the longitudinal stress.

Common mistake

Using the diameter in place of the radius doubles both answers. Reporting the longitudinal stress when the hoop was asked, or the hoop when the longitudinal was asked, swaps the factor of two. The two stresses are equal only in a sphere.

Another view: 2.001 lecture 15 cuts the same cylinder and the same sphere.

02A thin sphere

Cut a sphere through a great circle and keep one hemisphere. The pressure on the flat circle is \(\pi r^{2}p\). The wall around the rim has area \(2\pi rt\). Every membrane direction is that same cut, so there is one stress:

\[ \sigma = \frac{pr}{2t} \]

That is the cylinder's longitudinal stress, and half of the cylinder's hoop stress, at the same pressure, radius and thickness. A sphere has no direction that carries \(pr/t\).

Worked example · the sphere at the same pressure

The lab's sphere uses the cylinder's numbers: 2 MPa, inside radius 200 mm, wall 5 mm. The cap balance \(\pi r^{2}p = \sigma(2\pi rt)\) gives

\[ \sigma = 40\ \mathrm{MPa} \]

The hoop and longitudinal readouts match. On the chart the two lines lie on top of each other.

Common mistake

The cylinder hoop formula \(pr/t\) is twice the sphere stress. Using the diameter and also forgetting the 2 in the denominator is four times the sphere stress. A sphere does not have a larger hoop and a smaller longitudinal stress.

What the model leaves out

The wall is thin, the pressure is uniform, and the vessel has no nozzle, weld or head. A thick wall varies through the thickness, and that needs a different solution. A cylinder with domed ends is a cylinder in the long shell and a different shape near the head.

Part 2 · A tank, then three stresses | propane | Hooke | gauge

03A propane cylinder

A grill tank is a closed thin cylinder. Manchester Tank model 10504 is 12.2 in in outside diameter and is marked DOT-4BA240. The 240 in that mark is the service pressure in psig. 49 CFR 178.51 requires a wall of at least 0.078 in when the outside diameter exceeds 6 in. This lab uses that minimum, not a measured wall.

The inside radius is half the outside diameter, minus the wall. In inches that is \(6.1 - 0.078\):

\[ r = 6.022\ \mathrm{in} \]

The ratio \(r/t\) is about 77, so the thin-wall formulas apply. With pressure in psi and radius and thickness in inches, \(pr/t\) is the hoop stress in psi. Divide by 1000 for ksi. The longitudinal stress is half.

Worked example · service pressure on the minimum wall

At 240 psi the hoop stress is pressure times 6.022 in, divided by 0.078 in, then divided by 1000:

\[ \sigma_h = 18.53\ \mathrm{ksi} \]

The longitudinal stress is half:

\[ \sigma_l = 9.26\ \mathrm{ksi} \]

Half the service pressure halves both stresses. The chart is a straight line through the origin. The same arithmetic in pascals and metres is the second method, and it agrees.

Common mistake

The outside diameter is not the radius. Putting 12.2 in into \(pr/t\) is about twice the hoop stress. Leaving the answer in psi and labeling it ksi is a factor of 1000. Using the outside radius instead of the inside radius misses by only about one percent, which is inside the grading tolerance, so it is not a useful check.

What the course model leaves out

The shell is a long closed cylinder at the service pressure. A real cylinder has domed heads, a weld, and a wall that may be thicker than the 0.078 in minimum. The regulation computes a wall stress with \(S = P(1.3D^{2} + 0.4d^{2})/(D^{2} - d^{2})\) and applies it at the test pressure. The course answer stays \(pr/t\) at the pressure in hand. The lab prints the regulation formula beside the thin-wall stresses so the two can be compared. It is not the answer.

04Hooke's law in three directions

One normal stress produces a strain \(\sigma/E\) along itself and a strain \(-\nu\sigma/E\) on the sides. Apply \(\sigma_x\) alone: the strains are \(\sigma_x/E\), \(-\nu\sigma_x/E\) and \(-\nu\sigma_x/E\). Apply \(\sigma_y\) alone, then \(\sigma_z\) alone, and write their strains the same way. The material is linear, so the strains of the three loadings add. Each strain takes its own stress over \(E\) and subtracts Poisson's share of the other two. Roylance §2.2 writes the three equations. With \(E = 200\) GPa and \(\nu = 0.30\) in the lab:

\[ \varepsilon_x = \frac{1}{E}\left[\sigma_x - \nu(\sigma_y + \sigma_z)\right] \]

Swap the labels for \(\varepsilon_y\) and \(\varepsilon_z\). The volume change per unit volume is the sum of the three normal strains. A pure pressure, with \(\sigma_x = \sigma_y = \sigma_z\), gives a volume strain \((1 - 2\nu)\) times \(3\sigma/E\). Shear strains follow the shear stresses and do not appear in these three.

The same law, split another way

Write the stress as a hydrostatic piece, the average of the three normal stresses, plus whatever is left. The average changes the volume through \((1-2\nu)/E\). The leftover changes the shape through \((1+\nu)/E\). Adding those two pieces returns the same three strains. The lab checks the two writings against each other at every slider stop.

Worked example · uniaxial tension at 100 MPa

The Three-dimensional Hooke lab's uniaxial case is \(\sigma_x = 100\) MPa and the other two stresses zero. Then \(\varepsilon_x = \sigma_x/E\):

\[ \varepsilon_x = 0.5\ \text{millistrain} \]

The side strains are \(-\nu\) times that:

\[ \varepsilon_y = -0.15\ \text{millistrain} \]

The gold dashed line on the chart is \(\sigma_x/E\). With \(\sigma_y = 0\) the teal curve crosses it.

Worked example · equal biaxial tension

Add \(\sigma_y = 100\) MPa and keep \(\sigma_z = 0\). The axial strain loses \(\nu\sigma_y\):

\[ \varepsilon_x = 0.35\ \text{millistrain} \]

That is \((1-\nu)\sigma_x/E\). The strain along \(y\) equals it. An equal transverse tension makes the axial strain smaller than \(\sigma_x/E\). An equal transverse compression makes it larger, because the minus sign in front of \(\nu\) flips.

Worked example · hydrostatic tension

Set all three stresses to 100 MPa. Each strain subtracts \(\nu\) times the other two, which is \(0.6\) times 100 MPa:

\[ \varepsilon_x = 0.2\ \text{millistrain} \]

The three normal strains are equal, so the volume strain is three times 0.2 millistrain:

\[ \frac{\Delta V}{V} = 600\ \mu\varepsilon \]

A second tensile stress does not have to increase \(\varepsilon_x\). Here two of them cut it from 0.5 millistrain to 0.2.

Common mistake

Using \(\sigma_x/E\) while a transverse stress is present drops the Poisson terms. Adding \(\nu\sigma_y\) instead of subtracting it reverses the effect of a transverse tension. The side strain of a uniaxial bar, \(-\nu\sigma_x/E\), is not \(\varepsilon_y\) once \(\sigma_y\) has its own term.

Another view: 2.001 lecture 14 writes the same three strain equations from the hydrostatic part and the deviator.

05The stress a gauge does not name

A single gauge reads \(\varepsilon_x\). If the only stress were \(\sigma_x\), that reading would be \(\sigma_x/E\). When the reading is different, a stress the gauge does not point along is still in the equation. With \(\sigma_z = 0\), solve the strain equation for the unknown:

\[ \sigma_y = \frac{\sigma_x - E\varepsilon_x}{\nu} \]

\(\sigma_x - E\varepsilon_x\) is not yet the stress. Poisson's ratio still divides it. A reading equal to \(\sigma_x/E\) sends \(\sigma_y\) to zero, which is the uniaxial bar.

Worked example · the gauge that reads 0.35 millistrain

Take \(E = 200\) GPa, \(\nu = 0.30\) and \(\sigma_x = 100\) MPa. The gauge reads 0.35 millistrain, and \(\sigma_z = 0\). Then \(E\varepsilon_x = 70\) MPa. The numerator is \(100 - 70\):

\[ \sigma_y = 100\ \mathrm{MPa} \]

That is the lab's equal biaxial case. The axial strain is 0.35 millistrain, smaller than the 0.5 millistrain of \(\sigma_x/E\), because the hidden stress is tension.

Common mistake

Stopping at \(\sigma_x - E\varepsilon_x\) leaves the answer short by the factor \(\nu\). Reporting zero assumes the gauge matches \(\sigma_x/E\). If it does not, a transverse stress is present.

What the model leaves out

The material is isotropic and still on the elastic line. One gauge cannot separate \(\sigma_y\) from \(\sigma_z\). The lab sets \(\sigma_z\) to zero so the remaining unknown is one number. Three gauges at three angles can, which is Section 8.

Part 3 · Strain on any axis | turned gauge | strain circle | rosette | formulas

06Strain on a turned axis

A gauge reads the stretch along one line. Two normal strains and one shear strain describe the surface, so a gauge at any angle reads some mix of the three. This section finds the mix from geometry alone. No material law enters, so the result holds for steel, rubber or bone.

A short line, moved

Take a short line of length \(ds\) along the unit vector \(\mathbf{n} = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\). Its far end moves relative to its near end by \(du = (\varepsilon_x\,dx + \tfrac12\gamma_{xy}\,dy)\) across and \(dv = (\tfrac12\gamma_{xy}\,dx + \varepsilon_y\,dy)\) up, once the rigid turn is set aside. Here \(dx = ds\cos\theta\) and \(dy = ds\sin\theta\). The line's stretch is the part of that move along \(\mathbf{n}\), divided by \(ds\):

\[ \varepsilon_\theta = \varepsilon_x\cos^{2}\theta + \varepsilon_y\sin^{2}\theta + \gamma_{xy}\sin\theta\cos\theta \]

The double-angle identities turn that into

\[ \varepsilon_\theta = \frac{\varepsilon_x+\varepsilon_y}{2} + \frac{\varepsilon_x-\varepsilon_y}{2}\cos 2\theta + \frac{\gamma_{xy}}{2}\sin 2\theta \]

The same algebra on the right angle between \(\mathbf{n}\) and its perpendicular gives the shear strain on the turned axes:

\[ \frac{\gamma_{\theta}}{2} = -\frac{\varepsilon_x-\varepsilon_y}{2}\sin 2\theta + \frac{\gamma_{xy}}{2}\cos 2\theta \]

Half the shear strain plays the part the shear stress plays in Unit 5. Every result there carries over with \(\sigma\) read as \(\varepsilon\) and \(\tau\) read as \(\gamma/2\).

Worked example · a gauge at 30°

A steel surface has \(\varepsilon_x = 500\ \mu\varepsilon\), \(\varepsilon_y = -100\ \mu\varepsilon\) and \(\gamma_{xy} = 400\ \mu\mathrm{rad}\). A gauge at 30° from \(x\) has \(\cos^{2}\theta = 0.75\), \(\sin^{2}\theta = 0.25\) and \(\sin\theta\cos\theta = 0.433\). It reads \(375 - 25 + 173\):

\[ \varepsilon_{30} = 523\ \mu\varepsilon \]

The double-angle form agrees: \(200 + 300(0.5) + 200(0.866) = 523\ \mu\varepsilon\). A gauge at 120° reads \(-123\ \mu\varepsilon\). The two perpendicular readings add to 400, which is \(\varepsilon_x + \varepsilon_y\). That sum does not change as the axes turn.

Common mistake

The shear term is \(\gamma_{xy}\sin\theta\cos\theta\), or \(\tfrac12\gamma_{xy}\sin 2\theta\). Writing \(\gamma_{xy}\sin 2\theta\) doubles it, which here gives 696 \(\mu\varepsilon\) at 30°. A positive \(\gamma_{xy}\) closes the right angle between \(+x\) and \(+y\), so it stretches the 45° line and shortens the 135° line.

What the model leaves out

Strains are small, so a line's turn does not feed back into its stretch. The gauge reads the average strain under its grid, which is fine where the strain changes slowly. Near a hole the strain changes over a gauge length, and a small gauge or a field method is needed.

Another view: 2.001 lecture 17 turns the strain axes and draws the strain circle.

07Mohr's circle for strain

The two turned strains trace a circle as \(\theta\) runs. Plot \(\varepsilon\) across and \(\gamma/2\) down. The point for the \(x\) axis is \((\varepsilon_x, \gamma_{xy}/2)\). The point for the \(y\) axis is \((\varepsilon_y, -\gamma_{xy}/2)\). They sit at opposite ends of a diameter, and turning the gauge by \(\theta\) turns the point by \(2\theta\).

Centre and radius

Move the constant \((\varepsilon_x+\varepsilon_y)/2\) to the left of the turned-axis equations. Square both and add. The \(\theta\) terms collapse to one constant:

\[ C = \frac{\varepsilon_x+\varepsilon_y}{2} \] \[ R = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\varepsilon_x-\varepsilon_y}{2}\right)^{2} + \left(\frac{\gamma_{xy}}{2}\right)^{2}} \]

The principal strains are \(C \pm R\), on axes with no shear strain. They lie at \(\tan 2\theta_p = \gamma_{xy}/(\varepsilon_x - \varepsilon_y)\). The largest in-plane shear strain is \(2R\), on axes 45° from the principal ones.

Worked example · the principal strains

Use the strains of Section 6. The centre is 200 \(\mu\varepsilon\). The radius is \(\sqrt{300^{2} + 200^{2}}\):

\[ R = 361\ \mu\varepsilon \]

So \(\varepsilon_1 = 561\ \mu\varepsilon\) and \(\varepsilon_2 = -161\ \mu\varepsilon\). The angle is \(\tfrac12\arctan(400/600) = 16.8^\circ\) from \(x\), toward \(y\). A gauge at 16.8° reads 561 \(\mu\varepsilon\), the most any gauge on this surface can read. The largest shear strain is 721 \(\mu\mathrm{rad}\).

Common mistake

The vertical axis is \(\gamma/2\). Plotting \(\gamma\) makes the radius \(\sqrt{300^{2}+400^{2}} = 500\ \mu\varepsilon\), which is wrong. Reporting \(R\) as the largest shear strain halves it. The largest shear strain is the diameter, \(2R\).

What the model leaves out

This is the circle for the surface plane. The third principal strain, normal to a free surface, is not zero even though the stress there is. For an isotropic part in plane stress it is \(-\nu(\varepsilon_x+\varepsilon_y)/(1-\nu)\). The largest shear strain in three dimensions can use it.

08Strain rosettes

A gauge cannot read shear strain. Three gauges at three angles can. Each reading is one equation from Section 6, and three readings fix \(\varepsilon_x\), \(\varepsilon_y\) and \(\gamma_{xy}\). Section 5 could not separate the stresses with one gauge. A rosette can.

The two common layouts

A rectangular rosette has gauges at 0°, 45° and 90°. The 0° and 90° gauges read \(\varepsilon_x\) and \(\varepsilon_y\) directly. The 45° gauge reads \(\tfrac12(\varepsilon_x + \varepsilon_y + \gamma_{xy})\). Solve it for the shear:

\[ \gamma_{xy} = 2\varepsilon_b - \varepsilon_a - \varepsilon_c \]

A delta rosette has gauges at 0°, 60° and 120°. The same three equations give \(\varepsilon_y = (2\varepsilon_b + 2\varepsilon_c - \varepsilon_a)/3\) and \(\gamma_{xy} = (2/\sqrt3)(\varepsilon_b - \varepsilon_c)\).

From strains to stresses

On a free surface \(\sigma_z = 0\). Section 4's two equations, \(E\varepsilon_x = \sigma_x - \nu\sigma_y\) and \(E\varepsilon_y = \sigma_y - \nu\sigma_x\), can be solved for the stresses. Multiply the second by \(\nu\) and add it to the first:

\[ \sigma_x = \frac{E}{1-\nu^{2}}\left(\varepsilon_x + \nu\varepsilon_y\right) \]

Swap \(x\) and \(y\) for \(\sigma_y\). The shear stress is \(G\gamma_{xy}\), with \(G = E/(2(1+\nu))\).

Worked example · a rectangular rosette on steel

The three gauges read 500, 400 and \(-100\ \mu\varepsilon\). Then \(\varepsilon_x = 500\), \(\varepsilon_y = -100\), and \(\gamma_{xy} = 800 - 500 + 100 = 400\ \mu\mathrm{rad}\). These are the strains of Section 6. With \(E = 200\) GPa and \(\nu = 0.30\), \(E/(1-\nu^{2}) = 220\) GPa, and

\[ \sigma_x = 103\ \mathrm{MPa} \]

\(\sigma_y = 220(-100 + 150)\times 10^{-6}\) GPa, which is 11.0 MPa. \(G = 76.9\) GPa, so \(\tau_{xy} = 30.8\) MPa. The second method uses the principal strains of Section 7 in the same formula: \(220(561 - 48)\times 10^{-6}\) gives \(\sigma_1 = 113\) MPa. Unit 5's circle, drawn from the three stresses, gives \(57.1 + 55.5 = 113\) MPa as well.

Worked example · the same surface, a delta rosette

Put a delta rosette on the same spot. Its gauges read 500, 223 and \(-123\ \mu\varepsilon\), from Section 6's formula at 0°, 60° and 120°. Then \(\varepsilon_y = (446 - 246 - 500)/3 = -100\ \mu\varepsilon\), and \(\gamma_{xy} = 1.155\times 346 = 400\ \mu\mathrm{rad}\). Two layouts, one surface, the same three strains.

Common mistake

The middle gauge is not \(\gamma_{xy}\). Here it reads 400 \(\mu\varepsilon\) and \(\gamma_{xy}\) is also 400 \(\mu\mathrm{rad}\), a coincidence of these numbers. In general the shear is \(2\varepsilon_b - \varepsilon_a - \varepsilon_c\). Using \(\sigma_x = E\varepsilon_x\) on a rosette drops the Poisson coupling and gives 100 MPa, not 103. A gauge's own angle has to match the formula's: swapping gauges b and c flips the sign of the shear.

What the model leaves out

The surface is free and the material is isotropic and elastic. Each gauge has a small cross-sensitivity to strain across its grid, and a temperature change adds an apparent strain unless the gauge is matched to the metal. Those corrections belong to the lab bench, not to this algebra.

09Formula sheet

Closed thin cylinder, inside radius:

\[ \sigma_h = \frac{pr}{t} \] \[ \sigma_l = \frac{pr}{2t} \]

Thin sphere, the same in every membrane direction:

\[ \sigma = \frac{pr}{2t} \]

Normal strain when all three normal stresses act:

\[ \varepsilon_x = \frac{\sigma_x - \nu(\sigma_y + \sigma_z)}{E} \]

The transverse stress hidden in a measured \(\varepsilon_x\), when \(\sigma_z = 0\):

\[ \sigma_y = \frac{\sigma_x - E\varepsilon_x}{\nu} \]

A gauge at \(\theta\), and the strain circle (\(\gamma/2\) on the vertical axis):

\[ \varepsilon_\theta = \frac{\varepsilon_x+\varepsilon_y}{2} + \frac{\varepsilon_x-\varepsilon_y}{2}\cos 2\theta + \frac{\gamma_{xy}}{2}\sin 2\theta \] \[ \varepsilon_{1,2} = \frac{\varepsilon_x+\varepsilon_y}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{\varepsilon_x-\varepsilon_y}{2}\right)^{2} + \left(\frac{\gamma_{xy}}{2}\right)^{2}} \]

A 0-45-90 rosette, and plane stress from strain:

\[ \gamma_{xy} = 2\varepsilon_b - \varepsilon_a - \varepsilon_c \] \[ \sigma_x = \frac{E}{1-\nu^{2}}\left(\varepsilon_x + \nu\varepsilon_y\right) \]
