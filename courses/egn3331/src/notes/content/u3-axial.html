Axial load

A bar pulled along its axis gets longer by the sum of \(NL/AE\). When both ends are held, statics is one equation short, and a temperature change produces stress even though the length does not change.

Part 1 · How far the end moves | uniform | step | taper | truss joint

01Elongation of a uniform bar

On a uniform bar the internal force \(N\) is the same at every cross-section, and so is the area. The strain is \(N/AE\). Multiply by the length and the end has moved:

\[ \delta = \frac{NL}{AE} \]

Roylance §1.1 introduces this as the elastic elongation. Double the force or the length and the elongation doubles. Double the area or the modulus and it halves. The stress \(N/A\) does not contain \(L\) or \(E\).

Where the sum comes from

Slice the bar into short pieces of length \(dx\). Each piece sees the internal force at that station and stretches by \(N\,dx/AE\). Add the pieces:

\[ \delta = \int_0^L \frac{N(x)}{A(x)\,E}\,dx \]

When \(N\), \(A\) and \(E\) are constant the integral is the fraction above. When they jump, split the integral at the jump and add the pieces. That is the whole of this unit's deformation work.

Worked example · a uniform bar at 20 kN

The Axial bar lab's uniform case is a steel bar with \(E = 200\) GPa, area 200 mm² and a tension of 20 kN. The first segment is 0.8 m and the second is 0.6 m, with the same area and no load at the join, so the bar is 1.4 m of one cross-section. The stress is 100 MPa. With \(P\) in kN, \(L\) in m, \(A\) in mm² and \(E\) in GPa, \(PL/(AE)\) equals 0.000700 m, which is

\[ \delta = 0.700\ \mathrm{mm} \]

The lab's end dot sits on that displacement.

Worked example · one truss member

The same fraction is the change of length of a truss member, once the method of joints has given the force. A member carrying 30 kN in tension, 2 m long, with area 200 mm² and \(E = 200\) GPa, lengthens by \(PL/AE\):

\[ \delta = 1.50\ \mathrm{mm} \]

A joint moves by whatever displacement makes every member's \(NL/AE\) fit together. Unit 1 found the forces. This unit supplies the change of length. Section 3 fits the joints together. This unit's lab draws one bar, which is the member.

Common mistake

Feet, inches, millimetres and metres do not mix inside \(NL/AE\). Leaving a length in millimetres while \(A\) is in square metres multiplies the elongation by 1000. Reporting the stress, in MPa, as if it were the elongation forgets the factor \(L/E\).

What the model leaves out

The bar is straight, the load is along the axis, and the material is still on the elastic line. A load off the axis adds bending. Past yield the modulus in this fraction is no longer \(E\). A truss joint is taken as a pin, so a member carries force and no moment.

02Steps and tapers

A step in the load, or in the area, is a corner in the integral. Cut the bar there. On each side \(N\) and \(A\) are constant, so each side contributes its own \(NL/AE\). The end displacement is the sum. The internal-force diagram, not the external load alone, is what enters \(N\).

A taper is not the average area

A circular bar whose diameter runs linearly from \(d_0\) to \(d_1\) has a constant force and a changing area. Put \(A = \pi d(x)^{2}/4\) into the integral. The result is

\[ \delta = \frac{4PL}{\pi E d_0 d_1} \]

The product \(d_0 d_1\) is not the square of the average diameter, and it is not the average of the two end areas. Those two shortcuts both miss, because \(1/d^{2}\) is not a straight line.

Worked example · the stepped bar

The lab's stepped bar has \(E = 200\) GPa. The first segment is 0.4 m long and 300 mm². The second is 0.6 m long and 150 mm². A tension of 12 kN pulls at the step and 20 kN pulls the free end. The first segment carries both loads, so \(N_1 = 32\) kN and \(\delta_1 = 0.213\) mm. The second segment carries only the end load, so \(N_2 = 20\) kN and \(\delta_2 = 0.400\) mm. The free end moves

\[ \delta = 0.613\ \mathrm{mm} \]

The largest internal force is 32 kN. The gold line on the lab chart is the step, and the slope of the displacement changes there.

Worked example · the tapered bar

The lab's taper runs from 40 mm diameter to 20 mm over 0.6 m, with a tension of 30 kN and \(E = 200\) GPa. The closed form gives

\[ \delta = 0.1432\ \mathrm{mm} \]

An average of the two end areas gives a different number, and so does a constant bar at the 30 mm mean diameter. The lab checks the closed form against a numerical integral of \(P/(AE)\).

Common mistake

The step load does not run through the whole bar. It joins \(N\) only on the side between the step and the support. Using the end load on both segments drops \(Q\). Using the smaller area for the whole length, or averaging the two end areas of a taper, answers a different bar.

What the model leaves out

A real step has a fillet. The stress at the fillet is higher than \(N/A\), which is the stress-concentration problem and not this integral. The taper formula assumes the diameter changes in a straight line and the force stays on the axis. A shoulder that is abrupt is a step, not a taper.

03How a truss joint moves

A truss joint hangs on two bars. Statics gives each bar its force, and \(NL/AE\) gives each bar its change of length. The joint has to land where both bars, changed, still reach it. That condition fixes how far it moves.

One projection per bar

Let the joint move by \(u\) across and \(v\) up. Bar \(i\) points from its fixed pin to the joint along the unit vector \(\mathbf{e}_i = (\cos\theta_i, \sin\theta_i)\). For a small move, the bar's change of length is the part of the move along the bar:

\[ \delta_i = u\cos\theta_i + v\sin\theta_i \]

The part of the move across the bar only swings it about its pin. To first order that does not change its length. Two bars give two equations, and \(u\) and \(v\) follow. A bar in compression has a negative \(\delta\).

Worked example · a 3-4-5 bracket

A level bar 2 m long and a slanted bar from a pin 1.5 m up the wall meet at a joint 2 m out. The slanted bar is 2.5 m long. Both are steel, 400 mm², and the joint carries 30 kN down. Vertical equilibrium at the joint gives the slanted bar \(30/0.6\), so 50 kN in tension. Horizontal equilibrium gives the level bar \(50\times 0.8\), so 40 kN in compression. Their changes of length are

\[ \delta_h = -1.00\ \mathrm{mm} \] \[ \delta_s = +1.56\ \mathrm{mm} \]

The level bar lies along \(x\), so \(u = -1.00\) mm: the joint moves toward the wall. The slanted bar points along \((0.8, -0.6)\) from its pin, so \(0.8u - 0.6v = 1.5625\) mm. Then \(-0.6v = 2.3625\) mm, and the joint drops

\[ v = -3.94\ \mathrm{mm} \]

The drop is larger than either bar's change of length. A slanted bar swings, so a small stretch along it becomes a larger move across it.

The energy check

The load rises from zero, so it does work \(\tfrac12 P|v|\). The bars store \(N^{2}L/2AE\) each. The two must match:

\[ \tfrac12 (30\ \mathrm{kN})(3.94\ \mathrm{mm}) = 59.1\ \mathrm{J} \]

The level bar stores 20.0 J and the slanted bar 39.1 J, which add to 59.1 J. Unit 10 turns this balance into a method that finds a displacement without drawing the joint at all.

Common mistake

The drop is not the slanted bar's stretch. Dividing that stretch by \(\sin\theta\) alone gives 2.60 mm, which leaves out the level bar's shortening. A bar in compression shortens, so its \(\delta\) carries a minus sign into the equations. Mixing that sign up moves the joint the wrong way.

What the model leaves out

The moves are small next to the bars, so statics uses the undeformed shape and each bar's length change is one projection. The pins turn without friction. A slender bar in compression could buckle first, which is Unit 11.

Another view: 2.001 lecture 9 finds truss joint displacements the same way, bar by bar. Roylance §2.1 writes the same solve as a stiffness matrix.

04A bar between two walls

Fix both ends of a bar and apply a load somewhere in the span. Equilibrium says the two wall reactions add to the load. That is one equation and two unknowns. The bar is statically indeterminate. The extra fact is kinematic: the walls do not move, so the shortening of one segment equals the lengthening of the other.

Stiffness splits the load

Call the load \(P\) and put it a distance \(a\) from the left wall. The bar length is \(L\). The left segment is a spring of stiffness \(AE/a\), and the right segment is a spring of stiffness \(AE/(L-a)\). The load splits in proportion to those stiffnesses:

\[ R_L = P\frac{L-a}{L} \] \[ R_R = P\frac{a}{L} \]

The shorter side is the stiffer side, so it carries more of \(P\). At midspan the two lengths match and each wall carries \(P/2\). The area and the modulus cancel, as long as both segments are the same bar.

Worked example · the load near the left wall

The lab's bar is 2 m long, 400 mm², \(E = 200\) GPa, with \(P = 20\) kN. Place the load at \(a = 0.4\) m. The product \(AE\) is 80 MN, so the left stiffness is \(80/0.4\):

\[ k_L = 200\ \mathrm{MN/m} \]

Divided by the 1.6 m right-hand length, the right stiffness is 50 MN/m. The left wall carries \(20\times 200/(200+50)\):

\[ R_L = 16\ \mathrm{kN} \]

and the right wall carries the remaining 4 kN. The force method \(P(L-a)/L = 20\times 1.6/2\) gives the same 16 kN. The two methods are the lab's check.

Worked example · the load at midspan

Move the same 20 kN to \(a = 1\) m. Each segment is 1 m long and each stiffness is 80 MN/m. The reactions are 10 kN and 10 kN. Sliding the load off centre is what makes the shares unequal. Doubling \(P\) doubles both reactions and leaves the shares alone.

Common mistake

Half the load is the answer only at midspan. Using the near length in the numerator assigns the larger share to the longer, softer side. The two reactions have to add to \(P\). If they do not, one of them was copied from the other.

What the model leaves out

The walls are rigid and the bar is uniform. A support that sinks, or a second bar of a different area, changes the stiffnesses and the split. Temperature is not in this section. A temperature change on a bar already held at both ends is Section 6. It produces reactions with no external \(P\) at all.

Another view: 2.001 lecture 7 treats static indeterminacy as one equation short, closed by a displacement condition.

05Three bars and one joint

Give the joint a third bar. A pin joint has two equilibrium equations, and now there are three bar forces. The truss is statically indeterminate, like the bar between two walls. The fix is the same: ask how the joint moves, and make every bar agree with that one move.

The stiffer path takes more

Hang a joint from a ceiling on three bars of the same area and modulus. The middle bar is vertical and \(L\) long. The two side bars lean out at \(\theta\) from the vertical, so each is \(L/\cos\theta\) long. By symmetry the joint only drops, by \(\Delta\). The middle bar stretches by \(\Delta\). A side bar stretches by the part of the drop along it, \(\Delta\cos\theta\), over its longer length. Its strain is \(\cos^{2}\theta\) times the middle bar's, and so is its force:

\[ N_s = N_m\cos^{2}\theta \]

Vertical equilibrium is \(N_m + 2N_s\cos\theta = P\). Put the compatibility in and solve:

\[ N_m = \frac{P}{1 + 2\cos^{3}\theta} \]

At \(\theta = 0\) the bars are parallel and each carries \(P/3\). As the sides lean out they get longer and point away from the load, and the middle bar takes more.

Worked example · three hangers at 30°

The middle bar is 1.2 m, the sides lean 30°, every bar is 300 mm² of steel, and \(P = 60\) kN. Then \(\cos^{3}30^\circ = 0.650\) and

\[ N_m = 26.1\ \mathrm{kN} \]

Each side carries \(0.75\times 26.1 = 19.6\) kN. The check is equilibrium: \(26.1 + 2(19.6)(0.866) = 60.0\) kN. The stresses are 87.0 MPa in the middle and 65.2 MPa at the sides. The joint drops \(N_mL/AE = 0.522\) mm.

Worked example · splay the sides to 60°

Lean the side bars out to 60°. Now \(\cos^{3}60^\circ = 0.125\), so the middle bar carries \(60/1.25 = 48\) kN. Each side carries \(0.25\times 48 = 12\) kN. The middle bar's stress rises to 160 MPa. Wide-splayed side bars barely help hold a vertical load.

Any number of bars

Section 3's projection works for every bar at once. Bar \(i\) pulls with \(N_i = k_i\,\mathbf{e}_i\cdot\mathbf{d}\), where \(k_i = AE/L\). Equilibrium of the joint is then

\[ \Big(\sum_i k_i\,\mathbf{e}_i\mathbf{e}_i^{\mathsf T}\Big)\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{F} \]

That is a 2×2 solve for the joint move, however many bars there are. Two bars or ten, determinate or not, it is the same step. The course's checks solve the bracket and the three hangers this way and reach the same forces.

Common mistake

An even split, \(P/3\), holds only when the bars are parallel. Writing \(1 + 2\cos\theta\) forgets that the side bar is longer and that its force acts at an angle: the three cosines come from the projection, the length and the equilibrium. Saying the side bar stretches \(\Delta/\cos\theta\) projects the wrong way.

What the model leaves out

The ceiling is rigid, the bars are elastic, and they share one \(A\) and \(E\). Different bars change each \(k_i\) and break the symmetry, so the joint moves sideways too. If the middle bar yields first, the side bars pick up the rest. That plastic story is outside this course (D37).

Another view: 2.001 lecture 7 closes indeterminate problems with one displacement condition per extra unknown.

06Thermal stress

A free bar of length \(L\) grows by \(\alpha\Delta T\, L\) when the temperature rises. Fasten the ends so the length cannot change. The anchors have to push that growth back. The mechanical strain is \(-\alpha\Delta T\), and the stress is

\[ \sigma = -E\alpha\Delta T \]

Heating gives compression. Cooling gives tension. The length cancelled: a longer rail grows more, and the stretch that undoes that growth is larger by the same factor. The area cancelled too. A larger rail carries a larger force at the same stress.

The spring check

Treat the rail as a spring \(k = AE/L\). The displacement the anchors impose is \(-\alpha\Delta T\, L\). The force is \(k\) times that displacement, and the stress is the force divided by \(A\):

\[ \sigma = \frac{1}{A}\left(\frac{AE}{L}\right)(-\alpha\Delta T\, L) = -E\alpha\Delta T \]

The two paths agree. Neither one contains \(L\) or \(A\).

Worked example · a held rail at +30 °C

The AISC specification takes \(E = 29{,}000\) ksi for structural steel, and a thermal coefficient of \(6.5\times 10^{-6}\) per °F. That coefficient is \(11.7\times 10^{-6}\) per °C. The 20 m length in the Welded rail lab is assumed. At a rise of 30 °C the free expansion \(\alpha\Delta T L\) is

\[ 7.02\ \mathrm{mm} \]

The stress, with \(E\alpha = 2.34\) MPa/°C, is \(-(2.34)(30)\):

\[ \sigma = -70.2\ \mathrm{MPa} \]

The minus sign is compression. The same rise on a 40 m rail still gives \(-70.2\) MPa. The free expansion doubles, and the stress does not.

Worked example · the same rail in ksi

Work the product in the units AISC publishes. A rise of 50 °F uses \(6.5\times 10^{-6}\) per °F, not the per-°C figure. The product \(-E\alpha\Delta T\) is

\[ \sigma = -9.43\ \mathrm{ksi} \]

Fifty degrees Fahrenheit is \(50/1.8\) degrees Celsius. The lab, which is drawn in °C, reaches the same stress when the slider sits there. A per-°C coefficient used on a Fahrenheit number is too large by 1.8.

Common mistake

The sign follows the temperature. A rise is compression in a held bar, not tension. The coefficient has to match the degree: \(6.5\times 10^{-6}\) belongs with °F, and \(11.7\times 10^{-6}\) belongs with °C. Multiplying by the length puts a length into an answer that does not have one.

What the model leaves out

\(\alpha\) and \(E\) are taken as constant. A large swing in temperature moves both. The rail is not allowed to buckle, and the anchors are rigid. A real continuously welded rail is laid at a neutral temperature, can lift off the ties, and sheds some force into the ballast. The lab states that limit in its own words.

Another view: 2.001 lecture 15 adds temperature to the stress-strain relation. The per-°C form of the stress is the one-dimensional case.

07A joint that must close

A gap changes the story. The rail expands freely until the free expansion equals the gap. Up to that temperature the stress is zero. Past it, only the extra expansion is pushed back. The closing temperature is

\[ \Delta T_{\mathrm{close}} = \frac{\mathrm{gap}}{\alpha L} \]

and the stress after that is \(-E\) times the extra shortening over \(L\). A gap is one-sided. Cooling opens it further and does not pull on the joint.

Worked example · the gap still open

The lab's joint is a 20 m rail with a 6 mm gap. At a rise of 20 °C the free expansion \(\alpha\Delta T L\) is

\[ 4.68\ \mathrm{mm} \]

4.68 mm is less than 6 mm, so the ends have not met. The stress is 0. Using \(-E\alpha\Delta T\) here invents a compression the joint cannot carry yet.

Worked example · past the closing temperature

The same gap closes at \(\mathrm{gap}/(\alpha L)\):

\[ \Delta T_{\mathrm{close}} = 25.6^\circ\mathrm{C} \]

At a rise of 40 °C the free expansion is 9.36 mm. The extra 3.36 mm is what the anchors shorten. Then \(-E\) times that shortening over the length is

\[ \sigma = -33.6\ \mathrm{MPa} \]

The no-gap stress at 40 °C would be \(-93.6\) MPa. The gap has removed the part of the expansion that was free. The dashed line on the lab chart is the closing temperature.

Common mistake

Ignoring the gap reports \(-E\alpha\Delta T\) while the joint is still open, which is zero, or past closure, where only the extra expansion counts. A per-°F coefficient on a Celsius rise is a second, separate miss. The free expansion in millimetres is a length, not a stress in MPa.

What the model leaves out

The gap is a clean opening that closes all at once. A real joint has play, friction and a fastener that is not rigid. The section and the 6 mm gap in the lab are assumed. Buckling under the compression, and the neutral temperature the rail was laid at, are outside this one-dimensional bar.

08Stress at a hole

\(N/A\) is an average. Near a hole the lines of force bend around the opening and crowd its edge. The stress there is a multiple of the average. The multiple is the stress-concentration factor:

\[ K_t = \frac{\sigma_{\max}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{nom}}} \]

\(K_t\) is defined on a stated nominal stress. Change which area the nominal uses and \(K_t\) changes with it.

The circular hole

Pull a wide plate with a stress \(S\) along \(x\). Drill a hole of radius \(a\). The elastic solution, due to Kirsch and worked in Roylance §5.1, gives the hoop stress on the hole's edge as

\[ \sigma_\theta = S\,(1 - 2\cos 2\theta) \]

with \(\theta\) measured from the load axis. Across the load, at \(\theta = 90^\circ\), that is \(3S\). On the load axis it is \(-S\), a compression. Along the line across the load the stress is

\[ \sigma = S\left(1 + \frac{a^{2}}{2r^{2}} + \frac{3a^{4}}{2r^{4}}\right) \]

It is \(3S\) at the edge, \(1.22S\) one radius out and \(1.07S\) two radii out. The peak is local. The extra stress under that bump adds up to \(S\,a\) per unit thickness. That is exactly the force the hole removed from the section. Equilibrium still holds.

Worked example · a pinhole and a large hole

A wide steel plate carries 40 MPa. A 2 mm hole and a 20 mm hole both see 120 MPa at the edge. The 3 contains no size. The size sets how far the bump reaches: 1.22 times the far stress sits 2 mm from the small hole's centre and 20 mm from the large one's.

Sharpness, not size

Inglis solved the elliptical hole. With half-axis \(a\) across the load and \(b\) along it, the peak is

\[ K_t = 1 + \frac{2a}{b} \]

A circle has \(a = b\) and gives 3 again. The tip radius of the ellipse is \(\rho = b^{2}/a\), so the same result reads \(1 + 2\sqrt{a/\rho}\). A crack has a tip radius near zero and a very large \(K_t\). Drilling a small hole at a crack tip raises \(\rho\). That is why a stop hole slows a crack in a windshield or a skin panel.

Worked example · one slot, two ways round

An elliptical slot 20 mm long and 4 mm wide sits in a wide plate at 40 MPa. Across the load, \(a = 10\) mm and \(b = 2\) mm, so \(K_t = 11\) and the edge sees

\[ \sigma_{\max} = 440\ \mathrm{MPa} \]

That is past the 250 MPa yield of A36 steel. Turn the slot to run along the load. Now \(a = 2\) mm and \(b = 10\) mm, so \(K_t = 1.4\) and the edge sees 56 MPa. The same cut differs by a factor of 7.9 with its orientation.

A hole in a strip

A real bar is not infinitely wide. A hole of diameter \(d\) in a strip of width \(w\) and thickness \(t\) leaves the net area \((w-d)t\). Measured \(K_t\) curves, on the net stress \(P/((w-d)t)\), follow Heywood's fit closely:

\[ K_t = 2 + \left(1 - \frac{d}{w}\right)^{3} \]

As \(d/w\) goes to zero it returns to 3. A wider hole lowers \(K_t\) on the net stress. The net stress itself rises faster, so the peak still grows.

Worked example · a bolt hole in a flat bar

A steel bar 60 mm wide and 10 mm thick has a 12 mm hole and carries 30 kN. The net area is 480 mm², so the net stress is 62.5 MPa. With \(d/w = 0.2\), \(K_t = 2 + 0.8^{3} = 2.51\), and

\[ \sigma_{\max} = 157\ \mathrm{MPa} \]

The second view is the wide-plate estimate: the gross stress is 50 MPa, and three times that is 150 MPa. The finite width adds about 5%.

Common mistake

\(K_t\) multiplies the nominal stress it was measured on. Heywood's factor on the gross stress gives 126 MPa here, which is low. Three is the wide-plate circle only. A slot across the load is far worse, and the same slot along the load is milder. In a wide plate a bigger round hole does not raise the peak.

What the model leaves out

The plate is elastic and thin. Under one steady load a ductile metal yields in a small zone at the edge and spreads the load, so the peak matters most for fatigue and for brittle materials. Shoulder fillets and grooves have their own \(K_t\) charts, which this course does not reproduce. Plastic redistribution is outside the course (D37).

09Formula sheet

Elongation of one piece, and the sum along the bar:

\[ \delta = \frac{NL}{AE} \] \[ \delta = \int_0^L \frac{N}{AE}\,dx \]

A linear taper of a circular bar:

\[ \delta = \frac{4PL}{\pi E d_0 d_1} \]

A bar fixed at both ends, load \(P\) a distance \(a\) from the left:

\[ R_L = P\frac{L-a}{L} \] \[ R_R = P - R_L \]

A truss joint: each bar's change of length is the joint move projected on the bar. Three symmetric hangers:

\[ \delta_i = u\cos\theta_i + v\sin\theta_i \] \[ N_m = \frac{P}{1+2\cos^{3}\theta} \]

A fastened bar with a temperature change, and the temperature that closes a gap:

\[ \sigma = -E\alpha\Delta T \] \[ \Delta T_{\mathrm{close}} = \frac{\mathrm{gap}}{\alpha L} \]

Stress concentration: a circular hole in a wide plate, an elliptical hole, and a hole in a strip on the net stress:

\[ K_t = 3 \] \[ K_t = 1 + \frac{2a}{b} \] \[ K_t = 2 + \left(1 - \frac{d}{w}\right)^{3} \]

Citations: Roylance §1.1 for the elastic elongation, Roylance §2.1 for truss joints, and Roylance §5.1 for the hole. Indeterminate bars and thermal stress are cited by those topic names. The modulus and the thermal coefficient are the AISC values for structural steel. The lengths, areas and the joint gap drawn in the labs are assumed.
