01Integrating the curvature

Unit 6 found that a moment bends a beam to a curvature \(1/\rho = M/EI\). Deflection is that curvature, added up along the beam. Take \(y\) upward and the sagging moment as positive, the same signs as the shear and moment labs.

From curvature to the elastic curve

A curve \(y(x)\) has curvature \(y''/(1 + y'^{2})^{3/2}\). Beam slopes are small, a few hundredths of a radian, so the denominator is 1 to within a tenth of a percent. A sagging span is a local minimum of \(y\), so \(y''\) is positive where the moment is positive. The signs agree:

\[ EI\,y'' = M \]

Integrate once for the slope and again for the deflection. Each integration brings a constant. The supports fix them: a wall gives \(y = 0\) and \(y' = 0\), and a pin or roller gives \(y = 0\). Roylance §4.3 is the reading.

The cantilever, step by step

A tip load \(P\) down on a cantilever fixed at \(x = 0\) gives \(M = -P(L - x)\). Integrate:

\[ EI\,y' = -P\left(Lx - \frac{x^{2}}{2}\right) + C_1 \] \[ EI\,y = -P\left(\frac{Lx^{2}}{2} - \frac{x^{3}}{6}\right) + C_1x + C_2 \]

The wall gives \(y'(0) = 0\) and \(y(0) = 0\), so both constants are zero. At the tip, \(x = L\):

\[ \delta = \frac{PL^{3}}{3EI} \]

and the tip slope is \(PL^{2}/2EI\).

Worked example · the tip load

The lab's rectangle is 40 mm by 80 mm, E is 200 GPa, the span is 2 m, and the tip load is 2 kN. The tip drops, and the tip slope is

\[ \delta = 15.62\ \mathrm{mm} \] \[ \theta = 0.01172\ \mathrm{rad} \]

Up is positive on the chart, so the gold curve hangs below the axis. The gold dot is the tip. The slope there is not zero. The teal line is the wall, where both the slope and the deflection are zero. A free end can be the lowest point. An interior peak cannot: there the slope has to cross zero.

Common mistake

\(PL^{3}/48EI\) is a simple span with a midspan load. \(wL^{4}/8EI\) is this cantilever with a uniform load, not a point load. Doubling the depth divides the drop by 8, because \(I\) grows with \(h^{3}\). Doubling the width only halves it. Bending stress fell to a quarter when the depth doubled. Deflection falls further. Dropping a constant of integration without a support to justify it bends the wrong curve.

Another view: 2.001 lecture 22 integrates the moment to the elastic curve.

02Boundary conditions on a simple span

A uniform downward load \(w\) on a simple span has \(M = (wL/2)x - wx^{2}/2\). Integrate twice:

\[ EI\,y = \frac{wLx^{3}}{12} - \frac{wx^{4}}{24} + C_1x + C_2 \]

A pin at \(x = 0\) gives \(C_2 = 0\). A roller at \(x = L\) gives \(y(L) = 0\), so \(C_1 = -wL^{3}/24\). By symmetry the slope is zero at midspan, which is the lowest point. Put in \(x = L/2\):

\[ \delta = \frac{5wL^{4}}{384EI} \]
Worked example · the uniform load

The same 40 mm by 80 mm section, over 4 m, carries 5 kN/m. Midspan drops

\[ \delta = 48.8\ \mathrm{mm} \]

The teal line is midspan. Double the load and the drop doubles. Double the span and it grows with L⁴, sixteen times, which is not the eight times of a tip load. The check is a trapezoid integral of M/EI with the end deflections put back to zero.

Common mistake

Leaving out the 5 makes the drop five times too small, and the load that reaches a deflection limit five times too large. Using the cantilever formula \(wL^{4}/8EI\) on a simple span overshoots by 384/40. A simple span has no condition on its end slopes. Setting \(y'(0) = 0\) treats the pin as a wall.

03Where the beam is lowest

A point load at distance \(a\) from the left, with \(a < L/2\), peaks the moment at the load. The longer side still has room for the slope to reach zero. Set the slope of the longer side's curve to zero and solve. That station is \[ x = L - \sqrt{(L^2 - a^2)/3} \]

measured from the left. The drop under the load is smaller than the drop at that station.

Worked example · quarter span

A 10 kN load stands at 1 m on a 4 m span, on the same section. The beam is lowest at 1.764 m, and that point drops

\[ \delta = 27.3\ \mathrm{mm} \]

The teal line is 1.764 m. The pink line is the load, at 1 m. They are not the same line.

Worked example · under the load

The beam under the load itself drops 22 mm. That is \(Pa^{2}b^{2}/3EIL\), with \(b\) the longer piece. It is a real deflection. It is not the largest one. Slide the load to midspan and the two stations meet, and the drop becomes \(PL^{3}/48EI\), which is 39.1 mm for this load.

Why the peaks separate

\(dM/dx = V\), so the moment peaks where the shear changes sign. \(dy/dx = 0\) is a different sentence. It concerns the slope of the curve, not the shear. Only a symmetric load puts both zeros at midspan.

04One equation for the whole beam

With two loads on a span, the moment has three pieces, and each piece needs its own two constants. Discontinuity functions fold the pieces into one expression, so there are only two constants for the whole beam.

Brackets that switch on

Write \(\langle x - a\rangle^{n}\) for a term that is zero before \(x = a\) and \((x - a)^{n}\) after it. A load \(P\) down at \(a\) adds \(-P\langle x - a\rangle\) to the moment, because it only acts on cuts to its right. A uniform load \(w\) from \(a\) onward adds \(-\tfrac{w}{2}\langle x - a\rangle^{2}\). A counterclockwise couple \(M_0\) at \(a\) adds \(-M_0\langle x - a\rangle^{0}\), a step. A bracket integrates like an ordinary power:

\[ \int \langle x - a\rangle^{n}\,dx = \frac{\langle x - a\rangle^{n+1}}{n+1} \]

So one moment expression, integrated twice, gives one curve for the whole span, with two constants fixed by the two supports. Roylance §4.3 uses the same singularity functions.

Worked example · two loads on one span

The 40 by 80 mm steel section, \(EI = 341{,}333\) N·m², spans 4 m. It carries 6 kN at 1 m and 4 kN at 3 m. Moments about the left pin give the right reaction \((6 + 12)/4 = 4.5\) kN, so the left is 5.5 kN. In newtons and metres:

\[ \begin{aligned}EI\,y = {} & \frac{5500x^{3}}{6} - \frac{6000\langle x-1\rangle^{3}}{6} \\ & - \frac{4000\langle x-3\rangle^{3}}{6} + C_1x\end{aligned} \]

\(y(0) = 0\) removed \(C_2\). \(y(4) = 0\) gives \(C_1 = -7750\) N·m². At midspan only the first bracket is on:

\[ \delta = 26.9\ \mathrm{mm} \]

Section 5's method checks it: the 6 kN load alone drops midspan 16.1 mm, the 4 kN load alone 10.7 mm, and they add to 26.9 mm.

Common mistake

A bracket is zero before its load, so a term must not be kept to the left of where it starts. A uniform load that stops before the end needs a second bracket, \(+\tfrac{w}{2}\langle x - b\rangle^{2}\), to switch it off. Writing it without that term loads the rest of the span. The reactions belong in the expression too: the left one as \(R_Ax\), from the start.

05Superposition from a table

\(EI\,y'' = M\) is linear, and so is \(M\) in the loads. The deflection of several loads is the sum of the deflections each load makes alone, on the same supports. A table of single cases then answers most beams by addition.

The five cases worth knowing

A cantilever with a tip load drops \(PL^{3}/3EI\); with a uniform load, \(wL^{4}/8EI\). A simple span with a midspan load drops \(PL^{3}/48EI\); with a uniform load, \(5wL^{4}/384EI\). A simple span with \(P\) at \(a\) drops \(Pa^{2}b^{2}/3EIL\) under the load. Each comes from Sections 1 to 4. The end slopes in the same table are what Section 6 needs.

Worked example · a uniform load and a midspan load

The 4 m span carries 5 kN/m and also 10 kN at midspan. The uniform load alone drops midspan 48.8 mm. The point load alone drops it \(PL^{3}/48EI = 39.1\) mm. Both act at the same station, so

\[ \delta = 87.9\ \mathrm{mm} \]

The stations must match. The 10 kN load at 1 m, from Section 3, has its own largest drop at 1.76 m, so adding it to the uniform load's midspan drop would mix two stations.

Common mistake

Superposition adds deflections at one station on one set of supports. Adding each case's largest deflection, wherever it sits, overstates the total. It also needs the material to stay elastic and the slopes to stay small, or the sum is no longer the answer.

Worked example · both axles on a 20 ft bridge

On a 20 ft span the Civic's 9 ft wheelbase fits with room to spare. Put the front axle 13.1 ft from the left support, so the rear axle is at 4.1 ft. Each wheel line adds its own sag at midspan, from the table's case for a load a distance b from the nearer support:

\[ \Delta_{mid} = \frac{Pb(3L^{2} - 4b^{2})}{48EI} \]

The front wheel, 877 lb with \(b = 6.9\) ft, gives 0.246 in. The rear wheel, 561 lb with \(b = 4.1\) ft, gives 0.106 in. Together they sag the stringer 0.352 in. Back the car up until the front axle is at midspan and the total is only 0.311 in, because the rear axle then sits almost on the support.

06A redundant prop

Fix the left end and hold the right end up, and a uniform load has one support too many for equilibrium. Pick the prop's force \(R\) as the redundant and remove the prop. The cantilever that is left drops \(wL^{4}/8EI\) at the tip under the load, from Section 5's table. The force \(R\) alone raises the tip by \(RL^{3}/3EI\). The real tip does not move, so the two add to zero:

\[ R = \frac{3wL}{8} \]

The wall moment that remains is \(wL^{2}/2 - RL = wL^{2}/8\), hogging, a quarter of the free value. The lowest point is not at midspan. Its distance back from the prop is \(L(1 + \sqrt{33})/16\).

Worked example · the prop

The lab's beam is again 40 mm by 80 mm, E is 200 GPa, the span is 3 m, and the load is 4 kN/m. The prop, the wall moment, and the largest drop are

\[ R = 4.5\ \mathrm{kN} \] \[ M = -4.5\ \mathrm{kN \cdot m} \] \[ \delta = 5.14\ \mathrm{mm} \]

The lowest point is at 1.735 m, not at 1.5 m. The pink line is the prop, which does not move. The check is a finite-difference solution of EI y'' = M with R kept as an unknown.

Worked example · twice the span

The same load on a 6 m span doubles the prop, to 9 kN, because R grows with L. The wall moment grows with L², from −4.5 kN·m to −18 kN·m. The drop grows with L⁴. It is not another doubling.

Worked example · the prop removed

Take the prop away on the 3 m beam. The tip drops 118.7 mm and the wall moment is −18 kN·m, which is wL²/2. The prop had been holding that whole tip drop, and it had been carrying three quarters of that wall moment away.

Common mistake

\(wL/2\) is a simple-span reaction, not this prop. \(wL\) is the whole load, which the wall carries only when the tip is free. \(wL^{2}/12\) is the end moment of a beam fixed at both ends, Section 7. This far end is pinned, so its moment is zero and the wall keeps \(wL^{2}/8\).

07Fixed ends and symmetry

Fix both ends of a beam and load it symmetrically. There are four unknown reactions and two equilibrium equations. Symmetry gives one fact for free: the two vertical reactions are equal, and so are the two end moments. Superposition gives the other.

Release the end moments

Free both ends to turn. A simple span under a uniform load turns each end by \(wL^{3}/24EI\). Now apply equal hogging end moments \(M_e\). On a simple span they turn each end back by \(M_eL/2EI\). The real ends do not turn, so the two cancel:

\[ M_e = \frac{wL^{2}}{12} \]

The midspan moment is the simple span's \(wL^{2}/8\) less \(M_e\), which is \(wL^{2}/24\). The midspan drop is the simple span's \(5wL^{4}/384EI\) less the end moments' lift \(M_eL^{2}/8EI\):

\[ \delta = \frac{wL^{4}}{384EI} \]

For a midspan point load the same steps give \(M_e = PL/8\) and \(\delta = PL^{3}/192EI\).

Worked example · the 4 m span with both ends fixed

Fix both ends of the 4 m span under 5 kN/m. The end moments are \(5\times 16/12\):

\[ M_e = -6.67\ \mathrm{kN\cdot m} \]

hogging. Midspan carries +3.33 kN·m, a third of the simple span's 10. The drop is

\[ \delta = 9.77\ \mathrm{mm} \]

a fifth of the simple span's 48.8 mm. The largest moment has moved from midspan to the walls, and it is two-thirds of the simple-span peak.

Common mistake

\(wL^{2}/12\) is the fixed-end moment, not the midspan moment, and it is hogging. A propped cantilever's wall keeps \(wL^{2}/8\), because its far end is free to turn. Symmetry needs symmetric loads and supports: one off-centre load breaks it, and the two end moments differ.

What the model leaves out

A real fixed end is never perfectly rigid. A beam bolted to a column turns a little, and its end moment falls between zero and \(wL^{2}/12\). Design tables treat that as a semi-rigid connection.

Another view: 2.001 lecture 23 uses symmetry and superposition on statically indeterminate beams.

Worked example · a car on a fixed-end bridge

A 2024 Civic LX weighs 2,877 lb, 61% on the front axle. Two W8×10 stringers span 12 ft, one under each wheel line, so each takes 877 lb from the front wheels. With the front axle at midspan and the rear still off the bridge, a pinned stringer sags

\[ \begin{aligned}\Delta &= \frac{PL^{3}}{48EI} = \frac{877(144)^{3}}{48(29\times10^{6})(30.8)} \\ &= 0.0611\ \mathrm{in}\end{aligned} \]

Weld the ends so they cannot turn. Each end now takes \(PL/8\), and the sag is \(PL^{3}/192EI\), a quarter: 0.0153 in. The moment under the wheel halves, from 2,632 to 1,316 lbf·ft.

08A pine shelf

A dressed 1×12 lies flat: 0.75 in thick and 11.25 in wide, the PS 20 dry size, not the nominal 1 by 12. Eastern white pine, clear wood at 12 percent moisture, has a bending modulus of 1.24 million psi in the Forest Products Laboratory tables. That published modulus already includes shear deflection. A row of books is taken as 40 lb/ft. The span below is a chosen 72 in, not a rated shelf length.

Worked example · the 72 in span

With a support at each end and no bracket in the middle, midspan drops

\[ \delta = 2.38\ \mathrm{in} \]

Each end carries half the books, 120 lbf. The inch-pound formula and the SI formula agree. The board is deep the wrong way: the 0.75 in thickness is the h in bh³/12.

Worked example · a 48 in span

Shorten the span from 72 in to 48 in and keep the books. The sag multiplies by (48/72)⁴:

\[ \delta = 0.47\ \mathrm{in} \]

The load did not change. The span did, and deflection listens to the fourth power.

Worked example · the center bracket

A bracket at midspan of the 72 in shelf holds the center. Each half is a propped cantilever. The ends carry 45 lbf each, the center carries 150 lbf, and the largest sag falls to

\[ \delta = 0.0618\ \mathrm{in} \]

The open-span sag was 2.38 in. The pink line is the bracket. Turn the same board on edge and I grows by 225, because 11.25/0.75 is 15 and I follows the square of that ratio. The open-span sag would fall to 0.01057 in. The end reactions would not change.

What the course model leaves out

2.38 in is this EI, not a published shelf rating. The 40 lb/ft is an assumed row of books. Grade, knots, creep, and a bracket that itself flexes are outside the model. The handbook modulus already contains shear, so this bending formula does not add a second shear deflection on top. The nominal 1 by 12 is the wrong rectangle.

09Formula sheet

Curvature, with \(y\) upward and sagging moment positive, and the bracket rule:

\[ EI\,y'' = M \] \[ \int \langle x - a\rangle^{n}\,dx = \frac{\langle x - a\rangle^{n+1}}{n+1} \]

Cantilever, tip load and uniform load:

\[ \delta = \frac{PL^{3}}{3EI} \] \[ \delta = \frac{wL^{4}}{8EI} \]

Simple span, midspan load and uniform load:

\[ \delta = \frac{PL^{3}}{48EI} \] \[ \delta = \frac{5wL^{4}}{384EI} \]

Propped cantilever, uniform load, and a beam fixed at both ends:

\[ R = \frac{3wL}{8} \] \[ M_e = \frac{wL^{2}}{12} \] \[ \delta = \frac{wL^{4}}{384EI} \]

Citations: Roylance §4.3. The shelf modulus is the Forest Products Laboratory value for eastern white pine, and the dressed size is PS 20. The 40 mm by 80 mm section and the book load are assumed.
