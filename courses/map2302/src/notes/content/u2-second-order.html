Second-order linear equations

Two initial data select a motion. Characteristic roots describe free modes; forcing adds a particular response. A car suspension and an electrical circuit share the operator while keeping different physical meanings.

Part I

Free modes

1Modes and initial data

Start with \(mx''+cx'+kx=F(t)\), where \(m\ne0\). Position and velocity are separate initial data. Rewriting as \(x'=v\), \(v'=[F(t)-cv-kx]/m\) explains why one scalar initial condition leaves a family of solutions.

First set forcing to zero. Try \(x=e^{rt}\). Both derivatives preserve the exponential, so substitution leaves \((mr^2+cr+k)e^{rt}=0\). The exponential never vanishes. Its coefficient, the characteristic polynomial, must therefore vanish.

Derivation · Independent modes

Distinct roots give \(e^{r_1t}\) and \(e^{r_2t}\). Their Wronskian is \((r_2-r_1)e^{(r_1+r_2)t}\), which is nonzero. Thus their coefficients can satisfy two independent initial data. At a repeated root, the two exponentials coincide. The difference quotient \([e^{(r+\epsilon)t}-e^{rt}]/\epsilon\) tends to \(te^{rt}\). This supplies the missing independent mode.

Complex conjugate roots \(a\pm ib\) become real functions \(e^{at}\cos bt\) and \(e^{at}\sin bt\). The real part controls growth or decay; the imaginary part controls oscillation. Positive real parts give growth, even when the equation is linear. A passive mass–spring–damper has positive mass/stiffness and nonnegative damping, but an abstract linear equation need not satisfy those physical restrictions.

Worked example · Two data select two weights

For \(x''+4x=0\), take \(x(0)=1\), \(v(0)=2\). Write \(x=A\cos2t+B\sin2t\). Then \(A=1\), \(2B=2\), so \(x=\cos2t+\sin2t\). At \(t=1\), \(x\approx0.493151\) m and \(v\approx-2.65089\) m/s. Specifying only \(x(0)=1\) would not fix \(B\).

The same exponential idea works for higher constant-coefficient orders. Each characteristic root of multiplicity \(s\) contributes \(e^{rt},te^{rt},\ldots,t^{s-1}e^{rt}\). A real equation combines conjugate roots into real sine-cosine pairs. Its order counts independent initial data.

Common mistake

A repeated root does not leave only one arbitrary constant. Repeating the same exponential twice gives dependent functions, so use the t-multiplied mode.

Read: Lebl §§2.1–2.3. Another view: OCW modes and the characteristic equation.

2Damping and energy

For positive \(m,k\), define \(\omega_n=\sqrt{k/m}\), \(\alpha=c/(2m)\) and \(\zeta=\alpha/\omega_n\). Completing the square gives \((r+\alpha)^2=\alpha^2-\omega_n^2\). The discriminant separates three free-response structures.

If \(0\le\zeta<1\), the damped frequency is \(\omega_d=\sqrt{\omega_n^2-\alpha^2}\), and the response is a decaying sinusoid. If \(\zeta=1\), it is \((A+Bt)e^{-\alpha t}\). If \(\zeta>1\), it contains two real decay rates. These are statements about the basis, not the signs of every possible trajectory.

Worked example · Extra damping can leave more displacement

Release \(m=1\) kg, \(k=4\) N/m from \(x_0=1\) m at rest. With critical \(c=4\) N s/m, \(x=(1+2t)e^{-2t}\), giving \(x(1)\approx0.406006\) m. With \(c=8\), the two real modes give \(x(1)\approx0.630360\) m. Under these specified initial conditions, excessive damping has slowed the return.

“Critical damping returns fastest” needs a criterion and initial conditions. A critically damped response may cross equilibrium if its initial velocity points inward strongly enough. For the preceding mass and spring with \(v_0=-4\) m/s, the response is \((1-2t)e^{-2t}\), which crosses at \(t=0.5\) s.

Derivation · Where energy goes

For a fixed base, mechanical energy is \(E=mv^2/2+kx^2/2\). Differentiate and use \(mv'=-cv-kx\): \(E'=v(mv'+kx)=-cv^2\). A passive damper removes energy, even while spring and kinetic energy trade. Integrating gives \(E(t)+\int_0^t cv^2du=E(0)\).

Worked example · Energy need not fall at a constant rate

Use \(m=1\), \(c=1\), \(k=4\), \(x_0=1\), \(v_0=0\). Initial energy is 2 J. At \(t=1\), the model gives \(x\approx-0.0706446\), \(v\approx-1.17000\), and \(E\approx0.694432\) J. The instantaneous loss depends on \(v^2\); it is zero at a turning point, not constant throughout a cycle.

For underdamped peaks one full damped period apart, the sinusoid has the same phase. Their ratio is \(e^{-\alpha\Delta t}\), so \(\alpha=-\ln(x_{n+1}/x_n)/\Delta t\) and \(c=2m\alpha\). Use peaks with matching signs; adjacent positive and negative extrema are only half a cycle apart.

Common mistake

Natural frequency, damped free frequency and the forced displacement-gain peak are different quantities. Damping changes the latter two in different ways.

What the model leaves out

The linear spring and viscous damper assume force proportional to displacement and velocity. Dry friction, amplitude-dependent damping, slack and end stops are absent. The coordinate is measured from static equilibrium, so gravity's constant preload has already been removed.

Read: Lebl §2.4. Another view: OCW damped harmonic oscillators.

Part II

Input and response

3Exponential response

Let \(D\) denote differentiation and \(p(D)=mD^2+cD+k\). Since \(D e^{rt}=re^{rt}\), applying the operator gives \(p(D)e^{rt}=p(r)e^{rt}\). For forcing \(Fe^{rt}\), a particular solution is \(Fe^{rt}/p(r)\), provided \(p(r)\ne0\).

If the forcing rate is a root, that division fails. It does not imply zero response. The product rule gives \(p(D)[te^{rt}]=[tp(r)+p'(r)]e^{rt}\). At a simple root use \(Ft e^{rt}/p'(r)\). At a double root both \(p(r)\) and \(p'(r)\) vanish; differentiating the product relation again gives \(Ft^2e^{rt}/p''(r)\). Here \(p''=2m\).

Worked example · Resonance need not be oscillatory

Consider \(x''+2x'+x=e^{-t}\), with zero initial displacement and velocity. The rate −1 is a double root. The particular solution \(x=t^2e^{-t}/2\) already satisfies both zero data. At \(t=2\), \(x=2e^{-2}\approx0.270671\). The t² factor comes from root multiplicity, although the response does not oscillate.

For a nonresonant trial, a particular solution rarely matches arbitrary initial data. Add the full homogeneous family and solve for its coefficients. “Particular” means one function satisfying the forced equation, not the complete solution of an IVP.

Common mistake

Check whether p(r) is zero before using the exponential-response formula. A simple root needs one factor of t; a repeated root needs two. Increasing the denominator by guesswork does not repair a resonant trial.

Read: Lebl §2.5. Another view: OCW exponential response.

4Gain and phase lag

For forcing \(F\cos\omega t\), solve first with \(Fe^{i\omega t}\). The complex amplitude is \(X=F/[k-m\omega^2+ic\omega]\). The stiffness and inertial terms have opposite signs because two differentiations of a sinusoid contribute \(-\omega^2\).

\[A=\frac{F}{\sqrt{(k-m\omega^2)^2+c^2\omega^2}},\qquad \phi=\operatorname{atan2}(c\omega,k-m\omega^2).\]

The real particular response is \(A\cos(\omega t-\phi)\). With positive damping and frequency, the lag lies between zero and π. At \(\omega_n\), the real denominator vanishes, so lag is π/2. Above it, the real part is negative; ordinary arctangent without quadrant information gives the wrong lag.

Worked example · A periodic response plus a transient

Choose \(m=1\), \(c=1\), \(k=4\), \(F=2\), \(\omega=1\), with zero initial data. The steady amplitude is \(2/\sqrt{10}\approx0.632456\) m, and lag is \(\arctan(1/3)\approx0.321751\) rad. At \(t=2\), the steady term is about −0.0678286 m, but the total response is about 0.159889 m. The difference is the free transient required by the initial data.

Gain depends on the declared input. Under force excitation it is displacement per unit force. If the right side is \(kA\cos\omega t\) and the input is a displacement A, the displacement gain has an additional numerator k. Moving a spring end and applying a force are different input conventions.

Common mistake

A negative cosine coefficient is a phase reversal, not a negative amplitude. Also, do not silently drop a numerator such as k when the defined input is a spring-end displacement.

Read: Lebl §2.6. Another view: OCW gain and phase lag; Practice Problems 12.

5Undetermined coefficients

The method works when differentiation keeps the forcing inside a finite-dimensional family: polynomials, exponentials, sinusoids, or products of them. Guess the entire family and solve its coefficient equations. For a quadratic forcing, include a constant and linear term as well as the quadratic term, because derivatives feed lower powers.

For \(mx''+cx'+kx=F_2t^2+F_1t+F_0\), try \(x_p=At^2+Bt+C\). Matching descending powers gives \(kA=F_2\), \(2cA+kB=F_1\), \(2mA+cB+kC=F_0\), assuming \(k\ne0\). The equations are triangular.

Worked example · The second derivative reaches the constant equation

For \(x''+4x=2t^2+t+3\), the polynomial coefficients are \(A=1/2\), \(B=1/4\), \(C=1/2\). Thus \(x_p(1)=1.25\) m. It has initial data \(x_p(0)=1/2\), \(v_p(0)=1/4\). The lab shows this particular response; other initial data require a homogeneous correction.

For cosine forcing with damping, a cosine-only trial is generally insufficient: its first derivative generates a sine term. Use both cosine and sine coefficients. At resonance, multiply the whole trial family by the needed power of t, rather than multiplying only one term.

Common mistake

Matching only the highest polynomial power ignores derivative contributions to lower powers. Likewise, omitting the sine coefficient in a damped sinusoidal trial leaves an uncancelled term.

The method is not a universal integration rule. A forcing outside these derivative-closed families may require another representation. Unit 3 develops Fourier and Laplace methods for broader inputs. Read: Lebl §2.5; OCW undetermined coefficients.

6Linear operators and time invariance

Definition · Linear time-invariant system

A linear operator distributes over sums and constant multiples. A constant-coefficient differential operator is also time invariant: shifting a function in time shifts its derivatives without changing coefficients.

If \(Lx_1=F_1\) and \(Lx_2=F_2\), then \(L(ax_1+bx_2)=aF_1+bF_2\). Thus a sum of inputs gives a sum of responses when the initial conditions are combined consistently. For a single fixed forcing, the general solution is one particular response plus the homogeneous family.

Worked example · Scale the entire zero-state response

Use \(m=1\), \(c=1\), \(k=4\), \(\omega=1\). With zero initial data, doubling force amplitude from 1 to 2 doubles the response at every time. At \(t=2\), the doubled-input response is about 0.159889 m. Both its forced term and its initial-condition correction scale. Keeping the same nonzero initial state instead would leave an additional unscaled free motion.

Time invariance also requires care with the starting state. A delayed input applied to a system at rest gives a delayed zero-state response. It does not delay free motion that was already present. This distinction becomes explicit in convolution and transfer functions in Unit 3.

Common mistake

Adding two solutions of the same nonzero forced equation doubles the forcing. The sum is not generally another solution of the original equation with its original initial data.

Another view: OCW linear operators and time invariance.

7Pure and practical resonance

Without damping, forcing at \(\omega=\omega_n\) hits a characteristic root. The resonant particular solution for cosine force is \(Ft\sin(\omega_nt)/(2m\omega_n)\). Its envelope grows linearly. There is no finite fixed-amplitude steady sinusoid to report.

Worked example · A growing envelope can have a small sampled value

Set \(m=1\), \(c=0\), \(k=4\), \(F=1\), zero initial data. Then \(x=t\sin2t/4\). At \(t=3\), \(x\approx-0.209562\) m, while its envelope has magnitude 0.75 m. A small displacement at a selected phase does not mean resonance has stopped building energy.

With positive damping, minimize the squared denominator \(D=(k-m\omega^2)^2+c^2\omega^2\). Differentiating with respect to \(u=\omega^2\) gives \(-2m(k-mu)+c^2=0\). The interior displacement peak is therefore

\[\omega_r=\sqrt{k/m-c^2/(2m^2)}.\]

This peak exists only when its squared value is positive. Otherwise maximum force-to-displacement gain occurs at zero frequency. The free damped frequency contains \(c^2/(4m^2)\), not the same correction. Phase π/2 still occurs at \(\omega_n\), not generally at the displacement peak.

Near resonance without damping, zero initial data gives \(x=F[\cos\omega t-\cos\omega_nt]/(k-m\omega^2)\). A cosine-difference identity exposes a fast oscillation multiplied by a slow beat envelope. At exact resonance take the limit; do not divide by zero.

What the model leaves out

The indefinitely growing linear response assumes unlimited drive, a linear spring and no damping. Real amplitude limits, losses and nonlinear forces eventually invalidate it. A large but finite damped peak is a different prediction.

Read: Lebl §2.6. Another view: OCW pure resonance and practical frequency response.

Part III

Devices

8A car suspension

The application draws one car corner: body, coil spring, shock absorber, wheel and road. The wheel follows a prescribed road displacement \(r(t)\). The body position is \(x(t)\), measured from static equilibrium. Spring force depends on \(x-r\); shock force depends on \(x'-r'\).

\[mx''=-k(x-r)-c(x'-r'),\qquad mx''+cx'+kx=kr+cr'.\]

The right side is not just kr. A fast road change acts through relative velocity too. For a sinusoidal road, complex displacement transmissibility is \(H=(k+ic\omega)/(k-m\omega^2+ic\omega)\). It can exceed one near the body mode. A stiffer spring or a larger damping coefficient does not improve isolation at every frequency.

Eibach's 0600.250.0300 table specifies a linear spring rate of 300 lbf/in, free length 6 in and inside diameter 2.5 in. Conversion gives about 52,538.1 N/m. Sprung mass, damping and road shape are chosen model inputs. These are not measured vehicle or shock-absorber specifications.

Worked example · A real spring with a chosen body mass

With the cited spring, choose \(m=300\) kg and \(c=2000\) N s/m. Then \(\omega_n\approx13.2335\) rad/s and \(\zeta\approx0.251885\). A smooth 0.05 m bump lasting 0.4 s is modeled as \(r=0.05\sin^2(\pi t/0.4)\) during the bump and zero afterward. At \(t=0.8\), the body is about 0.0122190 m above equilibrium, although the road has returned to zero.

The squared-sine bump has continuous displacement and velocity at its endpoints. During it, kr+cr′ is a constant plus a sine-cosine pair. Solve those components and continue freely after the bump. RK4 checks the original relative-motion equation independently.

Worked example · Road motion can be amplified

Keep the same chosen mass and damping. Drive a 0.02 m sinusoidal road at \(\omega_n\). The steady body-to-road amplitude ratio is about 2.22269. At \(t=1\), the total body displacement is about 0.0391913 m. That one sample includes a transient; the steady ratio is a separate quantity.

Common mistake

The shock sees relative velocity, not body velocity alone. Also, do not treat the manufacturer's spring rate as a measured complete-car suspension response.

What the model leaves out

This one-mass quarter-car omits tyre compliance, unsprung mass, nonlinear shocks, linkage ratio, bump stops, roll and pitch. It cannot predict wheel hop. The drawing exaggerates motion and does not imply measured geometry or a particular vehicle fitment.

9The series RLC circuit

Let q be the charge on the chosen capacitor plate and \(I=q'\) the corresponding loop current. Kirchhoff's voltage balance gives \(LI'+RI+q/C=V(t)\). Substituting current yields

\[Lq''+Rq'+q/C=V(t).\]

Thus \(m\leftrightarrow L\), \(c\leftrightarrow R\), \(k\leftrightarrow1/C\). The mathematical operator is the same, while physical units and energy meanings differ. The undamped natural frequency is \(1/\sqrt{LC}\); critical resistance is \(2\sqrt{L/C}\).

Derivation · Electrical energy balance

Electric and magnetic energy sum to \(E=q^2/(2C)+LI^2/2\). Differentiate: \(E'=I(q/C+LI')=VI-RI^2\). With no source, resistance removes energy while charge and current exchange storage between the capacitor and inductor.

Worked example · Charged capacitor, zero initial current

Choose \(L=0.1\) H, \(C=10\) µF and \(R=40\) Ω. Initially capacitor voltage is 5 V and current is zero, so \(q_0=50\) µC. The natural frequency is 1000 rad/s and damping ratio is 0.2. At 4 ms, the model gives \(q\approx-19.2271\) µC and \(I\approx16.0867\) mA. Energy has fallen from 125 µJ to about 31.4231 µJ.

Common mistake

At a charge extremum, current is zero but capacitor energy need not be. Increasing resistance changes damping and damped frequency; it does not change the undamped quantity 1/√(LC).

What the model leaves out

The drawn circuit uses ideal wires, a linear inductor and a constant capacitor. Parasitic resistance, leakage, magnetic saturation and source impedance are absent. The component values are chosen teaching inputs, not a product specification.

Read: Lebl §2.4, electrical analogy. Another view: OCW LRC circuits and damping ratio.
