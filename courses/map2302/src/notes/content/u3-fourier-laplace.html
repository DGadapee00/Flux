Fourier series and Laplace transforms

A complicated input can be assembled from simpler inputs. Fourier series use periodic harmonics. Impulses and convolution keep a causal history. Laplace transforms turn differentiation and initial conditions into algebra.

Part I

Periodic inputs

1Fourier coefficients

Use a period of 2L. The fundamental angular frequency is π/L. Harmonic n completes n cycles in that interval. Seek an expansion in a constant, cosines and sines. These basis functions are orthogonal under the inner product given by integrating their product over a period.

Derivation · Projection selects one coefficient

Multiply the expansion by \(\cos(m\pi t/L)\) and integrate from −L to L. Product-to-sum identities make every cross-frequency integral vanish. The matching cosine squared integrates to L, so its coefficient is the original function's projection divided by L. The same argument gives sine coefficients. The constant has squared norm 2L, accounting for the conventional half factor.

\[f\sim a_0/2+\sum_{n\ge1}(a_n\cos(n\pi t/L)+b_n\sin(n\pi t/L)),\quad a_n=\frac1L\int_{-L}^{L}f\cos(n\pi t/L)dt,\quad b_n=\frac1L\int_{-L}^{L}f\sin(n\pi t/L)dt.\]

An odd function has zero cosine coefficients, including its mean. An even function has zero sine coefficients. Symmetry removes an entire family before integration. It does not imply that the remaining coefficients are positive.

Worked example · A square wave from rotating harmonics

Choose a 2π-periodic wave equal to −1 for −π<t<0 and +1 for 0<t<π. Oddness removes cosines. Integrating the sine products gives \(b_n=2[1-(-1)^n]/(n\pi)\). Only odd harmonics survive. Keeping n≤9 gives \(f_9(\pi/2)\approx1.06305\), although the plateau is 1. The rotating-vector drawing adds the retained terms.

Common mistake

The average is a0/2, not a0. Also distinguish angular frequency nπ/L from n/(2L) cycles per second. Calling L the full period doubles every frequency.

Read: Lebl §4.2. Another view: OCW Fourier basics.

2Convergence and operations

Theorem · Piecewise-smooth convergence

For a piecewise-smooth periodic function, the series converges to the function at continuous points and to the average of one-sided limits at a jump. This does not promise uniform convergence across a jump.

Changing one assigned point leaves coefficient integrals unchanged. Consequently the series cannot recover an arbitrary assigned value there. Near a jump, extra harmonics narrow the oscillatory region. Its limiting overshoot remains about 8.95% of the jump height. For a jump from −1 to 1, that means a peak near 1.179, not 1.0895.

Integration divides each nonconstant harmonic by its frequency. The mean contributes a linear term. Differentiation multiplies by frequency and can destroy convergence. Termwise differentiation needs stronger hypotheses; a square wave's derivative includes impulses at jumps.

Worked example · A corner is not a jump

Extend \(f(t)=|t|\) on −1<t<1 periodically. It is even and continuous, so \(a_0/2=1/2\), \(b_n=0\), and integration by parts gives \(a_n=2[(-1)^n-1]/(n\pi)^2\). Its coefficients decay quadratically. At t=0.4, the n≤9 sum is about 0.400125, close to 0.4. A slope corner does not create the same fixed jump overshoot.

A half-range sine series means an odd periodic extension from [0,L]. A cosine series means an even extension. The same interior formula can produce different endpoint behaviour through those different extensions. Select the extension from the boundary or modelling question; do not silently change it.

Common mistake

Pointwise convergence does not automatically permit termwise differentiation. A finite sum can be differentiated, but its derivative need not converge to an ordinary function representing the original derivative at a jump.

What the model leaves out

An ideal jump requires arbitrarily high frequencies. Real switches have finite rise times and measurement bandwidth. A truncated expansion describes a bandwidth choice, not an instantaneous physical switch.

Read: Lebl §§4.3–4.4. Another view: OCW operations on Fourier series.

3Periodic forcing and resonance

For a finite Fourier input, linearity lets us solve one harmonic at a time. At frequency ω the complex force-to-position gain is \(1/(k-m\omega^2+ic\omega)\). Multiply each input phasor by this gain, then add the real responses. Include each homogeneous correction if zero initial data are required.

For an infinite expansion, convergence and regularity must justify the limiting response. A stable damped system strongly attenuates high force harmonics. An undamped system is different: any nonzero harmonic matching its natural frequency creates a secular term. Avoiding resonance at the fundamental alone is insufficient.

Worked example · The third harmonic matters

Drive \(x''+0.5x'+9x=f_9(t)\), where f₉ is the preceding square-wave sum. Start at rest. The natural frequency is 3 rad/s, close to the third drive harmonic, although the fundamental is 1. Adding all retained zero-state responses gives \(x(2)\approx0.0525707\). Damping keeps the resonant harmonic bounded. The lab independently integrates the summed force.

What the model leaves out

Harmonic superposition requires linear coefficients. Saturation, impacts and amplitude-dependent stiffness generate new frequencies and invalidate simple independent-harmonic addition.

Read: Lebl §4.5. Another view: OCW periodic input and resonance.

Part II

Causal inputs

4Steps, impulses and derivatives

The unit step u(t−d) switches from zero to one at d. Its isolated point value usually has no effect on an ordinary integral. An impulse δ(t−d) has unit area concentrated at d; it has no ordinary finite height. Its defining sampling rule is \(\int f(t)\delta(t-d)dt=f(d)\) for a continuous multiplier.

The generalized derivative of u(t−d) is δ(t−d). A jump of height J contributes Jδ to the derivative. A continuous ramp \((t-d)u(t-d)\) has derivative u(t−d), because its delta coefficient contains the vanishing factor t−d. Differentiate piecewise formulas with their jumps included.

Derivation · Mechanical impulse jump

Integrate \(mx''+cx'+kx=J\delta(t-d)\) across a shrinking interval. For bounded position and finite one-sided velocities, the damping and spring integrals vanish. The acceleration integral gives \(m[v(d^+)-v(d^-)]=J\). Position remains continuous; a position jump would introduce a derivative of delta.

Worked example · The mass does not teleport

A 2 kg mass at rest receives a 3 N s impulse at t=1 s. Immediately afterward x is still zero and velocity is 1.5 m/s. The lab uses right-hand values at the impact instant, with zero preinitial data. Spring and damping govern the subsequent motion.

Common mistake

A step height is a force; an impulse weight is force times time. A finite force step changes acceleration without a velocity jump. An ideal force impulse changes velocity, not position.

What the model leaves out

A real impact lasts a finite time. The impulse approximation preserves its integrated momentum change, not its contact-force history, deformation or peak stress.

Another view: OCW steps, delta functions and generalized derivatives; Lebl §6.4.

5Step and impulse responses

The impulse response h vanishes before input arrival. For a second-order operator, solve the homogeneous equation afterward with x(0⁺)=0 and v(0⁺)=1/m. This separates the singular forcing from an ordinary post-impact IVP.

Integrating an impulse in time produces a step. Under zero preinitial data, the step response is therefore \(S(t)=\int_0^t h(v)dv\). A delayed force F u(t−d) gives F S(t−d)u(t−d). A finite pulse subtracts the same step response delayed to its switch-off time.

Worked example · A step response takes time

For \(x''+x'+4x=u(t-1)\), start at rest. Its impulse response is \(e^{-t/2}\sin(\sqrt{15}t/2)/(\sqrt{15}/2)\). Integrate it to obtain the step response. At t=2 s, one second after arrival, \(x\approx0.267661\) m and \(v\approx0.292500\) m/s. The final static displacement is 1/4 m, so the transient can overshoot it.

An existing free response must be added on its original time coordinate. Delaying the input does not freeze a mass already displaced from equilibrium.

Another view: OCW unit step and impulse response.

6Convolution

Derivation · Sum shifted impulse responses

An input slice arriving near τ has area f(τ)dτ. Its later contribution is h(t−τ)f(τ)dτ. Adding all slices gives \((h*f)(t)=\int_0^t h(t-\tau)f(\tau)d\tau\). Substituting v=t−τ proves commutativity. The same output can be viewed by input arrival time or response age.

Convolving with δ returns the original causal function. Convolving with the constant 1 takes its running integral. These are different identities: “unit impulse” and “unit-height constant” do not mean the same operation.

Worked example · A capacitor remembers an earlier pulse

For an ideal RC circuit, \(V_C'+aV_C=aV_{in}\). Choose a=2/s, zero initial voltage and a 3 V pulse from 0.5 to 1.5 s. Its impulse response is \(ae^{-at}u(t)\). At t=2, integrating only over the earlier pulse gives \(V_C=3(1-e^{-2})e^{-1}\approx0.954277\) V. The present input is zero, while the output is positive.

Common mistake

Convolution is not h(t)f(t). It integrates shifted products over the input history. After a source turns off, stored state can keep producing output.

What the model leaves out

The RC example assumes constant ideal components, no leakage and a source without internal impedance. Its chosen RC product is a teaching input, not a measured component specification.

Read: Lebl §6.3. Another view: OCW convolution.

Part III

Transform methods

7The Laplace transform

Definition · A weighted causal integral

For suitable f, \(F(s)=\int_0^\infty e^{-st}f(t)dt\). Piecewise continuity and exponential order provide a sufficient region of convergence for ordinary inputs. Impulses extend the definition through their sampling rule.

Integrating exp[−(s+a)t] gives \(\mathcal L\{e^{-at}\}=1/(s+a)\), requiring Re(s+a)>0. Integration by parts gives \(\mathcal L\{t\}=1/s^2\); repeating it yields \(\mathcal L\{t^n\}=n!/s^{n+1}\). For a complex sinusoid, the same integral gives 1/(s−iω). Its real and imaginary parts are s/(s²+ω²) and ω/(s²+ω²), the cosine and sine pairs for real s>0.

Changing variables t=v+d shows \(\mathcal L\{u(t-d)f(t-d)\}=e^{-sd}F(s)\). Multiplication by exp(−at) instead replaces s by s+a. These are different operations.

Worked example · A transformed RC step

Keep a=2/s and apply a permanent 3 V step at d=0.5 s. Then \(V_C(s)=6e^{-0.5s}/[s(s+2)]\). Inverting gives \(3[1-e^{-2(t-0.5)}]u(t-0.5)\). At t=2 s, the lab gives about 2.85064 V. The delayed waveform starts from zero at its arrival time.

Common mistake

The transform of u(t−d)f(t−d) uses the same F(s) times exp(−sd). For u(t−d)f(t), first rewrite the waveform in elapsed time. Gating and shifting a waveform are not identical.

For a periodic causal input of period P, split the defining integral into periods. Each later period contributes the first period integral times exp(−ksP). Summing that geometric series gives \(F(s)=\int_0^P e^{-st}f(t)dt/[1-e^{-sP}]\) where the sum converges. This is another representation of the periodic inputs studied earlier.

Read: Lebl §6.1. Another view: OCW Laplace basics.

8Partial fractions and inverse transforms

For distinct real poles, decompose a proper rational transform into simple fractions. Multiplying by s+a before substituting s=−a gives the residue at that pole. At a repeated pole of order r, include every power from 1/(s+a) through 1/(s+a)ʳ. Their inverses are polynomial times exponential, with factorial denominators.

For an irreducible quadratic, complete the square. A denominator \((s+a)^2+b^2\) pairs with a shifted-s numerator for cosine and a b numerator for sine. A general linear numerator must be split between those two pairs. Do polynomial division first if the transform is improper; polynomial pieces can represent impulses or their derivatives.

Worked example · Two poles with opposite residues

Invert \(1/[(s+1)(s+3)]\). Cover-up gives residues +1/2 and −1/2. Thus \(x=(e^{-t}-e^{-3t})/2\), with x(0)=0 and v(0)=1. It solves \(x''+4x'+3x=0\). At t=1, \(x\approx0.159046\). The lab shows this free IVP; identical poles with different numerators would give different mode weights.

Common mistake

A repeated pole needs its full partial-fraction ladder. Two copies of 1/(s+a) do not replace 1/(s+a)². Also, a pole location is not its residue.

Another view: OCW partial fractions and inverse transforms; Lebl §6.2.

9Initial-value problems

Integration by parts gives \(\mathcal L\{x'\}=sX-x(0)\). Apply it again to obtain \(\mathcal L\{x''\}=s^2X-sx_0-v_0\). For ordinary initial-value problems these are right-hand initial data; an impulse at the origin instead requires a consistent preinitial convention and explicit jump.

\[(ms^2+cs+k)X=U+m(sx_0+v_0)+cx_0.\]

The initial terms move to the right with positive signs. Divide by the characteristic polynomial only after collecting them. A finite pulse contributes \(F(e^{-ds}-e^{-(d+T)s})/s\). Invert its two shifted step terms, then add the free response without shifting its clock.

Worked example · Delayed input does not delay existing motion

Take m=1, c=1, k=4, x₀=1, v₀=0. A unit step arrives at t=1. At t=0.5 it has not arrived, yet free motion already gives \(x\approx0.607055\) m and \(v\approx-1.32538\) m/s. Replacing every t with t−1 would erase the initial-state motion. The level-3 practice problem adds a later switch-off too.

Common mistake

A transfer function does not encode nonzero initial displacement or velocity. Retain both derivative-transform terms and keep them separate from delayed forcing.

Another view: OCW Laplace IVPs; Lebl §6.2.

10Transfer functions and poles

With zero initial state, \(H(s)=X/U=1/p(s)\). The inverse transform h is the impulse response. Multiplication of transforms corresponds to convolution: inserting the two defining integrals and changing their order converts the triangular history region into the product F(s)H(s). Thus \(x=h*f\), Green's formula for this causal linear problem.

A simple pole r produces an exponential mode exp(rt). A repeated pole produces polynomial factors. For a stable system, evaluating H(iω) gives the steady sinusoidal gain and phase. Poles near the imaginary axis can produce narrow frequency peaks and long transients. Evaluate possible cancellations before identifying visible modes from a rational response.

Worked example · A critically damped pulse

Choose \(x''+2x'+x=u(t-0.5)-u(t-1.5)\), initially at rest. The double pole is −1. Its unit step response is \(S(t)=1-(1+t)e^{-t}\) for t≥0. At t=2, subtract \(S(0.5)\) from \(S(1.5)\), obtaining about 0.351971 m. The repeated-pole factor remains even after the input stops.

The exponential-response formula is the same operator relation evaluated at a forcing rate: H(r)=1/p(r), provided p(r)≠0. At a pole, division fails and root multiplicity determines the adjusted resonant trial. A stable transfer function also assumes a causal region of convergence consistent with decay.

Another view: OCW transfer functions and Green's formula, then poles and exponential response.

11A radio-clock tuner

NIST identifies WWVB's carrier as 60 kHz. The lab draws an antenna, coil, resistor, capacitor and clock. It models a chosen series RLC tuning stage, not a complete receiver or a particular commercial product.

The charge equation is \(Lq''+Rq'+q/C=V_0\cos(2\pi f_ct)\). Choose L=0.01 H and compute \(C=1/[(2\pi f_c)^2L]\approx703.619\) pF. Choose R≈188.496 Ω and an illustrative coupled voltage of 1 mV. These choices give Q=20 and envelope decay time \(2L/R\approx0.106103\) ms.

Worked example · Narrow tuning has a transient cost

At the baseline tuning, halving R doubles both Q and the envelope decay time. The circuit becomes more selective but retains its transient longer. Raising L by 20% at fixed C instead lowers the tuned frequency to \(60/\sqrt{1.2}\approx54.7723\) kHz. Tuning and settling time answer different questions.

To check a switched carrier, retain RF phase. Turning the cosine off at T subtracts a delayed sinusoid with coefficients \(V_0\cos(\omega T)\) and \(-V_0\sin(\omega T)\) in local elapsed time. The lab's RK4 step resolves at least 256 samples per carrier cycle. Refined integration checks the phase rather than only a slowly varying envelope.

Common mistake

A sharper resonance does not imply a faster transient. Also, changing L at fixed C changes the tuning frequency. A carrier-tuning model is not a time decoder.

What the model leaves out

Antenna coupling/calibration, amplifier noise, rectification, decoding, propagation and WWVB's complete modulation protocol are absent. The chosen switch-off test is a response experiment. The animated clock hand is illustrative, not a decoded time output.
