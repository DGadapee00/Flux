Part I

Local rules and solution intervals

1What a solution means

Write a first-order ordinary differential equation as \(y'=f(t,y)\). There is one independent variable, usually time. “First order” means no derivative above the first appears. The dependent variable need not represent a distance. It may be temperature, concentration or a dimensionless response.

Definition · Solution of an IVP

A solution is a differentiable function on an interval that satisfies the equation at every point. For an initial-value problem it also satisfies \(y(t_0)=y_0\). The interval must contain \(t_0\).

Checking means differentiating the proposed function and substituting it into the original equation. Checking an initial value alone is insufficient. Conversely, a function can satisfy the differential equation but miss the specified initial value.

If the right side depends only on time, integration solves the IVP directly. The fundamental theorem of calculus gives

\[y(t)=y_0+\int_{t_0}^t f(u)\,du.\]

Differentiation recovers the equation. At the initial point the integral vanishes, leaving the correct starting value. For \(y'=at^2\), this becomes \(y=y_0+at^3/3\). The integration constant is a datum to determine, not a term to omit.

Worked example · An integral is an exact solution

Take \(y'=e^{-t^2}\), \(y(0)=1\). Then \(y(1)=1+\int_0^1 e^{-u^2}\,du\approx1.746824\). No elementary antiderivative is needed. Quadrature evaluates the integral; RK4 integrates the original slope equation independently. Both routes agree. The slope-field lab shows the initial point and the resulting curve.

Keep classification separate from solving. An equation is linear when it has the form \(y'+p(t)y=q(t)\). Coefficients can depend on time. Thus \(y'=ty\) is linear, while \(y'=y^2\) is nonlinear. Both are separable, so “linear” and “separable” are not exclusive categories.

Common mistake

A definite integral is not an unfinished answer merely because it has no elementary antiderivative. The initial-value formula already specifies a function exactly.

Read: Lebl §1.1. Another view: OCW basic equations and separation.

2Separation without losing branches

Suppose \(y'=g(t)h(y)\). On an interval where \(h(y)\ne0\), divide and integrate:

\[\int\frac{dy}{h(y)}=\int g(t)\,dt+C.\]

First inspect the zeros of \(h\). Each constant root is an equilibrium solution excluded by division. Keep those branches separately. Also keep restrictions introduced by logarithms, inverse functions and denominators.

Derivation · Signed exponential growth

For \(y'=aty\), the nonzero branch gives \(\ln|y|=at^2/2+C\). Exponentiation initially gives \(|y|=e^C e^{at^2/2}\). The sign belongs to the arbitrary multiplier, so the full family is \(y=D e^{at^2/2}\), with positive, negative and zero \(D\). An absolute value inside a logarithm does not force positive solutions.

Worked example · A negative branch

For \(y'=ty\), \(y(0)=-2\), the constant is \(D=-2\). Hence \(y(1)=-2e^{1/2}\approx-3.29744\). Differentiating gives \(y'=ty\), and the value remains negative. The lab compares this expression with RK4.

Separation can also reveal finite-time failure. For \(y'=y^2\), nonzero solutions satisfy \(-1/y=t+C\). Initial data gives \(y=y_0/(1-y_0t)\). For positive \(y_0\), the denominator vanishes at \(t=1/y_0\).

Worked example · The interval is part of the answer

Let \(y(0)=2\) in \(y'=y^2\). At \(t=1/4\), \(y=4\). The forward endpoint is \(1/2\); the maximal interval containing zero is \((-\infty,1/2)\). The same rational expression beyond its pole does not extend this IVP solution through the pole.

Common mistake

Dividing by \(y\) or \(y^2\) deletes \(y=0\). Check it in the original equation. Also, excluding a pole does not make the two disconnected sides one solution interval.

A positive derivative does not imply an unbounded future value. For \(y'=k_0y/(a+t)^2\), integrate the logarithmic rate from zero: \(\ln(y/y_0)=k_0[1/a-1/(a+t)]\). Its total accumulated area is finite, so \(y\to y_0e^{k_0/a}\), despite continued positive growth.

Worked example · Finite cumulative growth

Choose a=2, k₀=4 and y(0)=2. At t=2 the solution is \(2e\approx5.43656\). Its limiting value is \(2e^2\approx14.7781\), rather than infinity. This is the same structure as OCW's variable natural-growth rate in Unit I, with fresh teaching inputs.

Not every separable solution can be solved explicitly for \(y\). An implicit relation is acceptable, with its domain and chosen branch stated. Read: Lebl §§1.1–1.3.

3Fields, existence and uniqueness

At each point \((t,y)\), draw a short segment with slope \(f(t,y)\). A solution must follow those tangents. An isocline is a set where \(f(t,y)=c\), so every segment on it has the same slope. Isoclines are not generally solution curves.

For \(y'=t-2y\), an isocline has equation \(y=(t-c)/2\). A horizontal tangent lies on \(y=t/2\), but that sloping line has derivative \(1/2\), not zero. It is not a solution. The nearby line \(y=t/2-1/4\) is a solution, because its derivative and the right side both equal \(1/2\).

Theorem · Local existence and uniqueness

If \(f\) is continuous in a rectangle around the initial point, a local solution exists. If \(f\) is also locally Lipschitz in \(y\), that local solution is unique. Continuous \(\partial f/\partial y\) on the rectangle is a useful sufficient condition for this second hypothesis.

“Local” matters. The smooth field \(y'=y^2\) has a locally unique solution through every initial point, yet a positive trajectory can blow up. Uniqueness does not imply existence for every future time. Where uniqueness holds, two trajectories cannot pass through the same point and then disagree nearby.

Worked example · Waiting at zero

For \(y'=2\sqrt{|y|}\), \(y(0)=0\), one solution is zero forever. Another is zero until a chosen time \(\tau\ge0\), then \(y=(t-\tau)^2\). At the join, both value and derivative are zero. With \(\tau=0\), the departing branch has \(y(1)=1\); the zero branch still has value zero. The lab draws these analytic branches separately.

The root function is continuous but not locally Lipschitz at zero. RK4 started exactly at zero follows the zero branch. That numerical result cannot establish uniqueness or discover every branch. In this scenario the lab pins the initial point at zero so the analytic comparison remains explicit.

Common mistake

A solver returning one curve is not evidence that an IVP has only one curve. Check the theorem's hypotheses. For \(y'=1/t\) at \(t=0\), the equation itself is not defined there, so even the continuity hypothesis fails.

Read: Lebl §1.2. Another view: OCW geometric methods.

4Euler steps and their limits

Use a tangent over one short interval. Taylor expansion gives \(y(t+h)=y(t)+hy'(t)+O(h^2)\). Replacing the derivative by the known slope produces Euler's update:

\[t_{n+1}=t_n+h,\qquad y_{n+1}=y_n+h f(t_n,y_n).\]

The omitted local term is order \(h^2\). There are about \(1/h\) steps on a fixed interval. Under smoothness and stability assumptions, these errors accumulate to order \(h\). Thus halving the step approximately halves the endpoint error once the computation is in the resolved regime.

Worked example · Resolving decay

For \(y'=-y\), \(y(0)=1\), take 20 steps to \(t=2\). Then \(h=0.1\), and repeated multiplication gives \(y_{20}=0.9^{20}\approx0.121577\). The exact value is \(e^{-2}\approx0.135335\). Absolute error is about \(0.0137586\). The gold curve is exact; the blue segments show the actual Euler steps.

Stability is a separate question from consistency. For \(y'=-ky\), Euler multiplies each value by \(1-kh\). Its magnitude decays only if \(|1-kh|<1\), which gives \(0<h<2/k\). When \(1<kh<2\), the samples alternate signs while shrinking. At \(kh=2\) they do not decay. Above that boundary they grow.

Common mistake

The exact stable exponential does not guarantee a stable numerical step. A coarse Euler trajectory can cross zero and grow, even though the exact positive solution does neither.

For nonlinear fields, recompute the slope after every update. Freezing it at the initial point draws one tangent line, not an Euler trajectory. A source-case audit reproduces OCW's three-step nonlinear example using both floating and exact rational arithmetic.

What the model leaves out

A fixed-step approximation has truncation and roundoff errors. Refinement cannot repair a missing physical process or choose among nonunique branches. A convergence claim needs a fixed interval away from singularities.

Read: Lebl §1.7. Another view: OCW numerical methods.

Part II

Linear input and response

5The integrating factor

Start from \(y'+p(t)y=q(t)\) on an interval where both coefficients are continuous. Seek a multiplier \(\mu\) that turns the left side into a product derivative. Since \((\mu y)'=\mu y'+\mu' y\), require \(\mu'=p\mu\). Separation gives a convenient nonzero choice:

\[\mu(t)=\exp\!\left(\int p(t)\,dt\right),\qquad (\mu y)'=\mu q.\]

Integrate the entire multiplied equation. A definite-integral version handles the initial value cleanly:

\[y(t)=\frac{\mu(t_0)y_0+\int_{t_0}^t\mu(u)q(u)\,du}{\mu(t)}.\]

A constant multiplier on \(\mu\) cancels. Choosing a convenient one does not discard solutions. Coefficient singularities still restrict the interval.

Worked example · A ramp has a lag

Solve \(y'+2y=t\), \(y(0)=1\). With \(\mu=e^{2t}\), integration by parts gives \(y=t/2-1/4+(5/4)e^{-2t}\). At \(t=1\), this is \(0.419169\). The tempting value \(t/2\) only makes the instantaneous right side zero; it ignores the derivative of a moving response.

Worked example · A variable coefficient

For \(y'+2ty=e^{t-t^2}\), \(y(0)=-1\), use \(\mu=e^{t^2}\). Then \((e^{t^2}y)'=e^t\), giving \(y=e^{t-t^2}-2e^{-t^2}\). At \(t=1\), \(y=1-2/e\approx0.264241\). Both the coefficient and forcing vary with time, but the equation remains linear.

A mixing model shows why the coefficient can vary. Let a vessel contain volume \(V\) and dissolved mass \(m\). With perfect mixing, the outgoing concentration is \(m/V\). Conservation gives

\[V(t)=V_0+(q_{\rm in}-q_{\rm out})t,\qquad m'=q_{\rm in}c_{\rm in}-q_{\rm out}\frac m{V(t)}.\]

For \(q=q_{\rm in}-q_{\rm out}\ne0\), the integrating factor is \(V^{q_{\rm out}/q}\). Integrating and applying the initial mass yields

\[m=c_{\rm in}V+(m_0-c_{\rm in}V_0)(V_0/V)^{q_{\rm out}/q}.\]
Worked example · Concentration follows current volume

Choose \(V_0=60\) L, \(m_0=15\) kg, inflow 4 L/min at 0.2 kg/L, and outflow 2 L/min. After 10 min, \(V=80\) L and \(m=16+3(60/80)=18.25\) kg. Concentration is \(18.25/80=0.228125\) kg/L. These are teaching inputs, not measurements of a real tank.

Common mistake

Multiply the forcing as well as the left side by the integrating factor. In a mixing balance, use current volume in the outgoing concentration; replacing it with the initial volume solves a different model.

What the model leaves out

The vessel model assumes uniform concentration and prescribed flow rates. It stops before overflow or depletion. Stratification, reaction, density changes and flow variation are absent.

Read: Lebl §1.4. Another view: OCW integrating factors.

6Complex arithmetic and exponentials

Write \(z=x+iy\), with \(i^2=-1\). Addition combines components. Multiplication distributes and replaces \(i^2\) by \(-1\). The conjugate is \(\bar z=x-iy\); multiplying by it gives \(z\bar z=x^2+y^2\). This real quantity explains the modulus \(|z|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) and the reciprocal \(1/z=\bar z/|z|^2\), for nonzero \(z\).

Derivation · Euler's identity

Expand \(e^{i\theta}\) in its power series. Even powers of \(i\) alternate between 1 and −1, producing the cosine series. Odd powers produce \(i\) times the sine series. Therefore \(e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\). Differentiating confirms that this function solves \(z'=iz\), with \(z(0)=1\).

Thus \(z=re^{i\theta}\). Products multiply radii and add angles; reciprocals invert the radius and negate the angle. For integer \(n\), \(z^n=r^n e^{in\theta}\). Arguments differing by \(2\pi\) describe the same nonzero point. A chosen principal argument requires a stated interval and careful quadrants.

Worked example · A reciprocal phasor

For \(1+i\), modulus is \(\sqrt2\) and argument is \(\pi/4\). Its reciprocal is \((1-i)/2\), with modulus \(1/\sqrt2\approx0.707107\) and argument \(-\pi/4\). In the response lab, set \(a=1\), \(\omega=1\), \(A=1\). The same reciprocal is the complex gain. The output lag is positive \(\pi/4\), because the output is written with a minus sign in its cosine argument.

Common mistake

Ordinary arctangent of y/x loses quadrant information. Use the signs of both components, or atan2. Also distinguish the gain's negative argument from the positive lag in cos(ωt−lag).

7Sinusoids, gain and phase

Expand \(R\cos(\omega t-\phi)\). Matching coefficients in \(a\cos\omega t+b\sin\omega t\) gives \(R\cos\phi=a\) and \(R\sin\phi=b\). Therefore \(R=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\), \(\phi=\operatorname{atan2}(b,a)\). The two coefficients form a vector, so adding them is not an amplitude calculation. Period is \(2\pi/\omega\), not \(1/\omega\).

Now solve \(y'+ay=A\cos\omega t\), with \(a>0\). Replace the forcing by \(Ae^{i\omega t}\), and seek a complex response \(Be^{i\omega t}\). Substitution gives \((a+i\omega)B=A\). Take the real part after division:

\[y_p=\frac{A}{\sqrt{a^2+\omega^2}}\cos(\omega t-\phi),\qquad \phi=\arctan(\omega/a).\]

The homogeneous solution is \(Ce^{-at}\). Fixing \(y(0)=y_0\) gives \(C=y_0-Aa/(a^2+\omega^2)\). The total response contains both the forced sinusoid and the free transient. Initial conditions affect the transient, not the eventual gain or phase.

Worked example · Gain and transient are different

Choose \(a=1\), \(A=2\), \(\omega=1\), \(y_0=0\). The steady response is \(\cos t+\sin t\), with amplitude \(\sqrt2\) and lag \(\pi/4\). The initial condition adds \(-e^{-t}\). At \(t=2\), the total is \(0.357815\), whereas the steady term alone is \(0.493151\). The lab overlays the input and total response.

Gain here is \(G=1/\sqrt{a^2+\omega^2}\). It approaches \(1/a\) at low frequency and approximately \(1/\omega\) at high frequency. Doubling frequency only approximately halves the gain in that latter regime. In a tracking equation \(y'=a(u-y)\), the right-side amplitude is \(a\) times the input amplitude, so the tracking gain has an extra factor \(a\).

What the model leaves out

This is a linear time-invariant model with constant coefficients. Saturation, delays and coefficients that change with amplitude are absent. A steady periodic approximation also omits the still-decaying initial transient.

Part III

Models and qualitative behavior

8Autonomous equations and stability

An autonomous equation has the form \(y'=f(y)\). Time does not appear explicitly. A phase line marks the roots of \(f\) and the sign on each interval between them. Positive slope moves the state upward along the line; negative slope moves it downward.

At an equilibrium \(y_*\), write \(y=y_*+\eta\). Taylor expansion gives \(\eta'=f'(y_*)\eta+O(\eta^2)\). A negative derivative gives local decay of a small perturbation; a positive derivative gives growth. If the derivative vanishes, inspect signs or higher-order terms. The linear term alone gives no verdict.

Logistic growth has \(f(y)=ry(1-y/K)\). Its equilibria are zero and \(K\). For positive \(r,K\), zero repels positive perturbations and \(K\) attracts positive states. Adding constant harvest gives \(f(y)=ry(1-y/K)-H\).

Derivation · A harvest threshold

The production parabola reaches its maximum at \(y=K/2\), where production is \(rK/4\). Solving the quadratic balance gives \(y_\pm=K[1\pm\sqrt{1-4H/(rK)}]/2\). Below the threshold there are two roots; the lower repels and the upper attracts. At the threshold they coalesce. Above it there is no real equilibrium.

Worked example · Two equilibria can disappear

Set \(r=1\), \(K=10\), \(H=1\). The roots are about \(1.12702\) and \(8.87298\); maximum sustainable harvest is \(2.5\). A trajectory starting at 8 reaches about \(8.77784\) at time 3 and continues toward the upper root. Raise \(H\) to 3: every arrow points downward and no equilibrium remains.

Common mistake

A positive derivative below an equilibrium does not imply that the trajectory crosses it. Where local uniqueness holds, the equilibrium is a separate solution. A stable root is approached asymptotically.

What the model leaves out

Logistic growth summarizes limited resources with constant r and K. Constant harvest may predict a negative signed state after depletion. That is a model-domain failure, not a negative physical population. Seasonal variation and demographic randomness are absent.

Read: Lebl §1.6. Another view: OCW autonomous equations.

9A cooling coffee server

The application lab draws a HARIO XGSR-60-TB glass server and a cup. HARIO publishes overall dimensions of 145 × 121 × 120 mm. Those dimensions identify the object and guide its silhouette. They do not specify a cooling coefficient. The 300 mL fill, temperatures and coefficient in the lab are chosen teaching inputs.

A lumped heat balance is \(mc_pT'=-hA_s(T-T_a)\). If mass, heat capacity, transfer area and effective heat-transfer coefficient are constant, define \(k=hA_s/(mc_p)>0\). Then \(T'=-k(T-T_a)\). Separate the temperature excess \(\theta=T-T_a\) to obtain \(T=T_a+(T_0-T_a)e^{-kt}\).

Worked example · Half the excess, not half the Celsius value

Choose \(T_0=90\) °C, \(T_a=20\) °C and \(k=0.001\) s⁻¹. After 600 s, \(T=20+70e^{-0.6}\approx58.4168\) °C. Excess half-time is \(\ln2/k\approx693.147\) s, when temperature is 55 °C. Doubling the initial excess leaves this half-time unchanged in the constant-k model.

A measured pair of temperatures can fit \(k\). With known ambient and elapsed time, divide the second excess by the first, then take its logarithm. The result is a fitted parameter for those conditions. It is not a manufacturer's retention measurement.

If ambient changes at \(t_s\), solve in two segments. First compute \(T_s=T_{a1}+(T_0-T_{a1})e^{-kt_s}\). Use \(T_s\) as the next segment's initial value. Temperature stays continuous, while its derivative can jump because the environment changed.

Worked example · A new ambient changes the equilibrium

Use the same initial data, but move the server into 10 °C air at 300 s. At the switch, \(T_s=20+70e^{-0.3}\). After another 600 s, \(T=10+(T_s-10)e^{-0.6}\approx43.9480\) °C. Starting the second exponential from the original 90 °C would reset the history incorrectly.

Common mistake

The law T=T₀ exp(−kt) in Celsius predicts cooling toward 0 °C. The equilibrium is the actual room temperature. Also, minutes and seconds change the numerical value of k by a factor of 60.

What the model leaves out

Evaporation, wall heat capacity, radiation, internal gradients and separate cup cooling are absent. The pouring animation conserves the chosen liquid volume but illustrates one common temperature. Pouring, exposed area and drafts can change a real fitted k. Overall dimensions alone cannot determine it.

Sources: HARIO dimensions; cooling method, Lebl §1.3. Another view: OCW Unit I first-order physical models.

10Linear versus nonlinear

For a linear operator \(L[y]=y'+p(t)y\), differentiation and multiplication distribute: \(L[ay_1+by_2]=aL[y_1]+bL[y_2]\). Consequently homogeneous solutions can be superposed. For nonzero forcing, adding two solutions doubles the forcing. Instead, add one particular solution to an arbitrary homogeneous solution.

Variable coefficients do not break linearity. Powers, products or nonlinear functions of the dependent variable do. A nonlinear equation may still be separable, easy to compute or useful as a model. Those are separate properties.

Worked example · Doubling initial data need not double the trajectory

For \(y'=y^2\), initial value 1 gives \(y(1/4)=4/3\). Initial value 2 gives \(y(1/4)=4\), not \(8/3\). Both satisfy the same smooth equation on their own intervals. The missing superposition property has nothing to do with numerical error or nonuniqueness.

Three questions should stay separate: Is the model linear? Is the IVP locally unique? Does its solution persist for all future time? The quadratic equation is nonlinear and locally unique, but may blow up. The root equation can be nonunique. The linear equations here are locally unique wherever their coefficients are continuous.

Unit 2 keeps the same input-response viewpoint and adds a second derivative. Complex exponentials will become modes, while initial data will determine two free constants.

Check the hypotheses and units before selecting a relation.

  • Accumulation: \(y=y_0+\int_{t_0}^t f(u)\,du\).
  • Separation: keep roots of \(h(y)\) before using \(\int dy/h(y)=\int g(t)dt+C\).
  • Linear IVP: \(\mu=e^{\int p\,dt}\), \(y=[\mu(t_0)y_0+\int_{t_0}^t\mu q\,du]/\mu(t)\).
  • Euler: \(y_{n+1}=y_n+h f(t_n,y_n)\); for decay \(y'=-ky\), require \(0<kh<2\) for decaying samples.
  • Complex arithmetic: \(e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\); \(1/z=\bar z/|z|^2\).
  • First-order forced response: \(G=1/\sqrt{a^2+\omega^2}\), positive lag \(\arctan(\omega/a)\), plus a free term \(Ce^{-at}\).
  • Cooling: \(T=T_a+(T_0-T_a)e^{-kt}\), excess half-time \(\ln2/k\).
  • Autonomous stability: inspect signs of \(f\); a nonzero \(f'(y_*)\) gives the local linear verdict.
