Part I

Linear state and modes

1From an equation to a state

A second-order equation needs position and velocity to determine its future. Introduce v=x′. Then \(mx''+cx'+kx=F\) becomes \(x'=v\), \(v'=(F-cv-kx)/m\). A scalar second-order equation is therefore a two-state first-order system.

\[\mathbf z'=A\mathbf z+\mathbf b(t),\qquad \mathbf z=\begin{pmatrix}x\\v\end{pmatrix},\quad A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-k/m&-c/m\end{pmatrix},\quad \mathbf b=\begin{pmatrix}0\\F/m\end{pmatrix}.\]

The order of state coordinates matters. Matrix multiplication means row-by-column dot products. A column of A tells where its corresponding coordinate basis vector is sent. Changing the coordinate order changes both the matrix and the interpretation of its output.

Worked example · One equation, two coordinates

For \(x''+2x'+2x=0\), choose x₀=1 and v₀=0. The companion matrix is \(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\-2&-2\end{smallmatrix}\right]\). Solving gives \(x=e^{-t}(\cos t+\sin t)\), \(v=-2e^{-t}\sin t\). At t=1, x≈0.508326 and v≈−0.619120. The phase-plane point contains both numbers at once.

Elimination can reverse the process. Differentiate one component equation and use the other to remove its second variable. This recovers a scalar equation when the coupling permits it. The matrix view avoids repeating elimination for every initial vector.

Common mistake

The companion matrix’s lower row has restoring coefficients −k/m and −c/m. It is not the row of positive coefficients from the original left-hand side. Keep the chosen coordinate order throughout.

Read: Lebl §§3.1–3.3. Another view: OCW linear systems.

2Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

Try a mode \(\mathbf z=e^{\lambda t}\mathbf v\). Substitution leaves \(A\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v\). A nonzero vector exists only when det(A−λI)=0. The eigenvalue gives the growth rate; its eigenvector gives a direction that the linear flow preserves.

Derivation · Modal coordinates

If independent eigenvectors form P, then AP=PΛ. Write z=Pw. Because P is constant, Pw′=APw=PΛw, so w′=Λw. Each modal coordinate evolves independently. Initial weights are \(w_0=P^{-1}z_0\), not the physical coordinates copied unchanged.

Worked example · Match the direction to the rate

For \(A=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}2&-4\\1&-3\end{smallmatrix}\right]\), eigenvalues are 1 and −2. The corresponding eigenvectors are (4,1) and (1,1). Starting on (4,1) gives \(z=e^t(4,1)\). At t=1, the coordinates are about (10.8731,2.71828). Pairing (4,1) with −2 would fail the original equation.

For a complex pair a±ib, take real and imaginary parts of a complex mode to obtain two real solutions. The flow rotates while its amplitude scales as exp(at). No real eigenline exists for a genuine complex pair, although the real two-dimensional solution space is complete.

Common mistake

Repeated eigenvalues do not guarantee independent eigenvectors. Count the nullspace dimension of A−λI. A two-dimensional repeated eigenvalue has a full eigenbasis only when A is scalar.

Another view: OCW eigenvalues and normal modes; Lebl §3.4.

3Coupled normal modes

Two masses linked by a spring generally exchange motion. Force balance gives \(M x''+Kx=0\), where \(M=\operatorname{diag}(m_1,m_2)\) and \(K=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}k_1+k_c&-k_c\\-k_c&k_3+k_c\end{smallmatrix}\right]\). A common-frequency trial x=v cos(ωt) gives Kv=ω²Mv.

Unequal masses require a generalised eigenproblem. Set q=M¹ᐟ²x. Then \(q''+M^{-1/2}KM^{-1/2}q=0\). The transformed stiffness is symmetric. Its orthonormal eigenvectors project both initial displacement and initial velocity into independent scalar oscillators. Transform back only after solving both sets of modal coefficients.

Worked example · One displaced mass excites two modes

Choose equal 1 kg masses, 2 N/m ground springs and a 1 N/m coupling spring. The symmetric mode has ω₁=√2 rad/s; the antisymmetric mode has ω₂=2 rad/s. Release from (1,0) m at rest. Both modes have equal physical-coordinate weights of 1/2. At t=1, x₁≈−0.130102 m and x₂≈0.286045 m. Total energy remains 1.5 J.

Derivation · Energy of the coupled system

Energy is \(E=(m_1v_1^2+m_2v_2^2)/2+k_1x_1^2/2+k_3x_2^2/2+k_c(x_1-x_2)^2/2\). Differentiate and substitute both force equations. Each spring-power term cancels its kinetic counterpart, leaving E′=0. Individual mass energies need not stay constant.

Worked example · Unequal masses need unequal mode vectors

Choose m₁=2, m₂=1, k₁=4, \(k_c=2\) and k₃=0 in SI. The frequencies are 1 and 2 rad/s, with displacement ratios (1,2) and (1,−1). Release from (1,−1) with velocities (0,6). Both sine and cosine weights matter. At t=1, positions are about (0.357498,4.69133) m. This chosen teaching motion is not a measured device.

What the model leaves out

Point masses and linear springs omit friction, finite-size impacts, spring mass and amplitude-dependent stiffness. Frequencies are independent of mode amplitude only within this linear model.

Read: Lebl §3.6; OCW matrix methods and normal modes.

4Phase portraits

For A with trace T and determinant D, the characteristic equation is λ²−Tλ+D=0. D<0 gives opposite-sign real roots and a saddle. D>0 with T<0 gives decay; T>0 gives growth. The discriminant T²−4D distinguishes a real node from a spiral.

When T=0 and D>0, a real linear two-state system has a centre. When D=0 or the discriminant vanishes, inspect the full matrix. A zero-real-part defective block can grow polynomially. These boundary cases cannot be labelled safely from a generic diagram alone.

Worked example · Same roots, different geometry

Compare \(A_1=-I\) with \(A_2=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}-1&1\\0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right]\). Both have trace −2, determinant 1 and double eigenvalue −1. From z₀=(0,1), the first gives (0,e⁻ᵗ); the second gives (te⁻ᵗ,e⁻ᵗ). At t=1, the second is about (0.367879,0.367879). Both decay, but their trajectories differ.

Common mistake

Zero eigenvalues do not imply bounded trajectories. The matrix [0,1;0,0] gives x=t, y=1 from (0,1), while the zero matrix keeps every state fixed. Their trace and determinant are identical.

Another view: OCW phase portraits; Lebl §3.5.

5Matrix exponentials

Definition · The identity-normalised fundamental matrix

Define \(e^{At}=I+At+A^2t^2/2!+\cdots\). Differentiating the convergent series gives E′=AE and E(0)=I. Thus the IVP solution is z=E(t)z₀. If Φ is another fundamental matrix, \(E(t)=\Phi(t)\Phi(0)^{-1}\).

A scalar matrix commutes with everything. Put τ=tr(A)/2 and B=A−τI. Cayley–Hamilton gives B²=δI, where δ=τ²−det(A). Separate even and odd powers. For δ>0, \(E=e^{\tau t}[I\cosh(\sqrt\delta t)+B\sinh(\sqrt\delta t)/\sqrt\delta]\). For δ<0 use cosine and sine. At δ=0 the expression becomes \(e^{\tau t}(I+tB)\). No eigenvalue-gap division is needed.

Worked example · Growth and rotation together

Take \(A=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\-4&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]\). Then τ=1 and B²=−4I. Consequently \(E=e^t\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\cos2t&\sin2t/2\\-2\sin2t&\cos2t\end{smallmatrix}\right]\). For z₀=(1,2), the t=1 state is about (1.34052,−7.20586). The real part causes growth while the imaginary part produces rotation.

A defective eigenvalue also admits a generalised vector w satisfying (A−λI)w=v. The second solution is \(e^{\lambda t}(tv+w)\). Multiplying the eigenvector alone by t misses the w contribution required by the coupled equation.

Common mistake

A fundamental matrix need not equal the matrix exponential until its initial matrix is normalised to I. Matrix factors generally do not commute; multiply Φ(0)⁻¹ on the right.

Read: Lebl §§3.7–3.8. Another view: OCW matrix exponentials.

Part II

Nonlinear geometry

6Equilibria and nonlinear models

For z′=f(z), an equilibrium satisfies every component of f=0. A nullcline sets one derivative to zero; it need not be an equilibrium. Intersections of all nullclines supply candidates. Check domain restrictions before interpreting them physically.

For predator–prey equations x′=ax−bxy and y′=−cy+dxy, factoring gives the origin and positive coexistence (c/d,a/b). At coexistence the Jacobian is \(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&-bc/d\\ad/b&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]\), with eigenvalues ±i√(ac). These imaginary roots alone do not prove nonlinear closed orbits.

Derivation · An invariant supplies more information

For positive populations define \(H=dx-c\ln x+by-a\ln y\). Differentiate along solutions. Substitution cancels the growth and interaction terms, so H′=0. Near the positive equilibrium its Hessian is positive definite. Nearby level sets are closed curves, providing nonlinear information beyond the Jacobian.

Worked example · Coexistence and its nearby orbit

Choose a=b=c=d=1, so coexistence is (1,1). Release from (1.3,1). At t=3 the nonlinear state is about (0.750186,0.994428). The tangent model instead gives (0.703002,1.042336). Both concern nearby motion, but the nonlinear orbit is not an exact circle.

What the model leaves out

This ideal ecology uses constant coefficients, continuous populations and bilinear encounters. Seasonal change, carrying capacity, demographic randomness and spatial structure are absent. Its chosen values are not field data.

Another view: OCW nonlinear systems; Lebl §§8.1,8.3.

7Linearization

Let z=z*+η near an equilibrium z*. Taylor expansion gives \(f(z_*+\eta)=J\eta+O(\|\eta\|^2)\), because f(z*)=0. The Jacobian J contains partial derivatives evaluated at the equilibrium. This is a tangent approximation to the vector field, not a replacement valid throughout the plane.

Theorem · Hyperbolic local behaviour

For a smooth autonomous system, an equilibrium is hyperbolic when no Jacobian eigenvalue has zero real part. Its nearby stability and qualitative orbit geometry persist under sufficiently small nonlinear terms. This does not promise equal numerical trajectories or global basins.

Worked example · The omitted term leaves a measurable difference

Consider x′=y, y′=−x+x²−0.3y. At the origin, \(J=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\-1&-0.3\end{smallmatrix}\right]\), a spiral sink. From (0.3,0), at t=3 the nonlinear x is about −0.141150, versus −0.183281 for the tangent model. Their state-vector difference has magnitude about 0.0576922. Reducing the initial offset improves local quantitative agreement.

The same undamped polynomial model has another equilibrium at (1,0). Its Jacobian has lower-left entry +1, so determinant is negative and the point is a saddle. Finding one stable equilibrium does not classify the whole vector field.

Common mistake

Differentiate first, then evaluate at the selected equilibrium. For −x+x² the derivative is −1+2x, not −1+x. A local stability theorem does not prove global accuracy.

Another view: OCW linearization near critical points; Lebl §§8.1–8.2.

8What a linear centre cannot decide

Consider \(x'=-y+ax(x^2+y^2)\), \(y'=x+ay(x^2+y^2)\). Every value of a has the same Jacobian \(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]\) at the origin. Linearisation therefore gives a centre for every a.

Differentiate r²=x²+y². The rotational terms cancel, leaving \(2rr'=2ar^4\), so r′=ar³ and θ′=1. Integrating gives \(r=r_0/\sqrt{1-2ar_0^2t}\) while the denominator is positive. Negative a attracts, positive a repels, and zero a preserves circles. Identical linear eigenvalues allow all three conclusions.

Worked example · The same centre can conceal repulsion

Choose a=0.5 and initial radius 0.3. At t=3, nonlinear radius is about 0.351123, while the linear radius remains 0.3. The nonlinear point is about (−0.347610,0.0495505). Its growth is slow near zero, which makes a short numerical trace especially easy to misread.

Common mistake

Purely imaginary Jacobian eigenvalues do not prove nonlinear stability. Seek an invariant, a radial equation, higher-order terms or another argument. A centre is a conclusion about the linearisation until additional evidence is supplied.

The conservative pendulum below has an energy invariant and genuine surrounding periodic orbits. Adding damping changes that conclusion. “Looks circular” is not a stability proof.

9Limit cycles

Definition · An isolated periodic orbit

A limit cycle is a closed nonconstant trajectory isolated from other periodic trajectories. It may attract, repel, or attract from only one side. A centre instead has a continuous family of neighbouring periodic orbits.

Use the normal form \(x'=(\mu-r^2)x-y\), \(y'=x+(\mu-r^2)y\). The radial equation is r′=μr−r³, while θ′=1. For μ>0, r′ is positive below √μ and negative above it. Thus r=√μ is an attracting cycle surrounding a repelling equilibrium. With μ≤0, no positive-radius equilibrium exists.

Worked example · Different starts approach one orbit

Choose μ=1 and r₀=0.3. Let s=r². Then s′=2s(1−s), a logistic equation, giving \(r=[1+(r_0^{-2}-1)e^{-2t}]^{-1/2}\). At t=5, r≈0.999771. A start outside the circle approaches the same radius from above. The lab marks successive positive-x-axis returns to distinguish convergence from a merely closed trace.

Return crossings are a useful numerical diagnostic. They are not a universal existence proof. In planar problems, trapping regions and the Poincaré–Bendixson theorem can establish periodic motion when their hypotheses hold. A divergence of one strict sign throughout a simply connected region rules out closed orbits there by the Bendixson criterion.

What the model leaves out

This radial normal form is chosen for an exact comparison. Real oscillators can have amplitude-dependent frequency, multiple cycles or slow variables. Its fixed angular speed is a modelling convenience.

Read: Lebl §8.4. Another view: OCW limit cycles and limits of linear models.

10Chaos and the scope of a portrait

A smooth autonomous two-state flow cannot cross its own trajectories where uniqueness holds. Under the usual bounded planar-flow hypotheses, long-term limit sets obey strong restrictions. A complicated-looking planar curve alone is not evidence of a chaotic attractor.

Three-state systems and periodically forced oscillators escape those two-dimensional autonomous restrictions. For a driven pendulum, include drive phase φ′=Ω to form an autonomous three-state system. Suitable forcing and damping can produce sensitive dependence on initial state. Nonlinearity makes this possible, not inevitable.

Worked example · A driven pendulum has an extra state

In the clock lab's chosen driven case, β=0.03/s, drive amplitude 0.05 rad/s² and Ω=π/2 rad/s. Position and velocity alone do not determine future acceleration without the drive phase. The lab plots a forced trajectory, but makes no chaos claim from it. Its initial phase is fixed at zero.

To investigate chaos, compare converged trajectories from nearby initial states and use an appropriate long-term diagnostic. Step-size error can also separate computations. A finite plot or a positive separation over a short interval does not by itself establish sensitive dependence on a bounded attractor.

What the model leaves out

This unit treats chaos qualitatively, as mapped in the OCW spine. It does not certify a chaotic parameter region or estimate a Lyapunov exponent. Numerical evidence and analytical claims remain distinct.

Read: Lebl §8.5. The OCW final is cumulative across all four units, not a separate “Exam 4”.

Part III

Timekeeping

11A pendulum clock

Parliament gives a 2 s beat and 4.4 m overall pendulum length for the Great Clock. A beat is half a full oscillation. Fit the teaching point-mass length to a small-angle period of 4 s: \(L_{eff}=g[4/(2\pi)]^2\approx3.97449\) m using standard gravity. Overall hardware length is not a pivot-to-centre-of-mass measurement.

Torque balance divided by mL² gives \(\theta''+2\beta\theta'+(g/L)\sin\theta=F\cos\Omega t\). Here β and F are chosen damping and angular-acceleration inputs. F is not the actual clock escapement law. Near zero, sin θ≈θ gives the linear centre when damping and drive vanish.

Derivation · Energy and the amplitude-dependent period

Without damping or drive, multiply by θ′ to obtain constant \(E=\omega^2/2+(g/L)(1-\cos\theta)\). At release from amplitude A, solve energy for angular speed and integrate dt=dθ/ω over a quarter cycle. Substituting \(\sin(\theta/2)=\sin(A/2)\sin\phi\) removes the turning-point singularity:

\[T=4\sqrt{L/g}\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{d\phi}{\sqrt{1-\sin^2(A/2)\sin^2\phi}}.\]

The integrand exceeds one at nonzero A, proving a longer period than the small-angle limit. With damping and drive, energy instead obeys \(E'=\omega[F\cos\Omega t-2\beta\omega]\). The lab checks this work balance as well as integration refinement.

Worked example · Large amplitude delays the teaching pendulum

At the fitted length, a 0.1 rad release has conservative period about 4.00250 s. At 1.2 rad it is about 4.39284 s, a ratio of 1.09821 to the small-angle reference. At t=1 s after the large release, θ≈0.173523 rad and ω≈−1.75286 rad/s. The gold linear rod and blue nonlinear bob separate visibly.

Worked example · Damping requires a sustaining input

Release from 0.5 rad with β=0.15/s and no drive. Normalised initial energy is about 0.302053 s⁻². At t=4 s it has fallen to about 0.0899641 s⁻². A fixed small-angle period does not mean the clock keeps running without energy replacement.

Common mistake

Do not use the 2 s beat as a full period, or use 4.4 m as the effective point-mass length. The displayed conservative period is a model reference; it is not a prediction of the Great Clock’s actual daily error.

What the model leaves out

The Great Clock is a compound mechanism. The drawing omits its mass distribution, actual escapement, compensation, friction laws and air effects. Damping and drive are illustrative choices. No real clock accuracy claim follows from this model.

The course has moved from one equation to a state, from exact modes to local approximations, and from a formula to a model with stated limits. Use the cumulative final practice to choose among those tools.
