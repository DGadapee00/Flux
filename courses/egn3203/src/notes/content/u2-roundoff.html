Part I

Round-off

1Numbers in binary

A double stores a number as \(\pm m \times 2^e\), with 53 binary digits in \(m\). That is about 16 decimal digits. Most decimals, such as 0.1, have no exact binary form and are rounded.

Derivation

With 53 binary digits the gap between neighbouring doubles is \(2^{-52}\) of their size, and rounding to the nearer one is off by at most half that:

\[ \operatorname{fl}(x) = x\,(1 + \delta), \qquad |\delta| \le u = 2^{-53} \approx 1.11\times10^{-16}. \]

Each addition, multiplication or function call rounds its result the same way.

Worked example · 0.1 + 0.2

Neither 0.1 nor 0.2 is exact in binary, and their rounded sum prints as 0.30000000000000004. The error is \(4\times10^{-17}\), a relative error of about \(u\). For almost every calculation that is far too small to matter.

2Cancellation

Subtracting two nearly equal numbers keeps their absolute error but throws away their shared leading digits. What is left can be mostly round-off.

Derivation

If \(a\) and \(b\) each carry an error near \(u|a|\), then \(a - b\) carries the same absolute error, so its relative error is about

\[ \frac{u\,|a|}{|a - b|}. \]

For \(1 - \cos x\) at small \(x\), \(a = 1\) and \(a - b \approx x^2/2\), so the relative error is about \(2u/x^2\). Rewrite with \(1 - \cos x = 2\sin^2(x/2)\) and nothing nearly equal is subtracted.

Worked example · one minus cosine at 10⁻⁸

At \(x = 10^{-8}\), \(\cos x = 1 - 5\times10^{-17}\). That is within \(u\) of 1, so it is stored as exactly 1, and \((1 - \cos x)/x^2\) comes out 0. The true value is very nearly \(1/2\). The rewritten \(2\sin^2(x/2)/x^2\) gives 0.5 to every digit.

Common mistake

Trusting all 16 printed digits. A computer prints 16 digits whatever happened to them. Where a formula subtracts close numbers, count how many leading digits cancel.

Key idea

The same trick fixes other formulas: \(e^x - 1\) by a library's expm1, and \(\sqrt{x^2+1} - x\) by \(1/(\sqrt{x^2+1} + x)\).

Part II

Truncation

3Order of accuracy

A derivative estimated from values a step \(h\) apart is off even in exact arithmetic, because the formula cuts a Taylor series short. That truncation error shrinks as a power of \(h\), and the power is the method's order.

Derivation

Expand \(f(x \pm h) = f \pm hf' + \tfrac{h^2}{2}f'' \pm \tfrac{h^3}{6}f''' + \dots\) Then

\[ \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} = f' + \frac h2 f'' + \dots, \qquad \frac{f(x+h) - f(x-h)}{2h} = f' + \frac{h^2}{6}f''' + \dots \]

The forward difference is first order and the central difference second. Combining four values cancels up to \(h^4\). If \(E \approx Kh^p\), halving \(h\) divides \(E\) by \(2^p\), so \(p = \log_2[E(h)/E(h/2)]\). On log axes the error is a line of slope \(p\).

Round-off adds about \(u|f|/h\), which grows as \(h\) shrinks. The best step balances the two.

Worked example · the forward difference at h = 0.1

For \(\sin x\) at \(x = 1\): \((\sin 1.1 - \sin 1)/0.1 = (0.891207 - 0.841471)/0.1 = 0.4974\). The true value is \(\cos 1 = 0.5403\), an error of 0.0429. The leading term predicts \((0.1/2)\sin 1 = 0.0421\). Halve the step and the error halves, to about 0.021.

Common mistake

Taking the smallest step the computer allows. Near \(h = 10^{-12}\) the forward difference is worse than at \(h = 10^{-4}\), because round-off divided by \(h\) has taken over.

4Root finding

Most equations a computer solves, it solves as \(f(x) = 0\) by iteration: guess, improve, repeat. Two methods show the trade between sure and fast.

Derivation

Bisection starts from a bracket \([a, b]\) where \(f\) changes sign, checks the midpoint, and keeps the half that still changes sign. The root never leaves the bracket, and after \(k\) steps the error is at most \((b - a)/2^{k+1}\): one binary digit a step.

Newton's method replaces \(f\) by its tangent at \(x_k\) and steps to where the tangent crosses zero:

\[ x_{k+1} = x_k - \frac{f(x_k)}{f'(x_k)}. \]

Near a simple root the error squares each step, so the correct digits roughly double. Far from one the tangent can point anywhere: where \(f'\) is small it throws the next guess far away, and some starts cycle.

Worked example · Newton from −2

\(f(x) = x^3 - 2x + 2\), \(f'(x) = 3x^2 - 2\). From \(x_0 = -2\): \(f = -2\), \(f' = 10\), so \(x_1 = -1.8\); then \(x_2 = -1.76995\), and the root \(-1.76929\) to ten digits by \(x_4\). From \(x_0 = 0\), though, \(x_1 = 1\) and \(x_2 = 0\) again: a cycle.

Common mistake

Expecting Newton to find the nearest root. On \(x^3 - x\), a start at 0.45 is nearest 0 but lands on −1; at 0.46 it lands on +1. Keep a bracket, or start close.

5Stepping an ODE

Most differential equations have no formula for their solution. A solver steps from \(y(t)\) to \(y(t + h)\) using the slope \(f(t, y)\), and each evaluation of \(f\) costs time. The question is how to spend a fixed number of them.

Derivation

Euler follows the slope at the start of each step, \(y_{n+1} = y_n + hf(t_n, y_n)\); its error per step is \(O(h^2)\), and over \(T/h\) steps \(O(h)\). Classical RK4 samples the slope four times per step, at the start, twice at the middle and at the end, and weights them

\[ y_{n+1} = y_n + \frac{h}{6}\left(k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4\right), \]

so that the Taylor series matches through \(h^4\): its error over the interval is \(O(h^4)\). Halving \(h\) halves Euler's error and cuts RK4's sixteenfold.

Worked example · a budget of 64

Solve \(y' = -y + \sin 3t\), \(y(0) = 1\), to \(t = 4\) with 64 slope evaluations each. Euler takes 64 steps of 0.0625 and misses by \(4.22 \times 10^{-3}\). The midpoint method takes 32 steps and misses by \(1.69 \times 10^{-3}\). RK4 takes only 16 steps of 0.25 and misses by \(1.03 \times 10^{-6}\), four thousand times less.

Worked example · y′ = y cos t with 256

The exact solution is \(e^{\sin t}\), so \(y(4) = 0.469\). With 256 evaluations Euler misses by \(2.2 \times 10^{-3}\) and RK4 by \(2.75 \times 10^{-8}\). Double the budget and Euler's error halves while RK4's falls by 16.

Common mistake

Buying accuracy with more, smaller Euler steps. To gain six digits Euler needs a million times as many steps; RK4 needs about 32 times as many.

6Interpolation and Runge’s function

Through \(n + 1\) points with distinct \(x\) passes exactly one polynomial of degree \(n\). How well it follows the function between the points depends on where the points are.

Derivation

Lagrange's form builds it from basis polynomials that are 1 at one point and 0 at the others:

\[ p(x) = \sum_{i=0}^{n} y_i\prod_{j\ne i}\frac{x - x_j}{x_i - x_j}, \qquad f(x) - p(x) = \frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}\prod_{i=0}^{n}(x - x_i). \]

On equally spaced points the product is far larger near the ends than in the middle. Chebyshev points, \(x_i = \cos(i\pi/n)\), crowd toward the ends and keep it small throughout.

Worked example · three points of Runge’s function

Through \(f(-1) = 1/26\), \(f(0) = 1\) and \(f(1) = 1/26\) passes \(p(x) = 1 - \tfrac{25}{26}x^2\). At \(x = 0.5\) it gives 0.760, while \(f(0.5) = 0.138\).

Common mistake

Expecting more points to give a better fit. For \(1/(1 + 25x^2)\) on equal spacing the largest error is 1.05 with 9 points and 14 with 17. On Chebyshev points it is 0.04 with 17.

7Differentiating by differences

A derivative is a limit as \(h \to 0\). On a computer \(h\) cannot go to zero: the subtraction loses digits.

Derivation

Taylor's series gives the truncation error of each formula:

\[ \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} = f' + \frac h2 f'' + \cdots, \qquad \frac{f(x+h) - f(x-h)}{2h} = f' + \frac{h^2}{6}f''' + \cdots. \]

Each value of \(f\) carries a relative error near \(\varepsilon = 2^{-52}\), and dividing the difference by \(h\) turns that into about \(\varepsilon|f|/h\). The total, \((h/2)|f''| + \varepsilon|f|/h\) for forward differences, is least at \(h = \sqrt{2\varepsilon|f|/|f''|}\), about \(10^{-8}\).

Worked example · sin x at x = 1

With \(h = 10^{-3}\) the forward difference misses \(\cos 1\) by \(4.2 \times 10^{-4}\), close to \((h/2)\sin 1 = 4.21 \times 10^{-4}\); the central difference by \(9.0 \times 10^{-8}\). At \(h = 10^{-13}\) both miss by about \(10^{-4}\).

Common mistake

Shrinking \(h\) as far as possible. Below about \(10^{-8}\) the forward difference gets worse, not better; at \(h = 10^{-15}\) it is wrong in the second digit.

8Stiff equations and stability

A method can be accurate for the motion you watch and still blow up. Every mode of the system must stay stable, including ones that died out long ago.

Derivation

For \(y' = \lambda y\), one step of explicit Euler multiplies \(y\) by \(1 + h\lambda\), and implicit Euler, which evaluates the slope at the new point, by \(1/(1 - h\lambda)\):

\[ y_{n+1} = (1 + h\lambda)\,y_n, \qquad y_{n+1} = \frac{y_n}{1 - h\lambda}. \]

For real \(\lambda < 0\) explicit Euler is stable only if \(|1 + h\lambda| \le 1\), that is \(h \le 2/|\lambda|\). Implicit Euler's factor is below 1 for every \(h\). A system \(y' = Ay\) behaves as one such equation per eigenvalue of \(A\).

Worked example · rates 1 and 1000

With eigenvalues \(-1\) and \(-1000\), explicit Euler needs \(h \le 2\) ms. At \(h = 2.1\) ms the fast mode's factor is \(-1.1\), and after 238 steps, 0.5 s, it has grown by \(10^{9.8}\), though the exact fast mode had decayed by \(e^{-500}\).

Common mistake

Choosing the step to resolve only the visible, slow motion. The fast mode sets the explicit limit even after it has vanished. That is what stiff means.

9Monte Carlo

Monte Carlo estimates an integral as an average of the integrand at random points. Its error does not depend on the dimension.

Derivation

With \(N\) points uniform in a region \(\Omega\), \(\int_\Omega f\,dV \approx (|\Omega|/N)\sum f(\mathbf x_i)\). Each term is an independent draw with standard deviation \(\sigma_f\), and the average of \(N\) of them has standard deviation \(\sigma_f/\sqrt N\):

\[ \text{standard error} = \frac{|\Omega|\,\sigma_f}{\sqrt N}. \]

For π: points in the unit square land inside the quarter circle with probability \(p = \pi/4\), so \(4 \times\) the share inside estimates π with standard error \(4\sqrt{p(1 - p)/N} \approx 1.64/\sqrt N\).

Worked example · ten thousand points

With \(N = 10\,000\) the standard error is \(1.64/100 = 0.016\): expect about two correct decimals. To make it 0.0016 takes a million points.

Common mistake

Expecting ten times the samples to give ten times the accuracy. It gives \(\sqrt{10} \approx 3.2\) times; a hundred times the samples gives ten.

10Newton’s method for systems

To solve \(\mathbf F(\mathbf x) = \mathbf 0\) for several unknowns, Newton's method replaces every equation by its tangent plane at once.

Derivation

Near \(\mathbf x_k\), \(\mathbf F(\mathbf x_k + \Delta\mathbf x) \approx \mathbf F(\mathbf x_k) + J(\mathbf x_k)\Delta\mathbf x\), with the Jacobian \(J_{ij} = \partial F_i/\partial x_j\). Setting that to zero gives a linear system for the step:

\[ J(\mathbf x_k)\,\Delta\mathbf x = -\mathbf F(\mathbf x_k), \qquad \mathbf x_{k+1} = \mathbf x_k + \Delta\mathbf x. \]

Near a root where \(J\) is invertible, the error is squared each step.

Worked example · one step from (−1, 1)

For \(\mathbf F = (x^2 + y^2 - 4,\ e^x + y - 1)\) at \((-1, 1)\): \(\mathbf F = (-2, 0.368)\) and \(J = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 2 \\ 0.368 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\). Solving gives \(\Delta = (-1, 0)\), so the first step lands at \((-2, 1)\). After four steps the point agrees with the root \((-1.8163, 0.8374)\) to \(10^{-9}\).

Common mistake

Solving a system one equation at a time: \(y\) from one, then \(x\) from the other, and repeat. Each move ignores how the other equation changes, so it undoes part of the last; it converges slowly, or not at all.
