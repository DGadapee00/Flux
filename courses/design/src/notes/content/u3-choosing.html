Part I

Deciding

1Requirements, then weights

Start from the need, not from a part. Write what the part must do as requirements you can check: a number and a limit, such as deflection at most 0.3 mm under the sensor's load. Write what you would like more of as criteria with weights. Then sketch more than one concept before you judge any.

Derivation

A requirement is pass or fail. A concept that misses one is out, whatever else it does well. Score the rest on each criterion from 1 (worst of the field) to 5 (best), and rank by the weighted mean:

\[ S_j = \frac{\sum_k w_k\, s_{jk}}{\sum_k w_k}. \]
Worked example · the run of 1,000

A sensor bracket can be machined aluminium, bent steel sheet or printed nylon. For a run of 1,000 with deflection at most 0.3 mm, the printed part bends 0.6 mm and is out. With weights 1, 5, 2, 1, 1 on mass, cost, deflection, lead time and heat limit, sheet scores 3.4 and machined 2.6.

Common mistake

Scoring the first concept you thought of against nothing in particular. A machined part is often the first sketch; for a run of 1,000 the bent sheet costs a quarter as much and still meets the stiffness requirement.

2How firm is the choice?

Weights are judgements. Before trusting a ranking, ask how far each weight can move before the leader changes.

Derivation

The leader \(L\) stays ahead of a rival \(j\) while \(\sum_k w_k (s_{Lk} - s_{jk}) > 0\). That is linear in each weight, so holding the others, the crossover is

\[ w_k^{*} = -\frac{\sum_{i \ne k} w_i\,(s_{Li} - s_{ji})}{s_{Lk} - s_{jk}}. \]
Worked example · how far the cost weight can fall

For the run of 1,000, sheet leads while the weight on cost stays at 3 or more. Below 3, the machined part's stiffness, lighter weight and shorter lead time carry it.

Key idea

A choice that flips when one weight moves a little is a choice the numbers have not made. Get better data on the close criteria, or build both and test.

What the model leaves out

Scores run straight from the worst concept to the best, so they say nothing about how good the best one is. A matrix ranks the concepts you have; it cannot find one you did not sketch.

Part II

Modelling and proving

3Dimension the reason

A parametric CAD model is a sketch plus dimensions and relations, turned into features. Two models can look the same and behave differently when edited. What decides it is whether each dimension says why the feature is there.

Derivation

Each sketch point has two unknowns, and each dimension or relation is an equation. The solver finds positions that satisfy them; the sketch's free degrees of freedom are the unknowns less the rank of the equations' Jacobian. A fully defined sketch has none. For two holes that must match a centred sensor of spacing \(S\) on a plate \(W\) wide, the dimensions that say so are

\[ x_1 + x_2 = W, \qquad x_2 - x_1 = S \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x_{1,2} = \frac{W \mp S}{2}. \]
Worked example · widening the plate

An 80 mm plate has holes 40 mm apart, centred: at 20 and 60 mm. Drawn as a chain, 20 from the edge then 40, the holes stay at 20 and 60 when the plate is widened to 100 mm, and miss the sensor by 10 mm. Drawn by intent, centred and spaced \(S\), they move to 30 and 70, where the sensor needs them.

Common mistake

Leaving a sketch under-defined because it looks right. The solver then places the free geometry wherever it last was, and the next edit moves it somewhere else.

Key idea

Before drawing, decide what each feature is for and what it is located from. Dimension from the features that matter to the function, such as a datum face or the centre line, not from wherever the sketch happened to start.

4A margin rests on its assumptions

Each requirement needs a way to show it is met: an analysis, a test, an inspection or a demonstration. A margin of safety says how much room is left, but only within what the analysis behind it assumed.

Derivation

For a cantilever arm of width \(b\), thickness \(h\) and length \(L\) with a tip load \(P\),

\[ \sigma = \frac{6PL}{bh^2}, \qquad \delta = \frac{PL^3}{3EI} + \frac{PL^2}{k_\theta}, \qquad \text{MS} = \frac{\text{allowable}}{\text{FS} \times \text{applied}} - 1. \]

The second deflection term is the mount turning under the root moment, with stiffness \(k_\theta\). An analysis that takes the root as rigid leaves it out.

Worked example · the bracket on thin sheet

A 6061-T6 arm 100 mm long, 20 mm wide and 8 mm thick carries 100 N. The root stress is 46.9 MPa, a strength margin of \(+2.43\) with a factor of 1.5. With a rigid root the tip moves 0.574 mm against a 1 mm limit, a margin of \(+0.74\). Bolted to sheet with \(k_\theta = 1.58\times10^6\) N·mm/rad, the test shows 1.21 mm: a margin of \(-0.17\).

Common mistake

Reading a positive margin on paper as proof. The analysis was right about the arm and silent about the mount. Test what the analysis assumed, or analyse it too.

What the model leaves out

A static load at the tip. Real brackets vibrate, so fatigue and the stress concentration at the root corner and bolt holes often govern. Design from the minimum yield, 241 MPa for 6061-T6, not the typical 270 MPa.
