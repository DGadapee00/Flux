Part I

Valves

1A valve in its system

A control valve is a variable restriction. Its flow factor \(K_v\) is the water flow in m³/h it passes with 1 bar across it, fully open. Its trim sets how \(K_v\) changes with travel: linear, equal percentage (each step changes \(K_v\) by the same percentage) or quick opening.

Derivation

For water, \(Q = K_v\sqrt{\Delta p}\). Installed, the valve shares the pump's pressure with the pipe:

\[ H_0 - aQ^2 = kQ^2 + \left(\frac{Q}{K_v f(x)}\right)^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad Q = \sqrt{\frac{H_0}{a + k + 1/(K_v f(x))^2}}. \]

The valve's authority \(N\) is its share of the drop when fully open. With low authority, closing the valve partway adds little to the whole system's resistance.

Worked example · a linear valve on a long pipe

A pump giving 4 bar at no flow drives water through a pipe that loses 3 bar at 10 m³/h and a linear valve with \(K_v = 10\). Fully open, the valve takes a quarter of the drop. Half open, it still passes 77% of full flow. An equal-percentage trim in the same place passes 29% at half open, which spreads the control over the travel.

Common mistake

Expecting half open to mean half the flow. That holds only for a linear valve that takes all the pressure. Size a control valve so it takes a fair share of the drop, and choose its trim for the system it sits in.

What the model leaves out

Cavitation and choked flow. A valve that drops too much pressure can let the liquid flash to vapour and collapse again, which erodes the trim and caps the flow.

Part II

Boards

2Copper is a resistor

On a schematic a wire has no resistance. On a board it is a strip of copper, usually 35 μm thick (1 oz), and a long thin trace has milliohms that matter when amps flow through it.

Derivation \[ R = \frac{\rho L}{w\,t}, \qquad \rho_\text{Cu} = 1.68\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,\text{m}. \]

A sensor chip puts out its signal above its own ground pin. If a motor's return current \(I\) flows through copper that the sensor's ground also uses, that pin rises by \(I R\), and the output with it. The ADC, referenced at the connector, reads the rise as a change in the sensor.

Worked example · a shared return

60 mm of 0.5 mm wide, 1 oz trace measures \(1.68\times10^{-8} \times 0.06/(0.0005 \times 35\times10^{-6}) = 57.6\) mΩ. A 2 A motor current through it lifts the sensor's ground 115 mV: 143 counts of a 12-bit ADC over 3.3 V. Give the motor its own return to the connector, a star ground, and the error falls to the sensor's own milliamp through its short ground stub: about 5 μV.

Common mistake

Routing every ground to the nearest ground. Plan the return paths: high currents to the connector by their own route, sensitive grounds separately, and a ground plane under the board where you can.

Key idea

Every current returns to its source. Draw where each one goes before routing the board, and keep the big ones away from the small ones.

Part III

Motors

3A DC motor's straight line

A brushed DC motor turns current into torque and speed into a back voltage, with one constant \(k\) for both. Everything about it follows from that and its winding's resistance.

Derivation \[ \tau = k\,i, \qquad V = iR + k\omega \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \omega = \frac{V}{k} - \frac{R}{k^2}\,\tau. \]

Speed falls in a straight line from the free speed \(V/k\) to stall at \(\tau_s = kV/R\). The power out, \(\tau\omega\), is a parabola that peaks at \(\tau_s/2\), where it is \(V^2/4R\).

Worked example · half the stall torque

A motor with a 2 Ω winding and \(k = 0.02\) N·m/A on 12 V stalls at 0.12 N·m. At 0.06 N·m it draws 3 A and turns 300 rad/s, 2,865 rpm, giving 18 W, its peak. It takes 36 W in, so it is only 50% efficient there.

Common mistake

Expecting the most power at top speed, or sizing a motor for its peak power. Motors run most efficiently well short of half stall, and the winding's heat at high current is what usually limits them.

4Driving it with PWM

A microcontroller pin cannot supply a motor's current, and a resistor to drop the voltage would waste power. Instead a transistor switches the full supply on and off, fast, under the control of a timer: pulse-width modulation.

Derivation

On for a fraction \(D\) of each period \(T\), the average voltage is \(DV\). The winding's inductance smooths the current, with time constant \(\tau = L/R\); the ripple about the mean is

\[ \Delta I = \frac{V}{R}\,\frac{(1 - e^{-DT/\tau})(1 - e^{-(1-D)T/\tau})}{1 - e^{-T/\tau}}. \]
Worked example · 20 kHz against 200 Hz

12 V into a 2 Ω, 1 mH winding at half duty: the mean current is 3 A at either frequency. At 20 kHz it ripples 0.15 A; at 200 Hz, with each period ten times the 0.5 ms time constant, it swings 5.9 A, nearly full on and full off.

Common mistake

Changing the frequency to change the speed. The duty cycle sets the speed. Choose the frequency fast against the winding's time constant and above hearing, then check the timer still has enough counts per period for fine steps of duty.

What the model leaves out

A coil's current cannot stop at once. When the transistor switches off, a flyback diode across the coil must carry it, or the voltage spikes and destroys the transistor.

5Charge must be close

Chips draw current in sudden steps as they switch. The current has to come from somewhere, and the copper to the regulator has inductance: it cannot change its current quickly without a voltage across it.

Derivation \[ \Delta V \approx L\,\frac{di}{dt} = L\,\frac{\Delta I}{t_r}. \]

A capacitor beside the chip holds charge behind much less inductance, so it supplies the step while the trace slowly recharges it. Wikipedia's Decoupling capacitor article advises about 100 nF of ceramic per chip, placed as close as possible.

Worked example · a step with no capacitor near

A chip 50 mm from its regulator, taken as 50 nH of trace, steps 0.1 A in 10 ns: about \(50\times10^{-9} \times 0.1/10^{-8} = 0.5\) V. The circuit dips 466 mV, below a 3.0 V reset. A 100 nF capacitor 30 mm away leaves a 194 mV dip; 2 mm away, 65 mV.

Common mistake

One large capacitor somewhere on the board. Capacitance far away sits behind inductance. Put a small ceramic at each supply pin, with short, wide connections to the pin and to the ground plane.
