Part I

Fits

1Limits decide the fit

No part is made exactly to size. A drawing gives each size a band, its upper and lower limits, and any part inside the band is good. A fit pairs a hole with a shaft. In the hole-basis system the hole's lower limit is the basic size, and the shaft's band is placed to give the fit wanted.

Derivation

Clearance is hole minus shaft. The tightest pair is the smallest hole with the largest shaft, and the loosest the largest hole with the smallest shaft:

\[ c_{\min} = D_{\min} - d_{\max}, \qquad c_{\max} = D_{\max} - d_{\min}. \]

If \(c_{\min} \ge 0\) every pair slides: a clearance fit. If \(c_{\max} \le 0\) every pair presses: an interference fit. Otherwise it is a transition fit.

Worked example · H8/f7 at 50 mm

An H8 hole is 0 to +39 μm over 50 mm and an f7 shaft −50 to −25 μm. The tightest pair has \(0 - (-25) = 25\) μm of clearance and the loosest \(39 - (-50) = 89\) μm. Every pair slides: a close-running fit.

Common mistake

Judging a fit by its nominal or average clearance. A pair with plenty of room on average can still interfere at its worst, if the bands are wide.

Key idea

Choose the fit for the job first, sliding, located or pressed, and let the standard grades set the limits. The grade number sets the band's width; the letter sets where it sits.

2How often a pair interferes

In a transition fit some pairs slide and some press. How many of each depends on the processes that make the parts, not only on the limits.

Derivation

Take each process as normal, centred in its band, with the band equal to \(\pm 3\sigma/C_p\). The clearance \(c = D - d\) is then normal too, with mean \(\bar c\) the difference of the band middles and

\[ \sigma_c = \sqrt{\sigma_D^2 + \sigma_d^2}, \qquad P(c < 0) = \Phi\!\left(-\frac{\bar c}{\sigma_c}\right). \]
Worked example · H7/k6 with Cp = 1

An H7 hole is 0 to +25 μm and a k6 shaft +2 to +18 μm over 50 mm. The middles are 12.5 and 10 μm, so \(\bar c = 2.5\) μm. With \(C_p = 1\), \(\sigma_D = 25/6\) and \(\sigma_d = 16/6\) μm, so \(\sigma_c = 4.95\) μm and \(P = \Phi(-0.505) = 30.7\%\) of pairs interfere.

Common mistake

Adding the spreads, \(\sigma_D + \sigma_d\). Independent spreads add as variances; adding the σ's overstates the scatter.

Part II

Stacks

3Worst case and RSS

When parts sit end to end, their errors add. The question is how much room to leave for the total.

Derivation

The worst case puts every part at the same extreme: \(T_\text{worst} = \sum T_i\). Independent errors add as variances, so if each \(T_i\) is \(3\sigma_i\),

\[ T_\text{RSS} = 3\sqrt{\textstyle\sum \sigma_i^2} = \sqrt{\textstyle\sum T_i^2}. \]

For \(n\) equal parts that is \(nT\) against \(\sqrt n\,T\).

Worked example · five parts at ±0.1 mm

The worst case is \(\pm 0.5\) mm, but the RSS band is only \(\pm 0.1\sqrt5 = \pm 0.224\) mm. Against a \(\pm 0.3\) mm limit, the worst case fails, yet only 57 stacks in a million do.

Common mistake

Designing every stack to the worst case. It wastes tolerance on every part to guard against a combination that almost never happens.

What the model leaves out

RSS assumes the parts are independent and centred. Parts cut from one bar, or on one worn tool, err together, and then the worst case comes back. Use worst case where a failure is unacceptable or the parts are not independent.
