Sequences and series

An infinite sum is the limit of its partial sums. The tests in this unit decide whether that limit exists without computing it. Power series then turn functions into infinite polynomials. The numbers below are recomputed.

01Sequences

A sequence is a list \(a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots\), one term for each whole number. It converges to \(L\) when its terms get and stay as close to \(L\) as we like. Precisely: for every \(\varepsilon \gt 0\) there is an \(N\) with \(|a_n - L| \lt \varepsilon\) for all \(n \gt N\). This is Unit 1's \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\), with \(N\) in place of \(\delta\).

Why

If \(a_n = f(n)\) for a function with \(\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = L\), then \(a_n \to L\) too. The sequence samples \(f\) at whole numbers. So every tool for limits at infinity, L'Hôpital's rule included, works on sequences.

Worked example · a famous limit

\(a_n = (1 + 1/n)^n\). Then \(\ln a_n = n\ln(1 + 1/n) = \ln(1 + 1/n)/(1/n)\), a \(0/0\) form. L'Hôpital gives \(1\). So \(a_n \to e\).

The terms climb slowly: \(2.594\) at \(n = 10\), \(2.705\) at \(n = 100\), \(2.717\) at \(n = 1000\). The limit is \(2.71828\).

Two more limits recur. A geometric sequence \(r^n\) goes to \(0\) when \(|r| \lt 1\) and diverges when \(|r| \gt 1\). A sequence that is increasing and bounded above converges. It has nowhere to go but up, and it cannot pass its bound.

Common mistake

A sequence and its series are different things. \(a_n = 1/n\) converges to \(0\). The series \(\sum 1/n\) diverges, as section 3 shows. Ask which one the question is about.

Key

A sequence converges when its terms settle on one number. Limits at infinity of functions transfer to sequences.

What it leaves out

Sequences defined by recursion, such as \(a_{n+1} = \sqrt{2 + a_n}\), need the monotone bounded theorem with an induction argument. The text works one in §5.1.

02Series and the geometric series

A series \(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} a_k\) is defined by its partial sums \(S_n = a_1 + \cdots + a_n\). The series converges to \(S\) when \(S_n \to S\). Otherwise it diverges.

Why

The geometric sum has a closed form. Let \(S_n = 1 + r + \cdots + r^{n-1}\). Then \(rS_n = r + \cdots + r^n\). Subtracting cancels every term but two: \(S_n - rS_n = 1 - r^n\). So

\[ S_n = \frac{1 - r^n}{1 - r} \quad (r \ne 1). \]

If \(|r| \lt 1\), \(r^n \to 0\), and the series sums to \(1/(1 - r)\). If \(|r| \ge 1\), the terms do not shrink, and it diverges.

Worked example · eight halvings

\(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} (1/2)^k\). After \(8\) terms, \(S_8 = (1 - 1/256)/(1/2) = 1.9921875\). The full sum is \(1/(1 - 1/2) = 2\). What is missing is exactly the next term doubled: \(2 \cdot (1/2)^8 = 0.0078\).

A telescoping series also has a closed partial sum. \(\sum 1/(k(k + 1))\) splits as \(1/k - 1/(k + 1)\). Every middle term cancels, leaving \(S_n = 1 - 1/(n + 1) \to 1\). At \(n = 99\), \(S_n = 0.99\).

Divergence test. If \(a_k\) does not go to \(0\), the series diverges. Since \(a_n = S_n - S_{n-1}\), a convergent series has \(a_n \to S - S = 0\).

Common mistake

The test only runs one way. Terms going to \(0\) do not make a series converge. \(1/k \to 0\), yet \(\sum 1/k\) diverges. The divergence test can show divergence. It can never show convergence.

Key

A series is the limit of its partial sums. \(\sum r^k\) converges to \(1/(1 - r)\) exactly when \(|r| \lt 1\). Terms that do not shrink mean divergence.

In the Series lab, the geometric scenario stacks partial sums under the level \(2\). Add terms and watch the gap halve each time.

What it leaves out

Few series have closed partial sums. The geometric and telescoping cases are the exceptions. For the rest, the tests below decide convergence without finding the sum.

03The integral test

If \(f\) is positive, continuous, and decreasing, and \(a_k = f(k)\), then \(\sum a_k\) and \(\int_1^{\infty} f(x)\,dx\) converge or diverge together.

Why

Draw a rectangle of width \(1\) and height \(f(k)\) over \([k, k + 1]\). Since \(f\) decreases, each rectangle lies above the curve on that interval. So \(S_n \ge \int_1^{n+1} f\,dx\). Shift the rectangles one step left and they lie under the curve: \(S_n - a_1 \le \int_1^n f\,dx\). The sum is trapped between two integrals.

Worked example · the harmonic series

\(\sum 1/k\) with \(f(x) = 1/x\). The bound gives \(H_n \ge \ln(n + 1)\), and \(\ln(n + 1) \to \infty\). So the harmonic series diverges. At \(n = 8\), \(H_8 = 2.718\), above \(\ln 9 = 2.197\).

It diverges very slowly. Passing \(10\) takes over \(12{,}000\) terms.

The test settles the \(p\)-series at once. \(\sum 1/k^p\) converges exactly when \(p \gt 1\), because \(\int_1^{\infty} x^{-p}\,dx\) does. That was Unit 2's improper-integral test.

Worked example · bounding a sum

\(\sum 1/k^2\). Four terms give \(S_4 = 1.4236\). The rest of the sum lies between \(\int_5^{\infty} x^{-2}\,dx = 1/5\) and \(\int_4^{\infty} x^{-2}\,dx = 1/4\). So the sum is between \(1.6236\) and \(1.6736\). The true value is \(\pi^2/6 = 1.6449\).

Common mistake

The integral is not the sum. \(\int_1^{\infty} x^{-2}\,dx = 1\), while \(\sum 1/k^2 = 1.645\). The test says whether both are finite. To estimate the sum, use the bounds above.

Key

A positive decreasing series behaves like its integral. \(\sum 1/k^p\) converges for \(p \gt 1\) and diverges for \(p \le 1\).

In the Series lab, the harmonic scenario keeps climbing with no level to approach. Add terms and compare the growth with \(\ln n\).

What it leaves out

The test needs \(f\) decreasing, at least eventually. A series whose terms wobble needs another test.

04Comparison and ratio tests

Comparison. If \(0 \le a_k \le b_k\) and \(\sum b_k\) converges, so does \(\sum a_k\). If \(\sum a_k\) diverges, so does \(\sum b_k\). The smaller series cannot outgrow the larger.

Ratio test. Let \(L = \lim |a_{k+1}/a_k|\). If \(L \lt 1\), the series converges absolutely. If \(L \gt 1\), it diverges. If \(L = 1\), the test says nothing.

Why

If \(L \lt 1\), pick \(r\) with \(L \lt r \lt 1\). Past some index, each term is less than \(r\) times the one before. So the tail is below a geometric series with ratio \(r\), which converges. If \(L \gt 1\), the terms eventually grow and cannot go to \(0\).

Worked example · k over 2 to the k

\(\sum k/2^k\). The ratio is \(\dfrac{(k + 1)/2^{k+1}}{k/2^k} = \dfrac{k + 1}{2k} \to \dfrac12\). The series converges. Its sum is \(2\), which section 8 derives.

Worked example · factorials

\(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} 1/k!\). The ratio is \(1/(k + 1) \to 0\). The series converges fast. Its sum is \(e - 1 = 1.71828\), as the series for \(e^x\) at \(x = 1\) shows.

Common mistake

\(L = 1\) is not convergence. Both \(\sum 1/k\) and \(\sum 1/k^2\) have ratio limit \(1\). The first diverges and the second converges. When the ratio test returns \(1\), use another test.

Key

Compare positive terms with a known series. Use the ratio test for factorials and powers. A ratio limit of \(1\) decides nothing.

What it leaves out

The limit comparison test and the root test are in §5.4 and §5.6 of the text. They handle the same cases more conveniently, with the same logic.

05Alternating series

An alternating series flips sign every term: \(b_1 - b_2 + b_3 - \cdots\) with every \(b_k \gt 0\). If the \(b_k\) decrease to \(0\), the series converges. The error after \(n\) terms is less than the first term left out.

\[ |S - S_n| \le b_{n+1}. \]
Why

The partial sums step down, up, down, each step shorter than the last. The even partial sums increase. The odd ones decrease. Every odd sum lies above every even one. Both families converge, and their gap is a single term, which goes to \(0\). The sum lies between any two consecutive partial sums, so it is within one step of each.

Worked example · eight alternating terms

\(1 - \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 - \cdots\). After \(8\) terms, \(S_8 = 0.6345\). The sum is \(\ln 2 = 0.6931\). The error \(0.0586\) is less than the bound \(b_9 = 1/9 = 0.111\).

A series converges absolutely if \(\sum |a_k|\) converges. Absolute convergence implies convergence. The alternating harmonic series converges, but not absolutely, since \(\sum 1/k\) diverges. That is conditional convergence.

Common mistake

Alternating signs alone do not give convergence. \(1 - 1 + 1 - 1 + \cdots\) alternates, but its terms do not shrink. Its partial sums flip between \(1\) and \(0\) forever. Check that \(b_k \to 0\), and decreases.

Key

Decreasing terms with alternating signs converge. The error is less than the next term.

In the Series lab, the alternating scenario zig-zags around the level \(\ln 2\). Each new term lands on the other side.

What it leaves out

Rearranging a conditionally convergent series can change its sum. Riemann proved it can be made to sum to any number. Only absolutely convergent series are safe to reorder.

06Power series

A power series about \(a\) is \(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} c_k (x - a)^k\). It is a polynomial with no last term. For each \(x\) it is a numerical series that may or may not converge.

Why

The ratio test fixes where. If \(|c_{k+1}/c_k| \to 1/R\), then the ratio of consecutive terms goes to \(|x - a|/R\). That is below \(1\) when \(|x - a| \lt R\), and above \(1\) when \(|x - a| \gt R\). So every power series converges inside an interval of radius \(R\) and diverges outside it. At the two ends, anything can happen.

Worked example · x to the k over k

\(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} x^k/k\). Here \(c_{k+1}/c_k = k/(k + 1) \to 1\), so \(R = 1\). At \(x = 1\) the series is harmonic and diverges. At \(x = -1\) it is the alternating harmonic series and converges. The interval of convergence is \([-1, 1)\).

The geometric series is the first power series: \(1/(1 - x) = \sum x^k\) for \(|x| \lt 1\). Many others come from it by substitution. Replacing \(x\) by \(-x^2\) gives \(1/(1 + x^2) = \sum (-1)^k x^{2k}\), also for \(|x| \lt 1\).

Common mistake

The ratio test says nothing at the endpoints, where the ratio is exactly \(1\). Each endpoint needs its own test. For \(\sum x^k/k\), the two ends behave differently.

Key

A power series converges on an interval of radius \(R\) about its centre. Find \(R\) with the ratio test. Test the endpoints separately.

What it leaves out

The radius can be \(0\), when only the centre works, or infinite, when every \(x\) works. Both occur. \(\sum k!\,x^k\) has \(R = 0\), and \(\sum x^k/k!\) has \(R = \infty\).

07Taylor polynomials

A Taylor polynomial matches \(f\) and its first \(n\) derivatives at a centre \(a\). The more derivatives it matches, the longer it follows the curve.

Why

Write \(p(x) = \sum c_k (x - a)^k\). Differentiating \(k\) times and setting \(x = a\) kills every term but one, leaving \(p^{(k)}(a) = k!\,c_k\). To match \(f^{(k)}(a)\), take \(c_k = f^{(k)}(a)/k!\).

\[ P_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}\,(x - a)^k. \]

About \(a = 0\) this is a Maclaurin polynomial. The derivatives of \(\sin x\) at \(0\) cycle \(0, 1, 0, -1\), so only odd powers appear:

\[ \sin x \approx x - \frac{x^3}{6} + \frac{x^5}{120}. \]
Worked example · the tangent line to sine

\(P_1(x) = x\). At \(x = 0.5\) it gives \(0.5\). The true value is \(\sin 0.5 = 0.47943\). The error is \(0.0206\).

Worked example · order five

\(P_5(0.5) = 0.5 - 0.020833 + 0.000260 = 0.479427\). The error is now \(1.54 \times 10^{-6}\). Two more nonzero terms cut the error by a factor of more than ten thousand.

Taylor's bound. If \(|f^{(n+1)}| \le M\) between \(a\) and \(x\), the error is at most \(M|x - a|^{n+1}/(n + 1)!\). For sine, every derivative is at most \(1\). With \(n = 2\) (the \(x^2\) term is zero), the bound at \(0.5\) is \(0.5^3/6 = 0.0208\). The true error, \(0.0206\), is under it.

Worked example · a centre at π/2

About \(a = \pi/2\), \(\sin\) has value \(1\), slope \(0\), and second derivative \(-1\). So \(P_2(x) = 1 - (x - \pi/2)^2/2\). At \(x = \pi/2 + 0.4\): \(P_2 = 1 - 0.08 = 0.92\). The true value is \(\cos 0.4 = 0.92106\).

Common mistake

Forgetting the factorial. Writing \(f''(a)(x - a)^2\) without the \(\tfrac12\) gives \(1 - 0.16 = 0.84\) at the last point. The \(k!\) is what makes the \(k\)-th derivative match.

Key

\(c_k = f^{(k)}(a)/k!\). A higher order follows the curve further. The error is bounded by the next derivative's size times \(|x - a|^{n+1}/(n + 1)!\).

In the Taylor lab, raise the order and watch the polynomial hug the sine curve over a wider stretch. Horner's nesting computes the same value, a second route.

What it leaves out

A Taylor series may converge to something other than \(f\). The standard example is \(e^{-1/x^2}\), whose derivatives at \(0\) are all \(0\). For \(\sin\), \(\cos\), and \(e^x\), the bound shows the error goes to \(0\) for every \(x\).

08Working with power series

Inside its interval, a power series can be differentiated and integrated term by term. The radius does not change. This builds new series from known ones without computing derivatives.

Worked example · the logarithm

\(1/(1 + t) = 1 - t + t^2 - \cdots\) for \(|t| \lt 1\). Integrate from \(0\) to \(x\):

\[ \ln(1 + x) = x - \frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{x^3}{3} - \cdots. \]

At \(x = 1\) this is the alternating harmonic series of section 5. So its sum is \(\ln 2\).

Worked example · an integral with no antiderivative

\(\int_0^{0.5} e^{-x^2}\,dx\). Substitute \(-x^2\) into \(e^u = \sum u^k/k!\), then integrate term by term:

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_0^{0.5} e^{-x^2}\,dx &= 0.5 - \frac{0.5^3}{3} + \frac{0.5^5}{10}\\ &\quad - \frac{0.5^7}{42} + \cdots. \end{aligned} \]

Four terms give \(0.461272\). The series alternates, so the error is less than the next term, \(0.5^9/216 = 0.000009\). Simpson's rule gives \(0.461281\).

Worked example · a sum from a derivative

Differentiate \(\sum x^k = 1/(1 - x)\): \(\sum k x^{k-1} = 1/(1 - x)^2\). Multiply by \(x\) and set \(x = 1/2\): \(\sum k/2^k = (1/2)/(1/4) = 2\). That is the sum promised in section 4.

Common mistake

Using a series outside its interval. \(\ln(1 + x) = x - x^2/2 + \cdots\) holds only for \(-1 \lt x \le 1\). At \(x = 2\) the terms grow, and the series diverges, though \(\ln 3\) is perfectly finite.

Key

Differentiate, integrate, and substitute known series. The radius stays the same. Check the endpoints again.

What it leaves out

Term-by-term work at the endpoints themselves needs a separate argument, Abel's theorem. It is why the alternating harmonic series sums to \(\ln 2\), and it is beyond this course.

09Formula sheet

  • \(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} r^k = (1 - r^n)/(1 - r)\). \(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} r^k = 1/(1 - r)\) for \(|r| \lt 1\).
  • Divergence test: \(a_k \not\to 0\) means divergence.
  • Integral test: \(\sum f(k)\) and \(\int_1^{\infty} f\) converge together, for \(f\) positive and decreasing.
  • \(\sum 1/k^p\) converges exactly when \(p \gt 1\).
  • Ratio test: \(L \lt 1\) converges, \(L \gt 1\) diverges, \(L = 1\) undecided.
  • Alternating series: \(|S - S_n| \le b_{n+1}\).
  • Taylor: \(c_k = f^{(k)}(a)/k!\). Error \(\le M|x - a|^{n+1}/(n + 1)!\).
  • \(e^x = \sum x^k/k!\). \(\sin x = \sum (-1)^k x^{2k+1}/(2k + 1)!\). \(\cos x = \sum (-1)^k x^{2k}/(2k)!\).
  • \(\ln(1 + x) = \sum_{k \ge 1} (-1)^{k+1} x^k/k\), for \(-1 \lt x \le 1\).
