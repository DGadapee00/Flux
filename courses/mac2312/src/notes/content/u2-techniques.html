Techniques of integration

Substitution undoes the chain rule. This unit adds the other tools: parts undoes the product rule, trigonometric identities and substitutions tame roots, partial fractions split quotients. When no tool fits, numerical rules estimate the integral, and a limit handles infinite ones. The numbers below are recomputed.

01Integration by parts

The product rule says \((uv)' = u'v + uv'\). Integrate both sides and rearrange. One integral trades for another.

\[ \int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du. \]
Why

Integrating \((uv)' = u'v + uv'\) gives \(uv = \int v\,du + \int u\,dv\), since \(du = u'\,dx\) and \(dv = v'\,dx\). Solve for \(\int u\,dv\). For a definite integral, the \(uv\) term is evaluated at the two limits.

The choice of \(u\) decides whether the new integral is easier. Choose for \(u\) the factor that gets simpler when differentiated. A common order of preference is: logarithms, inverse trig functions, algebraic powers, trig functions, exponentials.

Worked example · x times e to the x

\(\int_0^1 x e^x\,dx\). Take \(u = x\) and \(dv = e^x\,dx\). Then \(du = dx\) and \(v = e^x\). So the integral is \([x e^x]_0^1 - \int_0^1 e^x\,dx = e - (e - 1) = 1\).

Worked example · a logarithm alone

\(\int_1^e \ln x\,dx\). There seems to be one factor, but \(dv = dx\) is the other. Take \(u = \ln x\), so \(du = dx/x\) and \(v = x\). The integral is \([x\ln x]_1^e - \int_1^e dx = e - (e - 1) = 1\).

Worked example · x sin x over one hump

\(\int_0^{\pi} x\sin x\,dx\). Take \(u = x\), \(dv = \sin x\,dx\), so \(v = -\cos x\). The integral is \([-x\cos x]_0^{\pi} + \int_0^{\pi}\cos x\,dx = \pi + 0 = \pi\).

Sometimes parts returns the original integral. Then solve for it like an unknown. Two rounds on \(\int e^x \sin x\,dx\) give \(e^x\sin x - e^x\cos x - \int e^x\sin x\,dx\). Moving the integral across gives \(\tfrac12 e^x(\sin x - \cos x) + C\).

Common mistake

Choosing \(u = e^x\) and \(dv = x\,dx\) for \(\int x e^x\,dx\) makes it worse. The new integral is \(\int \tfrac12 x^2 e^x\,dx\), with a higher power. If the new integral is harder, swap the roles.

Key

\(\int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du\). Let \(u\) be the factor that simplifies when differentiated. If the original integral comes back, solve for it.

What it leaves out

Repeated parts on \(x^n e^x\) can be organised in a table, the tabular method. It is a bookkeeping shortcut for the same formula, and the text shows it.

02Trigonometric integrals

Integrals of products of powers of \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\) yield to two identities: \(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1\), and the half-angle forms

\[ \begin{aligned} \sin^2 x &= \tfrac12(1 - \cos 2x),\\ \cos^2 x &= \tfrac12(1 + \cos 2x). \end{aligned} \]
Why

If either power is odd, save one factor of that function for \(du\). Convert the rest with \(\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1\). The integral becomes a polynomial in the other function. If both powers are even, nothing can be saved. Lower the powers with the half-angle forms instead.

Worked example · an odd power of sine

\(\int_0^{\pi/2} \sin^3 x\cos^2 x\,dx\). Save one \(\sin x\). Write \(\sin^2 x = 1 - \cos^2 x\). With \(u = \cos x\), \(du = -\sin x\,dx\), and \(u\) runs from \(1\) to \(0\). The integral is \(\int_0^1 (u^2 - u^4)\,du = 1/3 - 1/5 = 2/15\).

Worked example · an even power

\(\int_0^{\pi} \sin^2 x\,dx = \tfrac12\int_0^{\pi}(1 - \cos 2x)\,dx = \tfrac12\bigl[x - \tfrac12\sin 2x\bigr]_0^{\pi} = \pi/2\). That is half the interval's length, since \(\sin^2\) and \(\cos^2\) share it equally.

Common mistake

Substituting \(u = \sin x\) in \(\int \sin^2 x\,dx\) needs a \(\cos x\,dx\) that is not there. Even powers of a single function do not reduce by substitution. Use the half-angle form.

Key

Odd power: save one factor and convert the rest. Even powers: half-angle formulas. Check the answer by differentiating.

What it leaves out

Powers of \(\tan\) and \(\sec\) follow the same idea, with \(\sec^2 x = 1 + \tan^2 x\) and \(du = \sec^2 x\,dx\). The text's §3.2 lists those cases.

03Trigonometric substitution

A root of a quadratic is a side of a right triangle in disguise. Substituting a trigonometric function turns the root into a single trig term.

  • \(\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}\): let \(x = a\sin\theta\). Then the root is \(a\cos\theta\).
  • \(\sqrt{a^2 + x^2}\): let \(x = a\tan\theta\). Then the root is \(a\sec\theta\).
  • \(\sqrt{x^2 - a^2}\): let \(x = a\sec\theta\). Then the root is \(a\tan\theta\).
Why

Each choice makes an identity do the work. With \(x = a\sin\theta\), \(a^2 - x^2 = a^2(1 - \sin^2\theta) = a^2\cos^2\theta\). On \(-\pi/2 \le \theta \le \pi/2\), \(\cos\theta \ge 0\), so the root is \(a\cos\theta\) with no sign trouble. The other two use \(1 + \tan^2 = \sec^2\).

Worked example · part of a circle

\(\int_0^1 \sqrt{4 - x^2}\,dx\). Let \(x = 2\sin\theta\), \(dx = 2\cos\theta\,d\theta\). Then \(\theta\) runs from \(0\) to \(\pi/6\), and the integrand becomes \(4\cos^2\theta\). The half-angle form gives \([2\theta + \sin 2\theta]_0^{\pi/6} = \pi/3 + \sqrt{3}/2 = 1.913\).

Geometry agrees. The region is a sector of angle \(\pi/6\) in a circle of radius \(2\), with area \(\pi/3\), plus a triangle of area \(\sqrt{3}/2\).

Worked example · the arcsine

\(\int dx/\sqrt{1 - x^2}\). With \(x = \sin\theta\), \(dx = \cos\theta\,d\theta\) and the root is \(\cos\theta\). The integrand becomes \(1\), so the integral is \(\theta = \arcsin x\), plus \(C\). From \(0\) to \(1/2\) it is \(\pi/6 = 0.524\).

Common mistake

Forgetting \(dx = a\cos\theta\,d\theta\). For part of the circle, dropping it leaves \(\int_0^{\pi/6} 2\cos\theta\,d\theta = 1\), far from \(1.913\). Every substitution converts \(dx\), not only the integrand.

Key

Match the root to a Pythagorean identity. Convert \(x\), \(dx\), and the limits. Read the answer back in \(x\) from a right triangle.

What it leaves out

A quadratic that is not in one of the three forms needs completing the square first. \(\sqrt{x^2 + 2x + 5}\) becomes \(\sqrt{(x + 1)^2 + 4}\), and then the tangent substitution applies.

04Partial fractions

A rational function whose bottom factors can be split into simpler fractions. Each piece integrates to a logarithm or an arctangent.

Why

For distinct linear factors, write

\[ \frac{p(x)}{(x - r_1)(x - r_2)} = \frac{A}{x - r_1} + \frac{B}{x - r_2}. \]

Multiply through by \((x - r_1)\) and let \(x \to r_1\). The \(B\) term vanishes, leaving \(A = p(r_1)/(r_1 - r_2)\). This is the cover-up rule: cover the factor, and evaluate the rest at its root.

Worked example · two linear factors

\(\int_0^1 \dfrac{3x + 5}{(x + 1)(x + 3)}\,dx\). Cover \(x + 1\) and set \(x = -1\): \(A = 2/2 = 1\). Cover \(x + 3\) and set \(x = -3\): \(B = -4/(-2) = 2\). The integral is \([\ln(x + 1) + 2\ln(x + 3)]_0^1 = \ln 2 + 2\ln(4/3) = 1.269\).

Worked example · a difference of squares

\(\int_2^3 dx/(x^2 - 1)\). The bottom is \((x - 1)(x + 1)\). The cover-up gives \(A = 1/2\) and \(B = -1/2\). The integral is \(\tfrac12[\ln|x - 1| - \ln|x + 1|]_2^3 = \tfrac12(\ln\tfrac24 - \ln\tfrac13) = \tfrac12\ln 1.5 = 0.203\).

Worked example · a constant on top

\(\int_0^1 \dfrac{dx}{(x + 1)(x + 2)}\). The cover-up gives \(A = 1\) and \(B = -1\). The integral is \([\ln(x + 1) - \ln(x + 2)]_0^1 = \ln\tfrac23 - \ln\tfrac12 = \ln\tfrac43 = 0.288\).

Common mistake

Partial fractions need the top's degree below the bottom's. For \(x^2/(x^2 - 1)\), divide first: \(1 + 1/(x^2 - 1)\). Splitting without dividing produces constants that do not exist.

Key

Divide if the top's degree is not lower. Factor the bottom. Find each constant by cover-up. Integrate each piece as a logarithm.

What it leaves out

A repeated factor \((x - r)^2\) needs both \(A/(x - r)\) and \(B/(x - r)^2\). An irreducible quadratic needs \((Ax + B)/(x^2 + c^2)\), which integrates to a logarithm plus an arctangent. Both are in §3.4 of the text.

05Numerical integration

Most integrals have no antiderivative in closed form. Numerical rules estimate them from samples of the integrand. Split \([a, b]\) into \(n\) panels of width \(h = (b - a)/n\).

The trapezoidal rule joins neighbouring samples with straight lines. Simpson's rule fits a parabola through each pair of panels, and needs \(n\) even.

\[ \begin{aligned} T_n &= \tfrac{h}{2}\,(f_0 + 2f_1 + \cdots + 2f_{n-1} + f_n),\\ S_n &= \tfrac{h}{3}\,(f_0 + 4f_1 + 2f_2 + \cdots + 4f_{n-1} + f_n). \end{aligned} \]
Why

A trapezoid over one panel has area \(\tfrac{h}{2}(f_i + f_{i+1})\). Adding them counts every interior sample twice. That is also the average of the left and right Riemann sums.

The parabola through three equally spaced points has area \(\tfrac{h}{3}(f_0 + 4f_1 + f_2)\). Integrate it with its vertex at the middle sample to check. Chaining those blocks gives the weights \(1, 4, 2, 4, \ldots, 4, 1\).

Worked example · four panels on sine

Estimate \(\int_0^{\pi}\sin x\,dx = 2\) with \(n = 4\). The trapezoidal rule gives \(1.8961\), off by \(0.1039\). Simpson's rule gives \(2.00456\), off by \(0.00456\). The same five samples feed both.

Worked example · doubling the panels

With \(n = 8\), the trapezoid error falls to \(0.0258\), about a quarter. Simpson's falls to \(0.000269\), about a sixteenth. The trapezoid error shrinks like \(1/n^2\). Simpson's shrinks like \(1/n^4\).

Error bounds make that precise. If \(|f''| \le M\) on \([a, b]\), then \(|E_T| \le M(b - a)^3/(12n^2)\). If \(|f^{(4)}| \le K\), then \(|E_S| \le K(b - a)^5/(180n^4)\). For sine with \(n = 4\), both \(M\) and \(K\) are \(1\). The bounds are \(0.162\) and \(0.0066\). The true errors sit under them.

Common mistake

Simpson's rule with an odd \(n\) is not defined. The parabolas pair the panels. With \(n = 5\), the last panel has no partner, and the weights do not close with a \(1\).

Key

Trapezoid: weights \(1, 2, \ldots, 2, 1\), times \(h/2\), with error like \(1/n^2\). Simpson: weights \(1, 4, 2, \ldots, 4, 1\), times \(h/3\), with error like \(1/n^4\).

In the Numerical lab, step from \(4\) to \(8\) to \(16\) panels. The readout shows both errors falling at their own rates.

What it leaves out

Both bounds need a bound on a derivative, which is often unknown. In practice, the estimate is recomputed with \(n\) doubled, and the change between the two is taken as the error.

06Improper integrals

An integral over an infinite interval, or of an unbounded function, is defined as a limit of ordinary integrals. If the limit is finite, the integral converges. Otherwise it diverges.

\[ \int_1^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = \lim_{b \to \infty}\int_1^b f(x)\,dx. \]
Worked example · the tail of 1 over x squared

\(\int_1^b x^{-2}\,dx = 1 - 1/b\). At \(b = 4\) the area is \(0.75\), and \(0.25\) is still missing. At \(b = 8\) the area is \(0.875\). As \(b \to \infty\), the area approaches \(1\). So \(\int_1^{\infty} x^{-2}\,dx = 1\): an unbounded region with finite area.

Why

For \(p \ne 1\), \(\int_1^b x^{-p}\,dx = (b^{1-p} - 1)/(1 - p)\). If \(p \gt 1\), \(b^{1-p} \to 0\), and the integral is \(1/(p - 1)\). If \(p \lt 1\), \(b^{1-p} \to \infty\). If \(p = 1\), the integral is \(\ln b \to \infty\). So

\[ \int_1^{\infty} \frac{dx}{x^p} \ \text{converges exactly when} \ p \gt 1. \]

An unbounded integrand works the same way, with the limit at the bad point. \(\int_0^1 x^{-1/2}\,dx = \lim_{a \to 0^+}(2 - 2\sqrt{a}) = 2\). Near \(0\) the exponent test flips: \(\int_0^1 x^{-p}\,dx\) converges exactly when \(p \lt 1\).

When no antiderivative is available, compare. If \(0 \le f \le g\) and \(\int g\) converges, so does \(\int f\). For \(x \ge 1\), \(e^{-x^2} \le e^{-x}\). Since \(\int_1^{\infty} e^{-x}\,dx = 1/e = 0.368\), the integral of \(e^{-x^2}\) converges, and is smaller.

Worked example · parts out to infinity

\(\int_0^{\infty} x e^{-x}\,dx\). By parts, \(\int_0^b x e^{-x}\,dx = 1 - (b + 1)e^{-b}\). The exponential beats the linear factor, so \((b + 1)e^{-b} \to 0\). The integral is \(1\). At \(b = 10\) the finite piece is already \(0.9995\).

Common mistake

Missing a bad point inside the interval. \(\int_{-1}^{1} x^{-2}\,dx\) evaluated as \([-1/x]_{-1}^{1}\) gives \(-2\). A positive function cannot have a negative integral. The integrand is unbounded at \(0\), and each half diverges. So the integral diverges.

Key

Replace the bad end with a variable, integrate, and take the limit. \(\int_1^{\infty} x^{-p}\) converges for \(p \gt 1\). \(\int_0^1 x^{-p}\) converges for \(p \lt 1\). Compare when you cannot integrate.

In the Improper lab, push the bound from \(2\) to \(8\). The area climbs toward \(1\), and the tail shrinks like \(1/b\).

What it leaves out

A limit comparison test, which compares the ratio \(f/g\) rather than \(f\) and \(g\) directly, is in the text. The same convergence ideas return for series in Unit 3.
