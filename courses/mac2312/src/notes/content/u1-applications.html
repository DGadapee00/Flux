Applications of integration

Every application here follows one pattern. Cut the quantity into thin pieces. Approximate one piece. Add the pieces as a Riemann sum, and let the pieces shrink to an integral. The numbers below are recomputed.

01Area between curves

Suppose \(f(x) \ge g(x)\) on \([a, b]\). A thin vertical strip at \(x\) has height \(f(x) - g(x)\) and width \(dx\). Adding the strips gives the area.

\[ A = \int_a^b \bigl(f(x) - g(x)\bigr)\,dx. \]
Why

Over a strip of width \(\Delta x\), the region is almost a rectangle of height \(f(x_i) - g(x_i)\). The sum of those rectangles is a Riemann sum for \(f - g\). As \(\Delta x \to 0\), it becomes the integral. The formula does not care whether either curve is above the axis. Only the difference counts.

Worked example · a line over a parabola

On \([0, 1]\), \(x \ge x^2\). The area between them is \(\int_0^1 (x - x^2)\,dx = 1/2 - 1/3 = 1/6\).

When the curves cross, the top one changes. Split the interval at each crossing, or integrate \(|f - g|\).

Worked example · curves that cross

\(y = x\) and \(y = x^3\) cross at \(-1\), \(0\), and \(1\). On \([0, 1]\), \(x\) is on top, and the area is \(1/2 - 1/4 = 1/4\). On \([-1, 0]\), \(x^3\) is on top, and the area is also \(1/4\). The total is \(1/2\).

Integrating \(x - x^3\) straight across \([-1, 1]\) gives \(0\). The two pieces cancel. That number is not an area.

Some regions are easier in horizontal strips. Then the right curve minus the left curve is the length, and \(dy\) is the width.

Worked example · horizontal strips

The region between \(x = y^2\) and \(x = y + 2\). They meet where \(y^2 = y + 2\), at \(y = -1\) and \(y = 2\). The line is on the right. The area is \(\int_{-1}^{2} (y + 2 - y^2)\,dy = 10/3 + 7/6 = 4.5\).

Vertical strips would need two integrals here, because the bottom boundary changes at \(x = 1\).

Common mistake

Subtracting in the wrong order gives a negative area. For the line over the parabola, \(\int_0^1 (x^2 - x)\,dx = -1/6\). Sketch first, and subtract the lower curve from the upper.

Key

Top minus bottom, in \(dx\). Right minus left, in \(dy\). Split where the curves cross.

What it leaves out

The intersections are found by algebra here. For most pairs of curves they need a numerical root finder, such as Newton's method. The area formula is unchanged.

02Volumes by slicing

Cut a solid into thin slabs across an axis. If the slab at \(x\) has cross-sectional area \(A(x)\) and thickness \(dx\), its volume is about \(A(x)\,dx\). Add them.

\[ V = \int_a^b A(x)\,dx. \]

A solid of revolution has circular slices. Rotating the region under \(y = f(x)\) about the \(x\)-axis makes disks of radius \(f(x)\), so \(A(x) = \pi f(x)^2\).

Why

A cone of radius \(r\) and height \(h\) checks the method. Lay it along the \(x\)-axis with its tip at \(0\). The radius grows linearly, so at \(x\) it is \(rx/h\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} V &= \int_0^h \pi\Bigl(\frac{rx}{h}\Bigr)^2 dx\\ &= \frac{\pi r^2}{h^2}\cdot\frac{h^3}{3} = \frac{\pi r^2 h}{3}. \end{aligned} \]

That is the cone formula from geometry, now derived. With \(r = 3\) and \(h = 4\) it gives \(12\pi = 37.7\).

Worked example · disks under a root

Rotate the region under \(y = \sqrt{x}\) on \([0, 4]\) about the \(x\)-axis. Each disk has area \(\pi(\sqrt{x})^2 = \pi x\). So \(V = \int_0^4 \pi x\,dx = 8\pi = 25.1\). Dividing out \(\pi\) leaves \(8\).

A region with a hole in the middle makes washers. The area of a washer is the outer disk minus the inner one: \(\pi(R^2 - r^2)\).

Worked example · a washer

Rotate the region between \(y = x\) and \(y = x^2\) on \([0, 1]\) about the \(x\)-axis. The outer radius is \(x\), the inner \(x^2\). So \(V = \pi\int_0^1 (x^2 - x^4)\,dx = \pi(1/3 - 1/5) = 2\pi/15 = 0.419\).

Common mistake

\(\pi(R^2 - r^2)\) is not \(\pi(R - r)^2\). For the washer above, squaring the difference gives \(\pi\int_0^1 (x - x^2)^2\,dx = \pi/30 = 0.105\). That is a quarter of the true volume. Square each radius, then subtract.

Key

Volume is the integral of cross-sectional area. Disks: \(\pi R^2\). Washers: \(\pi(R^2 - r^2)\). Radii are measured from the axis of rotation.

In the Solids lab, the Disks scenario stacks slices under \(\sqrt{x}\). The readout gives the volume by the antiderivative and by Simpson's rule.

What it leaves out

Rotation about a line other than an axis shifts every radius. About \(y = -1\), for instance, a disk's radius becomes \(f(x) + 1\). The text works those cases in §2.2.

03Volumes by shells

Instead of slicing across the axis, cut the solid into thin cylindrical shells around it. A shell of radius \(r\), height \(h\), and thickness \(dr\) has volume about \(2\pi r h\,dr\): unroll it into a thin slab of length \(2\pi r\).

Why

The exact volume of a shell is the outer cylinder minus the inner one: \(\pi h\bigl((r + dr)^2 - r^2\bigr) = 2\pi r h\,dr + \pi h\,dr^2\). The second term is a thin slab times a thin slab. In the Riemann sum it vanishes faster than \(dr\), so only \(2\pi r h\,dr\) survives the limit.

\[ V = \int 2\pi\,(\text{radius})(\text{height})\,d(\text{radius}). \]
Worked example · the same solid by shells

The solid under \(\sqrt{x}\) on \([0, 4]\), rotated about the \(x\)-axis. Shells around that axis are horizontal. At height \(y\), a shell has radius \(y\), and it runs from \(x = y^2\) to \(x = 4\). So

\[ \begin{aligned} V &= \int_0^2 2\pi y\,(4 - y^2)\,dy\\ &= 2\pi\,(8 - 4) = 8\pi. \end{aligned} \]

Disks gave \(8\pi\) too. Two slicings of one solid must agree.

Worked example · around the y-axis

Rotate the region under \(y = x - x^2\) on \([0, 1]\) about the \(y\)-axis. A vertical strip at \(x\) sweeps a shell of radius \(x\) and height \(x - x^2\). So \(V = 2\pi\int_0^1 (x^2 - x^3)\,dx = 2\pi(1/3 - 1/4) = \pi/6 = 0.524\).

Washers here would need \(x\) as a function of \(y\) on both sides of the hump. Shells avoid that.

Common mistake

The radius is the distance to the axis, not the coordinate. Around the line \(x = 2\), a strip at \(x\) has radius \(2 - x\). Using \(x\) builds the solid around the wrong line.

Key

Shells: \(2\pi \times\) radius \(\times\) height \(\times\) thickness. Slice parallel to the axis for shells and across it for disks. Pick the one whose integrand needs no solving.

In the Solids lab, the Both ways scenario shows disks and shells for one solid. The two readouts agree for every right end \(b\).

What it leaves out

The shell argument assumes the height is a single segment for each radius. A region that folds back on itself needs to be split first.

04Arc length and surface area

Cut a curve into short pieces. Each piece is nearly a straight segment. Its length is \(\sqrt{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2}\).

Why

Over a step \(\Delta x\), the Mean Value Theorem gives a point where \(\Delta y = f'(x_i^*)\,\Delta x\). So the segment's length is \(\sqrt{1 + f'(x_i^*)^2}\,\Delta x\). The sum of the segments is a Riemann sum.

\[ L = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + f'(x)^2}\,dx. \]
Worked example · a parabola to x = 1

For \(y = x^2\) from \(0\) to \(1\), \(f'(x) = 2x\), so \(L = \int_0^1 \sqrt{1 + 4x^2}\,dx\). The antiderivative is \(\tfrac{x}{2}\sqrt{1 + 4x^2} + \tfrac14\ln\bigl(2x + \sqrt{1 + 4x^2}\bigr)\). At \(1\) that is \(1.118 + 0.361 = 1.479\).

The straight chord from \((0, 0)\) to \((1, 1)\) is \(\sqrt{2} = 1.414\). The curve is longer, as it must be.

Worked example · a line, by both routes

For \(y = x\) from \(0\) to \(1\), \(f' = 1\), and the integrand is the constant \(\sqrt{2}\). So \(L = \sqrt{2}\). The distance formula gives the same number. A formula that works must agree with geometry on a line.

Worked example · a parabola to x = 2

Run the same parabola to \(x = 2\). The antiderivative gives \(L = 4.647\). The chord to \((2, 4)\) is \(\sqrt{20} = 4.472\). The gap has grown from \(0.065\) to \(0.175\), because the curve bends more over the longer run.

Rotating the curve about the \(x\)-axis sweeps a surface. Each short piece sweeps a band of radius \(f(x)\) and slant width \(\sqrt{1 + f'^2}\,dx\).

\[ S = \int_a^b 2\pi f(x)\sqrt{1 + f'(x)^2}\,dx. \]
Worked example · a sphere

Take \(f(x) = \sqrt{r^2 - x^2}\) on \([-r, r]\). Then \(f' = -x/f\), so \(f\sqrt{1 + f'^2} = \sqrt{f^2 + x^2} = r\). The integrand is the constant \(2\pi r\). So \(S = 2\pi r \cdot 2r = 4\pi r^2\). For \(r = 2\) that is \(50.3\).

Common mistake

Using \(2\pi f(x)\,dx\) for the band, without the slant factor, treats each band as a flat ring. For a sphere that gives \(\int_{-r}^{r} 2\pi\sqrt{r^2 - x^2}\,dx = \pi^2 r^2\), which is \(39.5\) for \(r = 2\) instead of \(50.3\). The slant is not small where the curve is steep.

Key

Arc length: \(\int\sqrt{1 + f'^2}\,dx\). Surface area: \(\int 2\pi f\sqrt{1 + f'^2}\,dx\). The square root is the slant of each piece.

In the Arc length lab, the readout gives the length by the antiderivative and by Simpson's rule, next to the chord. Push the right end out and watch the gap grow.

What it leaves out

Most arc-length integrals have no closed form. \(\int\sqrt{1 + \cos^2 x}\,dx\), the length of a sine wave, is one. They are evaluated numerically, as in Unit 2.

05Work

A constant force \(F\) over a distance \(d\) does work \(W = Fd\). A force that varies is constant over a short enough step. Add the steps.

\[ W = \int_a^b F(x)\,dx. \]
Worked example · a spring

A spring pulls back with force \(kx\) when stretched \(x\) from rest (Hooke's law). With \(k = 4\) lb/ft, stretching it \(2\) ft takes \(\int_0^2 4x\,dx = 8\) ft·lb.

The second foot costs three times the first: \(\int_1^2 4x\,dx = 6\), against \(\int_0^1 4x\,dx = 2\). The force grows as the spring stretches.

When different parts of a body move different distances, slice the body instead of the path. Each slice has its own weight and its own lift.

Worked example · pumping out a tank

A cylindrical tank has radius \(2\) m and height \(5\) m, full of water. Pump it all out over the top. A slab at height \(y\) has volume \(4\pi\,dy\), weight \(\rho g \cdot 4\pi\,dy\), and must rise \(5 - y\) m.

\[ \begin{aligned} W &= \int_0^5 \rho g\,(4\pi)(5 - y)\,dy\\ &= \rho g \cdot 4\pi \cdot 12.5. \end{aligned} \]

With \(\rho = 1000\) kg/m³ and \(g = 9.81\) m/s², \(W = 1.54 \times 10^6\) J.

Worked example · winding up a chain

A chain \(20\) m long, of mass \(2\) kg per metre, hangs from a winch. Winding it in lifts the piece \(y\) m below the winch by \(y\) m. That piece weighs \(2 \cdot 9.81\,dy\) N. So \(W = \int_0^{20} 19.62\,y\,dy = 19.62 \cdot 200 = 3920\) J.

The answer equals the chain's whole weight, \(392\) N, lifted \(10\) m. That is the distance its centre of mass rises, which section 6 explains.

Common mistake

Lifting the whole tank's weight the full \(5\) m doubles the answer, to \(3.08 \times 10^6\) J. The top slab barely moves. Only the bottom slab rises the full height. The integral weighs each slab by its own distance.

Key

Work is force integrated over distance. When the distance varies through the body, slice the body and integrate weight times lift.

What it leaves out

The water is lifted slowly, so it gains no speed and no kinetic energy. Friction in the pipe and the pump's efficiency are ignored. A real pump does more work than this integral.

06Centres of mass

The centre of mass is the balance point. For point masses \(m_i\) at positions \(x_i\), it is the weighted average \(\bar{x} = \sum m_i x_i / \sum m_i\). A thin plate of uniform density is the limit of many small masses.

Why

Cut the region under \(y = f(x)\) into strips. A strip at \(x\) has area \(f(x)\,dx\), so its mass is proportional to that. Its own centre is at \(x\) across and at \(f(x)/2\) up. Weight each position by the strip's area and add.

\[ \bar{x} = \frac{\int x f(x)\,dx}{\int f(x)\,dx}, \] \[ \bar{y} = \frac{\int \tfrac12 f(x)^2\,dx}{\int f(x)\,dx}. \]
Worked example · under a parabola

The region under \(y = x^2\) on \([0, 1]\) has area \(1/3\). Then \(\bar{x} = (1/4)/(1/3) = 0.75\), and \(\bar{y} = (1/10)/(1/3) = 0.3\). The balance point sits toward the tall end, and low, because most of the area is near the axis there.

Common mistake

The centroid is not the midpoint of the interval. For the parabola it is at \(0.75\), not \(0.5\). The tall end carries more area, and the average leans toward it.

Key

A centre of mass is a weighted average. Weight each strip by its area, and place it at the strip's own centre.

What it leaves out

The plate is uniform. A density that varies across it enters each integral as a weight. Pappus's theorem, which links centroids to volumes of revolution, is in §2.6 of the text.

07Formula sheet

  • Area: \(\int_a^b (f - g)\,dx\), top minus bottom. In \(y\): right minus left.
  • Volume: \(\int A(x)\,dx\). Disk \(\pi R^2\). Washer \(\pi(R^2 - r^2)\).
  • Shells: \(\int 2\pi\,(\text{radius})(\text{height})\,d(\text{radius})\).
  • Cone: \(\pi r^2 h/3\). Sphere's surface: \(4\pi r^2\).
  • Arc length: \(\int_a^b \sqrt{1 + f'(x)^2}\,dx\).
  • Surface area: \(\int_a^b 2\pi f(x)\sqrt{1 + f'(x)^2}\,dx\).
  • Work: \(\int F(x)\,dx\). Spring: \(F = kx\), work \(kd^2/2\).
  • Pumping: \(\int \rho g \times (\text{slab area}) \times (\text{lift})\,dy\).
  • Centroid: \(\bar{x} = \int x f\,dx / \int f\,dx\), \(\bar{y} = \int \tfrac12 f^2\,dx / \int f\,dx\).
